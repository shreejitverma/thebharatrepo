The Mughal Empire was founded in 1526 by Babur, a prince from Central Asia descended from both Timur and Genghis Khan.
At its height, around 1700, it ruled most of the Indian subcontinent and drew revenue from one of the largest economies of its day [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire; @src.raychaudhuri-1982-cambridge-economic-history-india].
It never held the whole subcontinent, since the far south and parts of the northeast remained outside its control, and much of its rule over local chiefs was indirect [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].

## Babur and Humayun

Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodi, the last Delhi sultan, at Panipat in April 1526, helped by field artillery and handguns, and defeated a Rajput confederacy under Rana Sanga at Khanwa the next year [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire; @src.babur-1921-memoirs].
He left a remarkable autobiography, the *Baburnama*, written in Turkic, which describes India's wealth and climate with a candid, curious eye [@src.babur-1921-memoirs].
He died in 1530.

His son Humayun lost the realm to the Afghan Sher Shah Sur in 1540 and spent years in exile in Safavid Iran, then regained Delhi in 1555 [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].
Sher Shah's short rule produced administrative reforms in revenue and roads that Akbar later built on [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].

## Akbar and the imperial system

Akbar came to the throne in 1556 as a teenager and ruled until 1605.
He expanded the empire into Gujarat, Bengal, Kashmir and Sind, and he secured the loyalty of many Rajput houses through marriage and rank in imperial service [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].

The empire's key institution was the mansabdari system.
Officers were given a numerical rank (mansab) that fixed their status, pay and the size of the force they had to provide, and most were paid by assignments of land revenue [@src.raychaudhuri-1982-cambridge-economic-history-india; @src.abulfazl-1873-ain-i-akbari].
The *Ain-i Akbari* of Abu'l Fazl, a court minister, is the great contemporary description of this machinery [@src.abulfazl-1873-ain-i-akbari].
The system took shape under Akbar, and the revenue demand rested on measuring land and assessing crops [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].

### Akbar and religion

Akbar abolished the tax on non-Muslim pilgrims and the jizya, and from 1575 he hosted debates among Muslim scholars, Jain monks, Hindus, Zoroastrians and Jesuit priests in his Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].
His court ideal is known as *sulh-i kull*, "peace with all" [@src.truschke-2016-culture-of-encounters].
The court also sponsored Persian translations of Sanskrit works, including the Mahabharata, which was rendered as the *Razmnama* [@src.truschke-2016-culture-of-encounters].

The *Din-i Ilahi* needs a plain description.
It is often called a new religion, but historians generally see it as a small circle of disciples bound to the emperor by loyalty, and not as a state religion or a faith with a following [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].
Much of what we know comes from a hostile contemporary, the orthodox chronicler Badauni, so the evidence is partisan.

## Jahangir and Shah Jahan

Jahangir (1605 to 1627) kept the empire stable and is famous for the naturalistic style of court painting and for the political power of his wife Nur Jahan [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire; @src.asher-1992-architecture-of-mughal-india].
His son Shah Jahan (1628 to 1658) built on an imperial scale, including the Red Fort and the new city of Shahjahanabad at Delhi [@src.asher-1992-architecture-of-mughal-india].
The Taj Mahal at Agra, a tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died in 1631, was built from the early 1630s, with the main complex taking until the early 1650s [@src.asher-1992-architecture-of-mughal-india].

Succession was a recurring danger.
When Shah Jahan fell ill in 1657, his sons fought a civil war, and Aurangzeb won, executing his brother Dara Shukoh and confining his father until his death [@src.faruqui-2012-princes-of-the-mughal-empire; @src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].

## Aurangzeb

Aurangzeb ruled from 1658 to 1707.
He spent the last twenty-six years campaigning in the Deccan, annexed Bijapur and Golconda in 1686 and 1687, and brought the empire to its greatest territorial extent, although the Maratha war was never won [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire; @src.truschke-2017-aurangzeb].

The older popular image presents him as a simple bigot, and a revisionist image presents him as a pious but pragmatic ruler.
The record supports neither cartoon.
He reimposed the jizya in 1679 [@src.chandra-1969-jizyah].
Chronicles record orders to demolish particular temples, such as those at Varanasi and Mathura, around 1669 and 1670 [@src.eaton-2000-temple-desecration; @src.truschke-2017-aurangzeb].
At the same time he issued grants and protective orders for other temples, and he employed Hindu nobles, with Rajputs and Marathas holding a larger share of high rank than under earlier emperors [@src.truschke-2017-aurangzeb; @src.eaton-2000-temple-desecration].
Historians explain the temple orders through a mix of politics, such as rebellion by local chiefs, and religious conviction, and they disagree about the balance [@src.eaton-2000-temple-desecration; @src.truschke-2017-aurangzeb; @src.roy-2023-temple-desecration-discussion].

## Why did the empire decline?

After 1707 the empire weakened quickly, and regional states such as Hyderabad, Awadh and Bengal became effectively independent [@src.metcalf-2012-concise-history-modern-india].
Nader Shah's invasion sacked Delhi in 1739, and the East India Company obtained the right to collect revenue in Bengal from the emperor in 1765 [@src.metcalf-2012-concise-history-modern-india].

Why this happened is a live debate, with three broad positions.
Irfan Habib and others stress an agrarian and jagirdari crisis in which over-assignment of revenue drove overtaxation and revolt [@src.raychaudhuri-1982-cambridge-economic-history-india].
Athar Ali emphasised the strength of a centralised system and the burden of the Deccan wars, and he criticised revisionists who play down centralisation [@src.ali-1993-mughal-polity].
Other historians argue that power shifted to regional elites who built on Mughal institutions rather than overthrowing them, so "decline" is the wrong word for the whole subcontinent [@src.metcalf-2012-concise-history-modern-india; @src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].

The final end came after the rebellion of 1857.
The British put the last emperor, Bahadur Shah II, on trial in 1858 and exiled him to Rangoon [@src.metcalf-2012-concise-history-modern-india].

## How we know

We have an unusually rich record.
Court chronicles such as the *Akbarnama* and the *Ain-i Akbari*, imperial memoirs by Babur and Jahangir, revenue manuals, and European travellers' accounts all survive [@src.abulfazl-1873-ain-i-akbari; @src.babur-1921-memoirs].
Chroniclers wrote for emperors, so modern historians compare them with archives, inscriptions, coins and buildings [@src.richards-1993-mughal-empire; @src.asher-1992-architecture-of-mughal-india].
