The Maratha story is usually told as a straight line from Shivaji to the Peshwas and then to defeat by the British.
The historical record shows something messier and more interesting: a regional kingdom in the Deccan that survived a long Mughal war, and then turned into a confederation of powerful commanders who, for much of the eighteenth century, collected revenue and fought wars across a large part of the subcontinent [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].

## Before Shivaji

Maratha warriors and officials, from the Marathi-speaking western Deccan, had served the Deccan sultanates of Bijapur, Ahmadnagar and Golconda for generations.
Shivaji Bhonsle's father, Shahaji, was one such commander.
The Maratha rise therefore grew out of the military and administrative world of the Deccan sultanates, not outside it [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].

Shivaji began in the 1640s by taking hill forts in the Sahyadri country and built a small, strongly fortified state.
He raided Mughal territory, including the sack of the port of Surat in 1664, and he was forced to accept the Treaty of Purandar with the Mughals in 1665.
He visited Aurangzeb's court at Agra in 1666 and escaped from custody there [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818; @src.laine-2003-shivaji].

## The coronation of 1674

In June 1674 Shivaji was formally enthroned at Raigad as chhatrapati, a king in his own right.
The ceremony was a deliberate act of political theatre: Brahmin scholars from Banaras, led by Gaga Bhatta, performed a Vedic coronation, partly because Shivaji's Bhonsle family was not traditionally counted as Kshatriya by many Brahmins in the region, and a ritual was needed to establish his kingship [@src.laine-2003-shivaji].
The coronation was both a claim to sovereignty and an argument about status, and the sources show that it was disputed within Maharashtra itself [@src.laine-2003-shivaji].

Shivaji died in April 1680.
His kingdom was not an empire at that point; it was a compact state along the western coast and the Sahyadri hills, with a navy, many forts and a growing revenue system [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].

## Survival against the Mughals

After Shivaji's death, Aurangzeb brought his army to the Deccan and spent the last quarter-century of his life there.
Shivaji's son Sambhaji was captured and executed by the Mughals in 1689.
The Maratha state did not collapse.
Authority passed to Rajaram and later to Tarabai, and Maratha commanders conducted a decentralised war that Mughal forces could not end [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].

This is the real basis of the tradition of Maratha "guerrilla" war, though the word oversimplifies it.
Maratha armies used fast-moving cavalry, raiding and forts, but they also fought pitched battles and besieged cities, and they adapted methods common to Deccan warfare of the period rather than inventing a wholly new style [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].

## The Peshwas and the confederacy

After Aurangzeb died in 1707, Shahu, Sambhaji's son, who had grown up at the Mughal court, was released and became chhatrapati at Satara.
He appointed Balaji Vishwanath as Peshwa (chief minister) in 1713, and the office later became hereditary within one Chitpavan Brahmin family, with the Peshwa governing from Pune while the chhatrapati became increasingly a ceremonial figure [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].

Baji Rao I, Peshwa from 1720 to 1740, led armies into Malwa, Gujarat and northern India.
The Marathas gained the right to collect chauth (a quarter of the revenue) and sardeshmukhi (a further tenth) from large regions, including areas under Mughal governors, and then extended their reach by sending commanders to levy these claims [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].
Andre Wink argues that these claims were not simply taxation: they were assertions of sovereignty, rooted in the Deccan idea of fitna (disorder) and svarajya (self-rule), which gave Maratha leaders a way to extract resources from territories they did not formally govern [@src.wink-1986-land-and-sovereignty].

By mid-century the system was a confederacy.
The Peshwa's government was one centre, but the Sindhia, Holkar, Gaekwad and Bhonsle of Nagpur families each held large territories and armies and often acted independently [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].

## Panipat and after

In January 1761 a Maratha army met the forces of Ahmad Shah Durrani, the Afghan ruler, at the Third Battle of Panipat and was heavily defeated.
The loss of the army and many commanders set back Maratha ambitions in the north, though it did not end Maratha power: Mahadji Sindhia later regained influence in Delhi and the region [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].

The more serious long-term problem was the rise of the East India Company.
The First Anglo-Maratha War ended in 1782 with the Treaty of Salbai, which restored an uneasy balance.
The Second (1803 to 1805) broke the power of several leaders, and in the Third Anglo-Maratha War of 1817 to 1818 the British defeated the Peshwa, Baji Rao II, who surrendered in June 1818; his territories were annexed and he was pensioned off [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818; @src.dalrymple-2019-the-anarchy].

## What "empire" means here

Scholars use "empire" loosely.
Gordon's account stresses that the Maratha polity was a shifting set of arrangements between the Peshwa, regional commanders and the courts and banking houses they depended on, not a centralised bureaucratic state like the Mughal one at its height [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].
Calling the Marathas the "paramount power" of the eighteenth century is therefore a judgement, not a fact; the Nizam of Hyderabad, Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan, and the East India Company were also strong powers, and the Marathas' reach varied greatly from decade to decade [@src.gordon-1993-marathas-1600-1818].

Shivaji himself remains a subject of strong public memory in Maharashtra, and historians such as Laine have shown how later writers and political movements remade his image, so that any single account of his motives has to be read with care for the period of the source [@src.laine-2003-shivaji].
