Vijayanagara, "City of Victory", was both a kingdom and the name of its capital, whose ruins lie beside the modern village of Hampi in Karnataka.
Between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries its kings ruled most of peninsular India south of the Krishna river, and the city became one of the largest in the Indian world [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara; @src.michell-1995-architecture-art-southern-india].
The Group of Monuments at Hampi is a UNESCO World Heritage Site [@src.unesco-1986-hampi].

## Beginnings

Tradition names two brothers, Harihara and Bukka, as the founders, and gives 1336 as the date.
Historians accept the broad picture but treat the origin story with care.
Contemporary inscriptions show the brothers serving as officers on the frontier of the Hoysala kingdom, and later sources link them to the Kampili chiefdom, to the Kakatiya kingdom of Warangal, and to the Delhi Sultanate [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
A well-known later story says the brothers were taken to Delhi, converted to Islam, sent south by the sultan and brought back to Hindu practice by the sage Vidyaranya.
This account is attested only in later narratives and is not accepted by most historians as a record of events [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
Their regional background, whether Kannada, Telugu or Hoysala-service, has been argued both ways, and the evidence does not settle it [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].

What is clear is the setting.
Delhi's armies had raided and then briefly administered parts of the south in the 1320s and 1330s, and Delhi's hold then collapsed [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
Vijayanagara grew in the resulting vacuum, alongside the Bahmani Sultanate to its north.

## Four dynasties

The empire was ruled by four dynasties in turn: Sangama, Saluva, Tuluva and Aravidu [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
It expanded east and west across the peninsula, and its main rival for the fertile Raichur doab, between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers, was the Bahmani state and then its successor sultanates [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara; @src.eaton-2005-social-history-deccan].

The Tuluva king Krishnadevaraya (reigned 1509 to 1529) is the best known ruler.
He fought the Gajapati kings of Odisha and the Sultan of Bijapur, taking the fortress of Raichur in 1520 [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara; @src.sewell-1900-forgotten-empire].
He is also remembered as a patron of Telugu and Sanskrit literature, and the Telugu poem *Amuktamalyada* is traditionally ascribed to him [@src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods].
His reign saw major temple building at the capital, including the Krishna and Hazara Rama temples [@src.michell-1995-architecture-art-southern-india].
Calling it a "golden age" is an evaluation rather than a finding, but it was certainly a peak of territory, wealth and building.

## The state and its capital

Stein described the empire as a "segmentary" state in which warrior chiefs, called nayakas, held territory and owed military service and tribute to the king [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
This interpretation shaped the field but is a scholarly reading and has been debated, and it should not be treated as settled [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].

Two foreign visitors describe the capital at its height.
Domingos Paes, a Portuguese traveller who visited around 1520, and Fernao Nuniz, who compiled a chronicle in the 1530s, praised its size, markets and festivals [@src.sewell-1900-forgotten-empire].
The claim that Vijayanagara was one of the wealthiest and largest cities of its day rests mainly on such visitors and on the scale of its surviving ruins, so its exact population is uncertain [@src.sewell-1900-forgotten-empire; @src.michell-1995-architecture-art-southern-india].
Horse imports from Arabia and Persia, arriving through west-coast ports, were vital to its army [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].

## Hindu bulwark or shared courtly world?

The historian Robert Sewell called Vijayanagara a "Hindu bulwark against Muhammadan conquests", and that description became standard in the twentieth century [@src.wagoner-1996-sultan-among-hindu-kings].
It is only part of the story.
Phillip Wagoner showed that the kings adopted the title "Sultan among Hindu kings" (*hindurāya suratrāṇa*), borrowed Persianate dress and courtly forms, and employed Muslim soldiers and officers [@src.wagoner-1996-sultan-among-hindu-kings].
Vijayanagara and the sultanates fought one another, but they also traded, allied and sometimes sided together against rivals [@src.eaton-2013-power-memory-architecture].

Temples were not only victims in this conflict.
Eaton and Wagoner study how war between the sultanates and the southern kingdoms touched temples, and how Vijayanagara kings in turn revived older Deccan architectural styles to claim ancient authority [@src.eaton-2013-power-memory-architecture].
Wagoner has also shown that a mosque at Adoni, wrongly believed to be a converted temple and damaged in a riot in 1977, had in fact been built for Muslim soldiers under Vijayanagara patronage [@src.wagoner-2023-temple-desecration-deccan].
Modern memory can distort the record in both directions, which is why the buildings themselves have to be examined as evidence.

## Talikota and after

In January 1565 an alliance of Deccan sultanates defeated Vijayanagara at Talikota, near the Krishna river, and the regent Aliya Rama Raya was killed [@src.sewell-1900-forgotten-empire; @src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
The victors plundered the capital for months, and Hampi was never again a royal capital [@src.sewell-1900-forgotten-empire].
Historians are careful about how much of the ruin at Hampi comes from the sack and how much from later abandonment and neglect [@src.michell-1995-architecture-art-southern-india].

The Aravidu dynasty continued from Penukonda and later Chandragiri.
Its power and its control over the nayakas faded, and 1646 is the conventional date of the dynasty's effective end [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
Several former nayakas, including those of Madurai, Thanjavur and Ikkeri, became independent states [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].

## How we know

The evidence is unusually varied.
Thousands of inscriptions in Kannada, Telugu, Tamil and Sanskrit record grants and royal orders [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
Foreign accounts such as those of Paes and Nuniz give eyewitness views, and the Persian chronicles of the Deccan sultanates give the rival perspective [@src.sewell-1900-forgotten-empire; @src.eaton-2005-social-history-deccan].
The standing monuments and archaeological survey at Hampi allow us to test all of these [@src.michell-1995-architecture-art-southern-india; @src.unesco-1986-hampi].

- 1336: conventional date of the foundation [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
- 1509 to 1529: reign of Krishnadevaraya [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
- 1565: defeat at Talikota and sack of the capital [@src.sewell-1900-forgotten-empire].
- 1646: conventional end of effective Aravidu rule [@src.stein-1989-vijayanagara].
