The Cholas are named as a southern people already in the edicts of Ashoka, in the 3rd century BCE [@src.hultzsch-1925-inscriptions-of-asoka].
But the "imperial" Cholas of this article belong to a later revival.
From about the middle of the 9th century CE, a line based in the fertile delta of the Kaveri river rose to dominate the Tamil country and then reached far beyond it [@src.sastri-1955-the-colas; @src.subbarayalu-2012-south-india-under-cholas].

## Rise

Vijayalaya, a chief who had served under the Pallavas, took Thanjavur from the Muttaraiyar chiefs around 850 CE.
His successors Aditya I and Parantaka I absorbed the Pallava lands and fought the Pandyas of Madurai [@src.sastri-1955-the-colas].
A setback came in 949 CE, when the Rashtrakuta king Krishna III defeated the Cholas at Takkolam, but the dynasty recovered later in the 10th century [@src.sastri-1955-the-colas; @src.karashima-2014-concise-history-south-india].

## Rajaraja I and Rajendra I

Rajaraja I (r. 985 to 1014 CE) conquered the Chera and Pandya territories, took the northern part of Sri Lanka and extended Chola power into Karnataka and the Andhra region [@src.sastri-1955-the-colas; @src.subbarayalu-2012-south-india-under-cholas].
His inscriptions also claim the conquest of the "old islands" which are often identified as the Maldives; this is a claim by the court, and the extent of any lasting control is uncertain [@src.sastri-1955-the-colas].
The Sri Lankan campaign was followed up under his son Rajendra I (r. 1014 to 1044 CE), when the Cholas seized the island's Sinhalese king and took control of Anuradhapura's region.
George Spencer argued that these campaigns were driven largely by plunder and the need to reward a military following, as much as by any wish to govern the island [@src.spencer-1976-politics-of-plunder-cholas-ceylon].

Rajendra also sent an army north to the Ganges region (c. 1021 to 1025) and founded a new capital, Gangaikonda Cholapuram, to commemorate it [@src.sastri-1955-the-colas].
Around 1025 CE Chola ships raided the Srivijaya kingdom in Sumatra and the Malay peninsula.
The original article said the navy "dominated the Indian Ocean"; the record shows a famous, daring expedition rather than lasting rule.
Scholars debate why it happened, with explanations ranging from protecting trade interests to a show of royal prestige, and most agree that it did not lead to permanent Chola territory in Southeast Asia [@src.kulke-2009-nagapattinam-to-suvarnadwipa].
Chola merchant guilds were nevertheless active across the region, and trade links were close [@src.kulke-2009-nagapattinam-to-suvarnadwipa; @src.karashima-2014-concise-history-south-india].

## How the state worked

Our best evidence is stone.
Thousands of Chola inscriptions, mostly in Tamil on temple walls, record land grants, taxes, gifts and disputes, with the year of the king's reign [@src.subbarayalu-2012-south-india-under-cholas; @src.hultzsch-1890-south-indian-inscriptions].
They show village assemblies (the *ur* and the *sabha*) managing land and local affairs, and inscriptions at Uttaramerur from the early 10th century describe how its members were chosen [@src.sastri-1955-the-colas; @src.subbarayalu-2012-south-india-under-cholas].
This is the basis for the popular statement that the Cholas had "local self-government", though it is better phrased as the importance of local assemblies within a monarchy.

Historians disagree about the size of the king's control.
K. A. Nilakanta Sastri described a centralised, bureaucratic empire.
Burton Stein countered with the model of a "segmentary state", in which the king held ritual supremacy but real power was spread among local groups and the temple [@src.stein-1980-peasant-state-society-medieval-south-india].
Y. Subbarayalu, working from the inscriptions in detail, argues for a stronger administrative organisation than Stein allowed, built on the unit called *nadu* [@src.subbarayalu-2012-south-india-under-cholas].

## Temples and bronzes

Rajaraja I's Rajarajesvara (Brihadisvara) temple at Thanjavur has a dedicatory inscription dated to the 25th year of his reign, which corresponds to 1010 CE, and its walls list the temple's staff and endowments [@src.hultzsch-1890-south-indian-inscriptions; @src.sastri-1955-the-colas].
The Thanjavur temple, with those at Gangaikondacholapuram and Darasuram, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site as the "Great Living Chola Temples" [@src.unesco-1987-great-living-chola-temples].
Chola workshops also made outstanding bronze images, by lost-wax casting, including the form of Shiva as Nataraja [@src.sastri-1955-the-colas].

## Decline

From the later 12th century, the Chola kings faced the rising Pandyas, the Hoysalas and revolts by their own chiefs.
Rajendra III, the last of the line, was overcome by the Pandyas by about 1279 CE [@src.sastri-1955-the-colas; @src.karashima-2014-concise-history-south-india].
The claim that the Cholas were "one of the longest-ruling dynasties in world history" is a popular boast with no careful comparison behind it, and it is better to say that a Chola line ruled with intervals for more than four centuries [@src.subbarayalu-2012-south-india-under-cholas].
