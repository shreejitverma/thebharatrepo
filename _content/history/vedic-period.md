The Vedic period is named for the Vedas, the oldest body of Sanskrit literature in South Asia.
It is the stretch of time in which those texts were composed and the society they describe took shape.
Because the texts were memorised and passed on orally for a very long time, and because no inscriptions of the period survive, its chronology is built from language, ritual vocabulary and archaeology, and it is a reasoned estimate, not a fixed record.

## The texts and how they are dated

The Rigveda, a collection of 1,028 hymns, is the earliest Vedic text [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].
Most scholars place its composition in the later second millennium BCE, roughly 1500 to 1000 BCE, on the grounds that its Sanskrit is very close to the oldest Iranian texts and that its world is pastoral and Bronze Age [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru; @src.erdosy-1995-indo-aryans-of-ancient-south-asia].
The Sama, Yajur and Atharva Vedas, the prose Brahmanas, and the early Upanishads follow in the early first millennium BCE, as the language shifts and the geographic horizon moves east [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru; @src.gonda-1975-vedic-literature; @src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
These dates are of grade C in this archive: reasoned and widely shared, but not directly attested.

Tradition within Hinduism regards the Vedas as eternal revelation, heard by seers (rishis), not authored in historical time [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
That is a statement about the status of the texts, and it sits alongside, rather than in competition with, the philological dating of the surviving compositions.
The Vedas were preserved by an exacting system of oral recitation, which UNESCO recognises as an intangible heritage [@src.unesco-2003-tradition-vedic-chanting].

## Early Vedic society

The Rigveda describes clans or tribes (jana), led by a chief called rajan, and smaller units such as the vish [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda; @src.thapar-2002-early-india].
Cattle, horses and chariots matter greatly; wealth is counted in livestock, and a hymn complex records a conflict known as the Battle of the Ten Kings (RV 7.18) [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].
The geography of the Rigveda centres on the northwest, the "land of seven rivers", and its river hymns praise the Sarasvati; which river that was is discussed in the article on the Indus civilisation [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda; @src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
Assemblies called sabha and samiti appear, though what powers they had is debated [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].

## Moving east, settling down

In the later Vedic period the centre of gravity moves to the upper Ganga-Yamuna region, the land of the Kuru and Panchala peoples [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
Archaeologically, this area is associated with Painted Grey Ware pottery, found at sites such as Hastinapura and Atranjikhera, and dated by radiocarbon to roughly the early first millennium BCE [@src.gaur-1983-excavations-atranjikhera; @src.allchin-1995-archaeology-early-historic-south-asia].
Whether Painted Grey Ware should be equated with the Vedic people is a matter of scholarly judgement, not demonstration, and several archaeologists caution against identifying a pottery style with a language group [@src.bryant-2001-quest-origins-vedic-culture].

Iron appears in the later texts as "dark metal" (shyama ayas or krishna ayas), and in the archaeology of the Ganga plain in the early first millennium BCE, with some excavators proposing considerably earlier dates for ironworking in the region [@src.chakrabarti-1992-early-use-iron-india; @src.tewari-2003-origins-iron-working-india].
Agriculture intensifies, and larger kingdoms form, among them Kuru and Panchala [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
The elaborate srauta rituals, among them the fire-altar ritual described in the Shatapatha Brahmana, belong to this stage [@src.staal-1983-agni-fire-altar; @src.eggeling-1882-shatapatha-brahmana].

## Varna and jati

The Rigveda uses varna (literally "colour" or "class") first in other senses; its fourfold scheme of Brahmana, Rajanya (later Kshatriya), Vaishya and Shudra appears in a late hymn, the Purusha Sukta (RV 10.90), not in the earlier layers [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].
In the later Vedic texts the fourfold ideal is elaborated by priests, who stand at its head [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].
Varna is a theoretical scheme of four classes; jati, the many endogamous birth-groups that people actually belong to, is a different and much more complex social reality that is documented later, and scholars debate how closely the two relate and how much colonial-era scholarship fixed what had been fluid [@src.bayly-1999-caste-society-politics; @src.olivelle-2005-manus-code-of-law].
For this period, it is safest to say that a hierarchical fourfold ideal was being articulated in priestly literature, not that a caste system as later known was already in place.

## Where did the Vedic people come from?

This is the most contested question in the field.
The older view that "Aryans" were a conquering race has been abandoned; the word arya in the texts is a term of self-description in a cultural and ritual sense, and the nineteenth-century racial reading owes more to European history than to the texts [@src.trautmann-1997-aryans-and-british-india].
Today the debate is about whether Indo-Aryan speakers migrated into South Asia, and when.

- **Migration model.** Vedic Sanskrit and Old Iranian are closely related. Indo-Aryan names and terms appear in a Hittite-Mitanni treaty of the fourteenth century BCE in Syria, far from India [@src.thieme-1960-aryan-gods-mitanni-treaties; @src.beckman-1999-hittite-diplomatic-texts]. Proponents such as J. P. Mallory, David Anthony and Elena Kuzmina argue that speakers moved from the Eurasian steppe through Central Asia in the second millennium BCE [@src.mallory-1989-search-indo-europeans; @src.anthony-2007-horse-wheel-language; @src.kuzmina-2007-origin-indo-iranians]. Ancient DNA from 2019 supports a southward flow of steppe-related ancestry into South Asia in the second millennium BCE [@src.narasimhan-2019-formation-human-populations], and the Rakhigarhi genome from the Indus cities lacks that ancestry [@src.shinde-2019-ancient-harappan-genome].
- **Indigenist model.** Scholars such as B. B. Lal and Shrikant Talageri argue that Vedic culture developed within the subcontinent, pointing to Sarasvati identifications and to the lack of a clear archaeological trace of invasion [@src.lal-2002-saraswati-flows-on; @src.talageri-2000-rigveda-historical-analysis].
- **A middle position.** Many archaeologists accept gradual, small-scale movements rather than a single invasion, and Edwin Bryant's survey notes how much of the argument is about interpretation of ambiguous evidence [@src.bryant-2001-quest-origins-vedic-culture].

The genetic and linguistic findings are, in my reading, difficult to reconcile with strict continuity, but the genetic evidence is statistical, concerns ancestry and not language, and cannot by itself say who composed the Rigveda.

## Toward cities again

By about 600 BCE the Vedic world gives way to the cities and kingdoms described in the article on the Mahajanapadas; the Upanishads, with their questions about the self and rebirth, belong to this transitional age [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.thapar-2002-early-india].
