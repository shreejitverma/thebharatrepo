Around 2,600 years ago, northern India filled with walled towns, coined money and ambitious kings.
Early Buddhist texts describe the political landscape as a set of "great countries", the Mahajanapadas.
This was also the world of the Buddha and Mahavira.

## The sixteen

The Anguttara Nikaya, a Buddhist collection, repeatedly lists sixteen great countries: Anga, Magadha, Kasi, Kosala, Vajji, Malla, Cetiya, Vamsa, Kuru, Panchala, Maccha, Surasena, Assaka, Avanti, Gandhara and Kamboja [@src.bodhi-2012-numerical-discourses-buddha].
Jain texts give a somewhat different list, which shows that "sixteen" is a literary convention rather than a census, and that the lists reflect the perspective of the community that composed them [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].
The Vajji and Malla are usually described as ruled by councils of clan leaders, not by hereditary kings; the others are generally monarchies [@src.thapar-2002-early-india; @src.singh-2008-ancient-early-medieval-india].

The original article called republics "oligarchic assemblies" and treated monarchy and republic as the only two forms.
Historians do describe the republican states (gana-sangha) as governed by assemblies of clan elites, but the sources are thin and later, and scholars disagree on how democratic or how stable these bodies were [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].

## Why now: the second urbanisation

The label "second urbanisation" counts the Indus cities as the first.
The Ganga plain cities developed after a long gap, and their rise is connected to iron tools, intensive rice and wheat agriculture, and trade routes [@src.chakrabarti-1992-early-use-iron-india; @src.allchin-1995-archaeology-early-historic-south-asia].
Archaeologists link the period to Northern Black Polished Ware, a fine pottery found across the plain, and to fortified sites such as Kausambi and Ahichchhatra [@src.allchin-1995-archaeology-early-historic-south-asia; @src.singh-2008-ancient-early-medieval-india].
Silver punch-marked coins, which circulate widely in the period, point to a money economy, though their exact date of origin is debated [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].
Because the older Vedic world lay to the west of these cities, the transition from the Vedic period is described in the companion article.

## The rise of Magadha

Over the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, Magadha, in modern Bihar, grew at the expense of its neighbours.
Texts connect this to the kings Bimbisara and Ajatashatru, who absorbed Anga and fought Kosala and the Vajji confederacy [@src.thapar-2002-early-india; @src.singh-2008-ancient-early-medieval-india].
Explanations offered for Magadha's success include its iron and copper resources, river routes along the Ganga and a fertile rice-growing hinterland [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].
Magadha later became the heartland of the Nanda and Maurya empires.

An alternative view comes from Johannes Bronkhorst, who argues that Magadha and the east formed a distinct "Greater Magadha" cultural area, with ascetic movements that were not rooted in Vedic Brahmanism [@src.bronkhorst-2007-greater-magadha].
This is a minority view of the cultural world that produced Buddhism and Jainism, but it has influenced discussion of how different the east was from the Kuru-Panchala region.

## Dating the age

The dates in the original article for Bimbisara (c. 544 to 492 BCE), the Buddha (563 to 483 BCE) and Mahavira are not secure.
They derive from one reading of the Sri Lankan chronicles, known as the "long chronology".
Many historians now place the Buddha's death later, around 400 BCE, and some move it by a decade or two either way [@src.cousins-1996-dating-historical-buddha].
Consequently, the regnal dates of Bimbisara and Ajatashatru move with it, and the article's dates should be read as one position, not as the standard dating [@src.thapar-2002-early-india; @src.cousins-1996-dating-historical-buddha].
The earliest firmly dated anchors in this region come later, with Alexander's campaign in the northwest in 327 to 325 BCE and the Maurya dynasty [@src.kulke-rothermund-2004-history-of-india].

## The northwest

Gandhara and Kamboja lay in the far northwest, a region that came under the Achaemenid Persian empire in the later sixth century BCE [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].
Panini, who composed the Sanskrit grammar Ashtadhyayi, is generally placed in this northwestern milieu around the fifth or fourth century BCE, though his date is itself debated [@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions; @src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini].

## What this period leaves behind

The Mahajanapada period established the political and economic template of early historic India: territorial kingdoms, cities, coinage and the beginnings of administrative bureaucracy.
It is also the setting in which Buddhism and Jainism arose, and in which the Upanishadic tradition continued [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.thapar-2002-early-india].
Most of our narrative comes from religious texts compiled centuries afterward, so the picture should be treated with caution.
