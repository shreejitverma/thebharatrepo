At independence in 1947 India was poor, mostly rural and largely illiterate, and it was divided and in the middle of a refugee crisis.
Many observers outside the country expected it to break up or lose its democracy.
It did not break up, and it has held regular elections since, though the quality of its democracy and the distribution of its growth are still debated [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].

## The Constitution

The Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949, and it came into force on 26 January 1950, a date now marked as Republic Day.
It guaranteed fundamental rights and universal adult suffrage, and it set up a federal parliamentary system [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].
B. R. Ambedkar chaired the drafting committee.
The first general election was held in 1951 and 1952, with a very large electorate, most of whom could not read [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].

## Building the state

The states were reorganised largely on linguistic lines from 1956, after agitation, and this has proved more compatible with national unity than many of the founders feared [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].
Jawaharlal Nehru, prime minister until 1964, supported planned industrial growth with a large public sector, and a policy of non-alignment in foreign affairs [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].
The war with China in 1962 was a military defeat and an important political shock [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].

## Food and the Green Revolution

India depended on large food imports in the early 1960s.
From the mid-1960s the government promoted high-yielding wheat and later rice varieties, together with fertiliser, irrigation and guaranteed procurement prices, and output grew, first in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].
The original claim that this achieved food self-sufficiency is too simple.
Grain output rose and large-scale imports ended, but the gains were regional, favoured wheat and richer farmers, and did not remove widespread undernutrition [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].

## War, the Emergency and politics

In December 1971 India and Pakistan fought a war that followed the crisis in East Pakistan, where millions of refugees crossed into India.
It ended in the surrender of Pakistani forces in Dhaka and the creation of Bangladesh.
Srinath Raghavan argues that the outcome was shaped by Cold War diplomacy and Indian decisions as much as by military events [@src.raghavan-2013-1971].

In June 1975 Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, after a court ruling against her election, declared a national Emergency.
Civil liberties were suspended, the press was censored and many opposition leaders were jailed.
It ended in 1977, when Congress lost the general election [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].
In the following decades, politics became more competitive, with regional parties, coalition governments and new demands from lower-caste groups [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].

## Nuclear and space programmes

India carried out an underground nuclear test at Pokhran in May 1974, which the government called a peaceful nuclear explosion, and conducted a series of tests there in May 1998, after which it declared itself a nuclear-weapon state [@src.perkovich-1999-indias-nuclear-bomb].
The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) launched its first satellite, Aryabhata, in 1975.
Chandrayaan-1 was launched in 2008, and Chandrayaan-3 landed on 23 August 2023 in the Moon's south polar region, at roughly 69 degrees south latitude and not at the pole itself.

## Economic reform

For its first four decades the economy was heavily regulated, with licences controlling investment and high tariffs on imports.
Growth was low by the standards of East Asia, though it accelerated in the 1980s [@src.panagariya-2008-india-the-emerging-giant].
In 1991, a balance-of-payments crisis led the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao, with finance minister Manmohan Singh, to devalue the rupee, reduce industrial licensing and lower tariffs [@src.panagariya-2008-india-the-emerging-giant].
Growth was faster afterward and poverty fell, though economists differ on how much of the change comes from the 1991 reforms and how much from earlier shifts, and inequality has risen [@src.panagariya-2008-india-the-emerging-giant].

## Continuing questions

Several issues remain open.
Relations with Pakistan and China, the status of Kashmir, the politics of religion and caste, and the balance between economic growth and welfare are all subjects of serious scholarly and political disagreement, and this article does not attempt to settle them [@src.guha-2007-india-after-gandhi].
