Around 4,500 years ago, in a region that is now Pakistan and northwestern India, people built cities with brick-lined drains, standardised weights and carved stone seals.
We call their world the Indus or Harappan civilisation, after the Indus river and after Harappa, the first of its sites to be dug.
Nobody knows what they called themselves, because their script has not been read.

## Name, place and time

"Indus Valley" is a convenient label, not a boundary.
Its sites spread well beyond the Indus itself, along the Ghaggar-Hakra channels, into Gujarat, and as far north as Shortugai on the Oxus in Afghanistan [@src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization; @src.wright-2010-ancient-indus].
Estimates of its extent depend on how one draws the edges, but it is often described as among the most extensive early urban societies of the Old World [@src.wright-2010-ancient-indus].

Archaeologists divide the story into phases.
An Early Harappan phase, roughly 3300 to 2600 BCE, shows regional village and town cultures growing together; the Mature phase, roughly 2600 to 1900 BCE, is the age of the big cities; and a Late phase, to about 1300 BCE, is a time of regional change [@src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization].
These are scholarly estimates based on radiocarbon and stratigraphy, and the boundaries shift by a century or so depending on the site.

The old description of the Indus as "one of three early civilisations of the Near East and South Asia" needs care.
Mesopotamia, Egypt and the Indus are indeed the three best-known early urban traditions west of China, but they are not the only early civilisations in the world, and "three" is a convention of textbooks rather than a finding [@src.wright-2010-ancient-indus; @src.coningham-2015-archaeology-south-asia].

## Roots

Farming villages existed in the region long before the cities.
At Mehrgarh, on the edge of the Indus plains, farming and herding are attested from about 7000 BCE, and the site shows continuing craft development afterwards [@src.coppa-2006-mehrgarh-dentistry; @src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization].
Whether the cities grew mainly out of these local roots or received strong stimulus from outside is a question most specialists answer in favour of local development, though with regional contacts [@src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization; @src.kenoyer-1998-ancient-cities-indus].

## Cities and craft

Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi and Ganweriwala are the largest known sites.
Excavators at Mohenjo-daro found streets on a grid, houses with bathrooms, and covered drains leading to the street [@src.marshall-1931-mohenjo-daro; @src.kenoyer-1998-ancient-cities-indus].
Dholavira, in Kutch, is notable for its sophisticated system of reservoirs and channels for storing seasonal water, recognised by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 2021 [@src.unesco-2021-dholavira].

Bricks were made in consistent proportions across great distances, and cubical stone weights follow a regular series, suggesting shared standards of measurement and perhaps of trade regulation [@src.kenoyer-1998-ancient-cities-indus].
Craftspeople drilled long beads of carnelian with specialised techniques, and shell, copper and steatite work were widespread [@src.kenoyer-vidale-1992-stone-drills; @src.kenoyer-1998-ancient-cities-indus].

Evidence of trade is strong.
Indus-style seals and beads turn up in Mesopotamia, and Mesopotamian texts mention a distant land called Meluhha, which many scholars identify with the Indus region, though the identification is an inference rather than a direct statement [@src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization].
The Lothal "dock" has long been cited as proof of maritime commerce; its function is debated, with some excavators calling it a harbour and others an irrigation or water-storage basin [@src.leshnik-1968-harappan-port-lothal].

## The script and what we cannot say

Thousands of inscribed seals and tablets survive, but most carry very short texts, and no bilingual inscription has been found [@src.parpola-1994-deciphering-indus-script].
Scholars disagree whether the signs record a language at all.
One study argued that the statistical structure of the signs resembles that of linguistic systems [@src.rao-2009-entropic-evidence-indus-script].
Another found that the evidence does not distinguish the Indus signs from non-linguistic symbol systems [@src.sproat-2014-statistical-comparison-symbol-systems].
Proposed decipherments, including Dravidian readings by Asko Parpola, remain unproven [@src.parpola-1994-deciphering-indus-script].
Because we cannot read the texts, claims about Indus religion or kingship rest on objects such as seals, figurines and architecture, and are interpretations, not records.
The "Priest-King" and "Pashupati" labels, for example, were modern coinages from analogy with later traditions [@src.marshall-1931-mohenjo-daro; @src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization].

## Transformation

From about 1900 BCE, the largest cities declined, long-distance exchange contracted and populations shifted toward the east and south [@src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization; @src.wright-2010-ancient-indus].
The idea that invaders destroyed the cities has not been supported by the skeletal and architectural evidence [@src.kenoyer-1998-ancient-cities-indus; @src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization].
Researchers now stress a mix of causes.
Records of a weakening monsoon and a drying climate around 4,200 years ago, the shifting of river courses, and changes in trade have all been proposed [@src.staubwasser-2003-climate-change-4-2-ka; @src.dixit-2014-abrupt-weakening-monsoon; @src.giosan-2012-fluvial-landscapes-harappan].
Different regions plausibly responded differently: some adapted to variable rainfall by changing crops and moving settlements [@src.petrie-2017-adaptation-variable-environments].

## What genetics adds

A genome recovered from a skeleton at Rakhigarhi, dated to around 2800 to 2300 BCE, shows ancestry related to Iranian-plateau farmers and South Asian hunter-gatherers, with no detectable steppe pastoralist ancestry [@src.shinde-2019-ancient-harappan-genome].
This is one piece of evidence in the continuing debate over how Indo-Aryan languages reached South Asia, discussed in the article on the Vedic period.
One genome from one site cannot stand for a whole civilisation, and the language the Harappans spoke is still unknown.

## Open questions

The Ghaggar-Hakra river system, which many Harappan sites line, is identified by some scholars with the Rigvedic Sarasvati, and by others it is considered a separate river or an inconclusive identification; the position is argued on hydrological, textual and archaeological grounds [@src.lal-2002-saraswati-flows-on; @src.danino-2010-lost-river-sarasvati; @src.giosan-2012-fluvial-landscapes-harappan].
How the cities were governed, and what their people believed, remain open.
What is secure is the scale of their engineering and the reach of their exchange networks, and that their story is much more than an "invasion" prelude to later history.
