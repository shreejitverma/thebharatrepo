For about two centuries, from the early 4th century CE, a dynasty called the Guptas dominated the Ganges plain and, at times, much of northern and central India.
We know their story unusually well, because they left dated inscriptions, a rich gold coinage and fine sculpture, and because Chinese pilgrims wrote about the lands they governed [@src.fleet-1888-inscriptions-early-gupta-kings; @src.agrawal-1989-rise-and-fall-imperial-guptas].

## The first kings

The dynasty's early rulers, Gupta and Ghatotkacha, are known mostly by name.
The first major figure was Chandragupta I, who married Kumaradevi of the Licchavi family, a connection his son stressed in his inscriptions.
A new calendar, the Gupta era, begins in 319 or 320 CE, and later Gupta records are dated in it.
That date marks the start of the era, and probably the start of the dynasty's rise; the imperial "zenith" came later [@src.fleet-1888-inscriptions-early-gupta-kings; @src.thapar-2002-early-india].

## Samudragupta and Chandragupta II

Samudragupta (reigned from about 335 CE) is known from a long praise-poem, composed by his courtier Harishena, carved on an Ashokan pillar at Prayagraj (Allahabad).
It lists northern kings he uprooted, southern kings he defeated and then restored, and frontier states and distant rulers that paid tribute or homage [@src.fleet-1888-inscriptions-early-gupta-kings].
Like every royal eulogy, it is a statement of how the court wished the king to be seen, and historians read it as such, noting the difference between direct rule in the Gangetic core and looser overlordship elsewhere [@src.thapar-2002-early-india; @src.agrawal-1989-rise-and-fall-imperial-guptas].
His coins show him as a warrior, a lute player and a performer of the horse sacrifice.

His son Chandragupta II (c. 376 to 415 CE) defeated the Shaka Western Kshatrapa rulers of Gujarat and Malwa, which opened the western coast to the Guptas and their trade.
The Chinese Buddhist monk Faxian travelled in India in his reign and described a peaceful, well-run country in Buddhist terms [@src.agrawal-1989-rise-and-fall-imperial-guptas; @src.singh-2008-ancient-early-medieval-india].
Later Sanskrit tradition associates the poet Kalidasa with a king called Vikramaditya, and some scholars identify this king with Chandragupta II, but that identification is not demonstrated [@src.singh-2008-ancient-early-medieval-india].

## Late Guptas and the Hunas

Kumaragupta I ruled for about four decades, and under his son Skandagupta the dynasty faced invaders from Central Asia known as the Hunas.
Skandagupta's inscriptions claim that he repelled them, but the strain on the treasury shows up in the debasement of Gupta gold coins in his period [@src.agrawal-1989-rise-and-fall-imperial-guptas].
After about 467 CE the central line weakened, regional governors and allies became independent, and the Huna rulers Toramana and Mihirakula pressed from the northwest.
The dynasty lingered in a reduced form until about the middle of the 6th century [@src.agrawal-1989-rise-and-fall-imperial-guptas; @src.thapar-2002-early-india].

## Society, religion and the arts

Gupta kings issued copper-plate charters granting villages and land revenue to brahmanas and religious institutions.
Historians use these to trace a shift toward local landed intermediaries and an increasingly regionalised polity [@src.thapar-2002-early-india; @src.singh-2008-ancient-early-medieval-india].
The kings themselves described themselves as devotees of Vishnu while supporting Buddhist and Jain communities too [@src.agrawal-1989-rise-and-fall-imperial-guptas].

This is the age when the stone Hindu temple, as an architectural form, appears in northern India.
Early examples at sites such as Udayagiri near Vidisha and Deogarh have been studied as the setting in which images of gods were given permanent homes [@src.willis-2009-archaeology-of-hindu-ritual].
Gupta-period sculpture, with smooth, refined figures of the Buddha at Sarnath and Mathura, set a style that spread widely [@src.williams-1982-art-of-gupta-india].
The great painted caves at Ajanta in the Deccan are mostly of the same century, but they were patronised by the Vakataka dynasty and its nobles, not by the Guptas themselves [@src.singh-2008-ancient-early-medieval-india].

In science, the astronomer and mathematician Aryabhata composed his *Aryabhatiya* in 499 CE, setting out a method for computing planetary positions and treating mathematical topics including a close approximation of pi [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
He worked at Kusumapura (identified with Pataliputra), but the text names no king, so it is a product of the Gupta age, not a Gupta commission [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].

## Was it a "Golden Age"?

The phrase arose in the 19th and early 20th centuries, partly because scholars wanted a classical age of Indian civilisation to match Greece and Rome.
Modern historians point out that much of what is admired, in literature, mathematics, trade and religion, also flourished in other regions and centuries, and that Gupta power was concentrated in the north [@src.thapar-2002-early-india; @src.williams-1982-art-of-gupta-india].
The word "classical" is more defensible than "golden", and it should be used with the evidence in mind.
