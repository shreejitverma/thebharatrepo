The Delhi Sultanate is the name historians give to a sequence of five dynasties that ruled from Delhi between 1206 and 1526.
They were Turkic or Afghan in origin, they wrote in Persian, and at their widest extent they controlled most of northern India and reached deep into the Deccan [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
The story is one of ambitious conquerors, fragile succession, Mongol threats, remarkable buildings and a long argument among modern historians about how to read the evidence.

## From Ghur to Delhi

The Sultanate grew out of the military expansion of the Ghurids, a dynasty from the mountains of central Afghanistan.
In 1192 the Ghurid ruler Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad defeated the Chahamana (Chauhan) king Prithviraj III at Tarain, north of Delhi, and left his slave-commanders to hold the conquered ground [@src.raverty-1881-tabakat-i-nasiri; @src.wink-2002-al-hind-volume-2].
Military slavery was a normal route to power in the Turko-Persian world, so a "slave" commander could be a great general and governor, not a menial.
When Mu'izz al-Din died in 1206, his commander Qutb al-Din Aibak ruled as an independent sultan, which is why 1206 is the conventional starting date [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].

Aibak's successor Iltutmish (reigned 1211 to 1236) secured the new state and received formal recognition from the Abbasid caliph in 1229 [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
His daughter Raziyya ruled for a few years after him, a rare case of a woman holding the throne [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
The label "Slave Dynasty" is a modern convenience, since the rulers of this first phase came from different lineages.
Many historians therefore prefer to speak of the early sultans individually or use the term Mamluk with care.

## Khalji and Tughluq expansion

In 1290 Jalal al-Din Khalji seized power, and in 1296 his nephew Ala al-Din Khalji murdered him and took the throne [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
Ala al-Din's reign (1296 to 1316) is the Sultanate's turning point.
His armies repelled several Mongol invasions, conquered Gujarat and Rajasthan strongholds, and, under his general Malik Kafur, raided the Deccan and the far south, extracting tribute from the Yadavas, Kakatiyas, Hoysalas and Pandyas [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].

The chronicler Ziya al-Din Barani, writing decades later, credits Ala al-Din with fixing the prices of grain, cloth and horses in Delhi to feed a very large army cheaply.
Modern historians accept that some such controls existed but debate how far they reached and how long they lasted [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate; @src.raychaudhuri-1982-cambridge-economic-history-india].

The Tughluq dynasty (1320 to 1414) took the Sultanate to its largest extent.
Muhammad bin Tughluq (reigned 1325 to 1351) tried bold experiments, among them a second capital at Daulatabad in the Deccan around 1327 and the issue of token currency [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
It is common to call the Daulatabad scheme a failure, but that is Barani's verdict.
Reports of a forced evacuation of Delhi, and the scale and aims of the move, are interpreted differently by modern scholars, and Delhi remained a major city [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
Ibn Battuta, a Moroccan traveller who served the sultan as a judge for several years in the 1330s, left a vivid eyewitness account of the court [@src.battuta-2004-travels-asia-africa].

## Fragmentation, Timur and the last dynasties

Distance and revolt wore the state down.
Under Muhammad bin Tughluq, Madurai, Bengal and the Deccan slipped away, and in 1347 the Bahmani Sultanate was founded in the Deccan [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
The Vijayanagara kingdom also emerged in the south in these years, as the companion article on that empire describes.
Firuz Shah Tughluq (1351 to 1388) restored order, dug canals, founded new towns and brought two ancient Ashokan pillars to Delhi, but the state remained brittle [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].

In December 1398 the Central Asian conqueror Timur sacked Delhi with great loss of life, which broke the Tughluq state [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
The Sayyid dynasty (1414 to 1451) and then the Afghan Lodi dynasty (1451 to 1526) ruled a much smaller territory.
Ibrahim Lodi was defeated and killed by Babur at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526, and the Mughal Empire began [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate; @src.richards-1993-mughal-empire].

## How the Sultanate worked

The Sultanate was a military state.
Its backbone was an aristocracy of soldiers paid from the revenue of assigned districts (iqta), and from the Delhi region it standardised silver and copper coinage, whose legends survive as dated evidence [@src.raychaudhuri-1982-cambridge-economic-history-india].
Persian was the language of court and administration, and the Sufi orders, notably the Chishti order based in Delhi, became important centres of devotion [@src.eaton-2019-india-persianate-age].
Local Hindu officials, merchants and bankers remained essential to collecting revenue and financing the state [@src.raychaudhuri-1982-cambridge-economic-history-india].

Sanskrit and regional languages continued alongside Persian.
The Sultanate-era vernacular of the Delhi region was called Hindavi, and the name Urdu for the language comes much later [@src.eaton-2019-india-persianate-age].

In architecture, Sultanate builders worked with Indian craftsmen and brought techniques of true arches, vaults and domes, which they combined with the older post-and-lintel tradition of Indian masonry [@src.asher-1992-architecture-of-mughal-india].
The Qutb Minar and the neighbouring mosque, begun at the end of the twelfth century, are the best known examples.
Arches and domed forms were not entirely new to India, so the older claim that the Sultanate "introduced" them is too simple.

## Conflict, temples and coexistence

This is the most contested part of the Sultanate's history, and the evidence has to be read with care.
Persian chronicles of the period often describe campaigns in the language of holy war and the smashing of idols, and some scholars see this as a sincere religious motive [@src.wink-2002-al-hind-volume-2].

Richard Eaton compiled about eighty documented cases of temple desecration in India between 1192 and 1760.
He argued that most were acts of political warfare, directed at temples that embodied the legitimacy of a defeated royal house.
Hindu kings did the same to each other's royal temples before and during the Sultanate era, and temples under the sultans' own rule were generally left alone, often protected and sometimes given grants [@src.eaton-2000-temple-desecration].
Cynthia Talbot's study of inscriptions from Andhra found that Hindus and Muslims did not see each other mainly through a lens of religious opposition [@src.talbot-1995-inscribing-the-other].
The numbers circulating in modern nationalist rhetoric, which Talbot notes can run to tens of thousands of temples, are far larger than anything the documented cases support [@src.talbot-1995-inscribing-the-other; @src.eaton-2000-temple-desecration].

Eaton's reading has critics.
Some, such as Babul Roy, argue that chronicle evidence alone is not enough and that the political explanation understates religious motive [@src.roy-2023-temple-desecration-discussion].
The responsible position is to hold both facts together: real violence and real temple destruction occurred, and so did patronage, accommodation and ordinary daily cooperation.

## How we know

Our main narrative sources are Persian chronicles: Minhaj-i Siraj's *Tabaqat-i Nasiri* (about 1260), Barani's *Tarikh-i Firuz Shahi* (about 1357) and others [@src.raverty-1881-tabakat-i-nasiri].
They are court histories, written for patrons and shaped by their authors' views, so historians test them against coins, inscriptions in Persian, Arabic and Sanskrit, monuments, and outside witnesses such as Ibn Battuta [@src.battuta-2004-travels-asia-africa; @src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate].
Modern syntheses by Jackson and Eaton give the best overviews [@src.jackson-1999-delhi-sultanate; @src.eaton-2019-india-persianate-age].
