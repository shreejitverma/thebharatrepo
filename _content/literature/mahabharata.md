The Mahabharata is a Sanskrit epic of about 100,000 verses in its traditional form, many times longer than the Greek Iliad and Odyssey put together.
It tells how two branches of the royal family of the Kurus, the five Pandava brothers and their hundred Kaurava cousins, quarrel over the kingdom at Hastinapura and fight an eighteen-day war on the plain of Kurukshetra [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
That plot is only the frame.
The poem also holds dozens of subsidiary stories, long discourses on kingship, law and liberation, and the *Bhagavad Gita*, the dialogue between the warrior Arjuna and his charioteer Krishna, which sits in the sixth book, the Bhishma Parva [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].

## The story and its eighteen books

The epic is divided into eighteen *parvans* (books).
The first, the Adi Parva, tells of the ancestry of the Kuru house and the births of the heroes.
The middle books follow the dice game in which Yudhishthira loses his kingdom, the thirteen years of exile, and the failed embassy before the war.
The war books run from the Bhishma Parva to the Sauptika Parva.
The long twelfth and thirteenth books, the Shanti and Anushasana Parvas, are given to the instruction of the victorious king Yudhishthira on dharma, governance and release [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics; @src.fitzgerald-2004-mahabharata-books-11-12].
The poem frames itself as recited by Vaishampayana at the snake sacrifice of Janamejaya, the great-grandson of the Pandavas, and then retold by the bard Ugrashravas to sages in the Naimisha forest [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
Tradition ascribes the whole to the sage Vyasa, who is also a character in the story.

## A text that grew

No scholar treats the Mahabharata as the work of one author at one moment.
The earlier forms of the story are usually imagined as bardic poetry, and the title *Jaya* ("victory") and then *Bharata* are names that tradition gives to smaller versions of the poem [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
The oldest datable traces of the story are modest.
Panini's grammar, the *Ashtadhyayi*, shows that Vasudeva and Arjuna were already objects of devotion when it was composed, and the name Mahabharata appears in later ritual manuals; Brockington reviews this evidence [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
Stone and copper-plate inscriptions of the Gupta age speak of a "hundred-thousand verse" Mahabharata, which tells us that the huge version existed by about the 5th century CE [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].

Between these points scholars see growth by stages.
The usual mainstream estimate for the period of composition and expansion is about 400 BCE to 400 CE, with the heroic core earlier than the didactic books and the *Gita* and the avatara theology of Krishna among the later layers [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
This is a reasoned estimate with real disagreement at the edges, so it is graded C here.
James Fitzgerald, in the long introduction to his translation of the Books of the Women and of Peace, argues for a more deliberate shaping of the epic as a Brahmanical response to the world after the Mauryan emperor Ashoka, which would mean a more compact composition than a slow bardic accretion [@src.fitzgerald-2004-mahabharata-books-11-12].

The standard modern text is the Critical Edition prepared at the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute in Pune, completed in 1966 under V. S. Sukthankar and later editors.
It reconstructs the oldest text that the manuscripts allow and comes out noticeably shorter than the 100,000 verses of the traditional vulgate [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].

## When did the war happen? Three kinds of answer

It helps to keep two questions apart: when the *text* was composed, and when the *events* it describes are placed.

### Scholarship: a possible Kuru kernel

The Kuru realm, the Panchalas and the king Parikshit are known independently from the Vedic literature, where Parikshit and Janamejaya appear in texts such as the Atharvaveda hymns, the Aitareya Brahmana and the Shatapatha Brahmana [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
Michael Witzel places the rise of the Kuru state in the early first millennium BCE, in the early Iron Age of the upper Ganga-Yamuna region [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
Archaeologically that world is associated with Painted Grey Ware settlements, and B. B. Lal's 1950-52 excavation at Hastinapura found Painted Grey Ware levels and, above them, a flood deposit, which Lal related to the Puranic tradition that the Ganga washed Hastinapura away [@src.lal-1955-excavation-hastinapura].
Romila Thapar and others caution that a match of this kind proves little: the epic took shape centuries later, and nothing outside it records a single great war [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].
Many historians therefore suppose a smaller conflict among Kuru-Panchala peoples as the memory behind the poem, perhaps in the Late Vedic period, without claiming that it can be dated or even securely shown to have happened.

### Tradition: the turn of the yugas

In the traditional reckoning the war falls at the junction of the Dvapara and Kali yugas.
The astronomer Aryabhata, writing in 499 CE, fixed the beginning of the Kali Yuga at what we call 3102 BCE, and this became the usual traditional date for the war and the death of Krishna [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india; @src.pingree-1981-jyotihsastra].
A second astronomical tradition, that of Varahamihira, counts 2526 years from King Yudhishthira to the Shaka era and so reaches about 2449 BCE [@src.pingree-1981-jyotihsastra].
These dates are part of a cosmological scheme and they are presented here as tradition, graded D, not as results of archaeology or text criticism.

### Astronomical reconstructions

Since the late 20th century several authors have tried to fix the war by matching the planetary and eclipse observations scattered through the epic against computer sky models.
B. N. Narahari Achar used planetarium software and, stressing Saturn's position near Rohini and the pair of eclipses before the war, argued for 3067 BCE [@src.achar-2018-correspondence-bhatnagar].
Ashok K. Bhatnagar, an astronomer formerly of the India Meteorological Department, reassessed the same references in the *Indian Journal of History of Science* and concluded that the war began on 14 October 1793 BCE [@src.bhatnagar-2017-date-mahabharata-war].
These two results are about 1,300 years apart, and other authors have published still other dates.
The method assumes that the observations in the poem are precise, that they reflect real sightings and not later poetic or ritual composition, and that the text has not been altered, which are the very points at issue.
Because the results disagree and rest on single-author reconstructions, they are graded E, speculative, without wide acceptance among historians and astronomers.

## How we know, and what we do not

What we can say with confidence is that the Mahabharata is a composite text of the first millennium BCE and the early centuries CE, that it was given its present length in the Gupta period or before, and that its heroes belong to a world of Kuru-Panchala kingship that is also visible in Vedic literature.
What we cannot say is that the war happened on a particular day, or at all, as described.
Each of the three positions above answers a different question, and treating them as rival answers to a single question is the commonest source of confusion.
