Kalidasa wrote in Sanskrit, probably between the 4th and 5th centuries CE, and many readers rank him the finest poet and dramatist in that language.
Seven works are traditionally credited to him: three plays, two long poems and two shorter ones.
Everything else about him is inference.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry]

## The works

The plays are *Malavikagnimitra* ("Malavika and Agnimitra"), *Vikramorvashiya* ("Urvashi Won by Valour") and *Abhijnanashakuntala* ("The Recognition of Shakuntala").
The two long poems (*mahakavya*) are *Raghuvamsha* ("The Dynasty of Raghu") and *Kumarasambhava* ("The Birth of Kumara").
The shorter poems are *Meghaduta* ("The Cloud Messenger"), in which an exiled spirit asks a monsoon cloud to carry a message to his wife, and *Ritusamhara* ("The Gathering of the Seasons"), a poem on the six seasons.
Some scholars doubt that the *Ritusamhara* is by the same author as the others.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry]

## Where his stories come from

The old summary that his works rest on "the Vedas, the Ramayana, the Mahabharata and the Puranas" is partly right and partly too broad.
The story of *Shakuntala* is taken from the *Mahabharata*, where it is told much more bluntly, and Kalidasa reshapes it into a drama of memory, loss and recognition.
*Raghuvamsha* retells the family of Rama and draws on the *Ramayana*.
*Vikramorvashiya* develops the old tale of the king Pururavas and the nymph Urvashi, which is already told in a dialogue hymn of the *Rigveda* and again in the *Shatapatha Brahmana*.
*Malavikagnimitra*, however, is a court comedy built around a historical king, Agnimitra of the Shunga dynasty, and has no epic source.
[@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics; @src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda; @src.eggeling-1900-satapatha-brahmana-part-5, 11.5.1]

## Style and reputation

Kalidasa's gift lies in the compression and tenderness of his Sanskrit, and in his handling of nature.
In the *Meghaduta*, the route of the cloud over the Indian landscape becomes a map of longing.
Later Sanskrit critics treated his verses as the model of the courtly style, and later poets imitated him and wrote commentaries on his work.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry; @src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods]

## Dating him: what is known

Three pieces of evidence bracket him.

**An earliest date.**
*Malavikagnimitra* makes Agnimitra, a Shunga ruler of the 2nd century BCE, its hero, so the poet is later than that king.
It also names Bhasa, Saumilla and Kaviputra as earlier poets.
That is a very loose lower bound.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry]

**A latest date.**
The Aihole inscription, composed by the poet Ravikirti for the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II and dated to 634 CE, praises Ravikirti as having won the fame of Kalidasa and Bharavi.
It is the earliest inscription known to name Kalidasa.
[@src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods; @src.salomon-1998-indian-epigraphy]

**A closer upper bound, with a caveat.**
A Mandasor inscription of 473 CE, composed by one Vatsabhatti, appears to contain verses that echo Kalidasa's poems, among them the *Meghaduta* and the *Ritusamhara*.
If those echoes are real, Kalidasa was earlier than 473.
Not every scholar accepts that the verbal parallels prove borrowing.
[@src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods]

On the basis of these brackets and the style of his work, many scholars place him in the 4th to 5th century CE, in the time of Chandragupta II (reigned about 380 to 415 CE) or of his immediate successors.
Others argue for different dates, and the identification with a particular Gupta patron has no contemporary documentary support.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry]

## The tradition of Vikramaditya

Indian tradition places Kalidasa at the court of a King Vikramaditya of Ujjain, as one of the "Nine Gems", and links that king with the era beginning in 57 BCE.
Taken as tradition, this is a coherent and widely told story.
As history it is not supported by the surviving evidence: the Nine Gems story is attested only long after the period, and a 1st-century BCE date sits uneasily with the language and literary conventions of Kalidasa's works, which are those of a fully developed courtly style.
This site therefore records the tradition as a traditional dating, not as an established one.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry]

## What is not known

We do not know where he was born, what he did for a living, or whom he served.
Stories about his life are legends from later centuries, and they should be read as evidence of his reputation, not of his biography.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry]
