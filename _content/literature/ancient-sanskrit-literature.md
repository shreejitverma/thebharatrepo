Sanskrit began as the language of ritual and hymn, but it did not stay there.
Over several centuries around the turn of the Common Era it also became a language of grammar, epic storytelling, plays, lyric poetry and prose romance.
This article follows that widening, and is honest about how little is firmly dated.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry; @src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods]

## From Vedic speech to a fixed language

The oldest Sanskrit is Vedic.
It was gradually standardised by grammarians, above all Panini, whose *Ashtadhyayi* describes the language in about four thousand compact rules.
Scholars place Panini somewhere between the 6th and 4th centuries BCE, and the date is argued over rather than settled.
[@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions]

Patanjali's *Mahabhasya*, a commentary on Panini and on his early critic Katyayana, is usually placed around the middle of the 2nd century BCE.
By then Sanskrit was a language with a fixed grammar, learned by study rather than simply inherited at home.
[@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions; @src.thapar-2002-early-india]

Meanwhile the two great epics, the *Mahabharata* and the *Ramayana*, were growing into their present shape.
Their composition was not a single event: both were expanded and revised across many generations, and the stretch from about 400 BCE to about 400 CE is commonly given for this process.
They are the background against which later poets worked, and the subject matter of many of their plays and poems.
[@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics]

## The rise of kavya

The Sanskrit word *kavya* covers ornate, self-consciously artistic literature, in verse or prose.
Its early history is best seen not in manuscripts but in inscriptions, which can be dated.
The Junagadh rock inscription of the Western Kshatrapa ruler Rudradaman, dated to the year 72 of the Shaka era (about 150 CE), is one of the earliest long Sanskrit inscriptions and is written in an already polished literary prose.
Such a text implies a literary culture that was well formed before it was carved in stone.
[@src.salomon-1998-indian-epigraphy; @src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods]

The Buddhist poet Ashvaghosha, whose *Buddhacarita* retells the life of the Buddha in polished court verse, is usually placed around the 1st to 2nd century CE, in the Kushan age.
Some of his work survives complete, and it shows that the new style was not confined to Brahmanical authors.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry]

## Drama

Sanskrit plays survive from this period, but their authors are often hard to pin down.

**Bhasa.**
The play *Svapnavasavadatta* ("The Dream of Vasavadatta") belongs to a group of thirteen plays published in 1912 by T. Ganapati Sastri from palm-leaf manuscripts found in Kerala.
Sastri attributed them all to Bhasa, a dramatist whom Kalidasa names with respect as a predecessor.
Scholars still disagree about whether one author wrote all thirteen, and the date of Bhasa is uncertain; it is placed anywhere between the last centuries BCE and the 4th century CE, and certainly before Kalidasa.
The older formulation "c. 2nd to 3rd century CE" is therefore one guess among several.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry]

**Shudraka.**
The *Mricchakatika* ("The Little Clay Cart") follows the impoverished merchant-Brahmin Charudatta and the courtesan Vasantasena, with a political revolution woven through the plot.
Its prologue credits a king named Shudraka, but nothing independent shows who he was or when he lived.
The play has a close relationship to the unfinished *Charudatta* among the plays ascribed to Bhasa, and scholars debate which came first.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry]

**Vishakhadatta.**
The *Mudrarakshasa* is a political drama about the minister Chanakya and his intrigues to secure the throne of Chandragupta Maurya.
It was written many centuries after the Mauryan events it dramatises, and its own date is argued, with proposals ranging across the Gupta and post-Gupta centuries.
It should be read as a later literary imagining of Maurya politics, not as a contemporary record.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry; @src.thapar-2002-early-india]

The most celebrated dramatist, Kalidasa, has his own article.

## Prose and the court of Harsha

Banabhatta served at the court of King Harsha of Kanyakubja, who ruled in the first half of the 7th century CE.
His *Harshacharita* is a prose life of his patron, and his *Kadambari* is a long romance in intricately ornamented prose.
Because Harsha's reign is independently documented, Bana is one of the few Sanskrit authors who can be fixed to within a few decades.
[@src.lienhard-1984-history-classical-poetry; @src.thapar-2002-early-india]

## The "Golden Age" label

Older histories called the period of the Gupta kings (roughly the 4th to mid-6th century CE) the golden age of Sanskrit literature.
The label is misleading in two ways.
Much of what it covers is earlier or later than the Guptas: Ashvaghosha and Rudradaman precede them, and Bana follows them by about a century.
And Sanskrit literary culture was by then supported by many courts across the subcontinent, not only by one dynasty.
[@src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods; @src.thapar-2002-early-india]

## How we know

Almost no Sanskrit manuscript from before the second millennium CE has survived on the subcontinent, so the date of an author is usually inferred rather than read off a document.
Historians combine several kinds of evidence: quotations by later authors, who set a latest date; references to datable kings, which set an earliest date; and inscriptions, which both show literary styles in use and sometimes name poets.
The 634 CE Aihole inscription, for instance, praises the poets Kalidasa and Bharavi, and so fixes a latest date for both.
This method yields reliable ranges for some authors and only broad guesses for others.
[@src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods; @src.salomon-1998-indian-epigraphy]
