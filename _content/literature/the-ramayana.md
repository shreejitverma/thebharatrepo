The Ramayana tells of Rama, prince of Ayodhya, who is sent into forest exile through a palace intrigue, loses his wife Sita to the demon king Ravana of Lanka, and wins her back with the help of the monkey warrior Hanuman and an army of forest allies.
It is the second of the two great Sanskrit epics, and in tradition it is the first poem, the *adikavya*, composed by the sage Valmiki [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
It is much shorter than the Mahabharata, at about 24,000 verses in the traditional count, and its single, tightly built plot has made it one of the most retold stories in the world.

## The story in seven books

Valmiki's text has seven *kandas*: Bala (childhood), Ayodhya, Aranya (forest), Kishkindha, Sundara, Yuddha (war) and Uttara (the later book).
The Ayodhya Kanda holds the exile, forced by the promise a king once made to his queen Kaikeyi.
In the forest Sita is abducted; Rama allies with the monkey king Sugriva, Hanuman leaps to Lanka and finds Sita, and the war in the sixth book ends in the death of Ravana [@src.goldman-1984-ramayana-balakanda].
The seventh book tells of the aftermath, including Rama's rejection of Sita and the births of their sons [@src.goldman-1984-ramayana-balakanda].

## A text with an older core and later layers

Robert Goldman, introducing the Princeton translation of the epic, and John Brockington, in his survey of the epics, both conclude that the work grew in stages.
Books 2 to 6 are the older core and, in them, Rama is chiefly a human hero and an ideal king.
Books 1 and 7 are usually judged later additions, and they stress that Rama is an incarnation of the god Vishnu [@src.goldman-1984-ramayana-balakanda; @src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
The usual estimate for the earliest core is around the 5th century BCE, and for a text close to our present one the early centuries CE [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
That is a reasoned estimate with real disagreement, so it is graded C.
There is also a Critical Edition, prepared at the Oriental Institute in Baroda, which reconstructs the oldest accessible text from manuscripts of several regional versions [@src.goldman-1984-ramayana-balakanda].

## Dating the story versus dating the text

As with the Mahabharata, two questions need to be kept apart.

### Scholarship

Scholars date the *poem*, not Rama.
The epic describes the world of early north India, with kingdoms such as Kosala centred on Ayodhya, rather than a world datable to any particular centuries, and no inscription or contemporary record mentions Rama or Ravana [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
Historians therefore do not treat the story as evidence for an event with a date; whether a distant memory lies behind it is an open question that the evidence cannot settle.

### Tradition

In the traditional scheme Rama belongs to the Treta Yuga, the second of four ages, which precedes the Dvapara Yuga that in turn ends at the Mahabharata war.
Manu's law book gives the yuga lengths in divine years, and on the standard reckoning the Dvapara Yuga alone has 864,000 human years [@src.olivelle-2005-manus-code-of-law].
On that scheme Rama is set very roughly 869,000 years ago.
This belongs to a cosmological framework and is graded D; it is not a date derived from the epic's text.

### Astronomical reconstructions

Some modern authors have tried to match the planetary positions the epic mentions at Rama's birth and exile against computer sky models and have proposed dates in the sixth millennium BCE and earlier.
These are single-author reconstructions that rely on treating the poem's astronomical details as exact records.
I could not locate a peer-reviewed publication that I could verify for any such date, so no figure is graded or cited here, and none can be treated as an established result.

## Many Ramayanas

The story spread far beyond Valmiki.
A. K. Ramanujan's essay "Three Hundred Ramayanas" shows that a Jain Ramayana, a Tamil Ramayana by Kamban, a Thai Ramakien and many others retell the tale with different emphases, and that in some of them the characters' roles and moral stance change substantially [@src.ramanujan-1991-three-hundred-ramayanas].
There is no single "original" in the story's living history, only Valmiki's Sanskrit poem as an early and influential version among many.

Sheldon Pollock has argued that Rama's role as a divine ideal of kingship became politically important mainly in the second millennium CE, when rulers and temple traditions made Ayodhya and Rama central to public life, and that this later history should not be read back into the epic [@src.pollock-1993-ramayana-political-imagination].

## How we know

For the text we have manuscripts, the Critical Edition and comparison with regional recensions [@src.goldman-1984-ramayana-balakanda].
For the date of composition we have indirect arguments from language, content and the relation to other literature [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
For the events we have only tradition, and honesty requires saying so.
