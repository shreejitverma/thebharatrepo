"Modern Indian literature" is not one literature.
It is the writing of many languages, among them Bengali, Hindi, Urdu, Tamil, Malayalam, Marathi, Telugu, Kannada and English, each with its own history, and only some of it has been translated.
What they share is a set of conditions that arrived roughly together in the 19th and early 20th centuries: printing and cheap periodicals, colonial education, new kinds of writing such as the novel and the modern short story, and a nationalist movement in which writers took part.

## Older roots

The new writing grew out of older ground.
Sheldon Pollock's study of the Sanskrit and vernacular worlds argues that, in the second millennium CE, regional languages gradually took over many literary functions that Sanskrit had once held, a change he calls a vernacular revolution [@src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods].
So by the 19th century, Bengali, Tamil, Marathi, Hindi and many others already had long poetic traditions with their own audiences, and modern writers worked with, as well as against, those traditions.

## Rabindranath Tagore

Rabindranath Tagore was born in Calcutta in 1861 and wrote in Bengali and English.
He published poetry from the 1880s and in 1901 founded an experimental school at Shantiniketan [@src.nobel-1913-tagore-facts].
In 1913 he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature.
The Nobel committee's citation named his "profoundly sensitive, fresh and beautiful verse, by which, with consummate skill, he has made his poetic thought, expressed in his own English words, a part of the literature of the West" [@src.nobel-1913-tagore-facts].
The citation shows that the prize recognised his work as it reached readers in English, and not only his Bengali writing [@src.nobel-1913-tagore-facts].

## Premchand and the Hindi and Urdu public

The Hindi writer Premchand (Dhanpat Rai, who began writing in Urdu) is widely seen as the central figure in the move of Hindi and Urdu fiction toward social realism, with the village and the poor as its subject.
Francesca Orsini's study of the Hindi public sphere between 1920 and 1940 shows the world in which he worked: journals, publishers, literary societies and debates over language and nationalism, in which questions of Hindi, Urdu and Hindustani were fought in public [@src.orsini-2002-hindi-public-sphere].
His novel *Godan* ("The Gift of a Cow"), about a peasant family in debt, is the work most often cited as the summit of that realism, and the popular title "Emperor of Novels" (*Upanyas Samrat*) is an honorific, not a formal award [@src.orsini-2002-hindi-public-sphere].

## Other voices, and what this article leaves out

The same age produced poets and novelists in nearly every Indian language, among them the Tamil poet Subramania Bharati, whose songs were part of the independence movement, and R. K. Narayan, who wrote in English about the fictional town of Malgudi.
Indian writers in English, and writers of the diaspora, form a further strand, and Salman Rushdie's *Midnight's Children*, a Booker Prize winner in 1981, is one of its best-known works.

## How we know

For living and recent authors the evidence is simple: first editions, periodicals, prize records and the authors' own words.
The harder problem for any survey is range, because much of this literature exists in languages that few readers can read in the original, and the standard of "modern Indian literature" in English translation reflects what has been translated as much as what has been written.
