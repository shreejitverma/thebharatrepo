Sangam literature is the earliest substantial body of Tamil writing that survives.
It is a corpus of short poems and long poems composed by many poets, mostly about love and about war and kingship, and it describes a Tamil-speaking world of chieftains, bards and traders in the last centuries BCE and early centuries CE.
It gives a rare, poet's-eye view of life in the far south of the subcontinent.
[@src.zvelebil-1992-companion-studies; @src.hart-1979-poets-tamil-anthologies]

## What the corpus contains

The corpus is made of two collections.
The *Ettuttokai* ("Eight Anthologies") holds the *Ainkurunuru*, *Kuruntokai*, *Narrinai*, *Akananuru*, *Purananuru*, *Patirruppattu*, *Paripatal* and *Kalittokai*.
The *Pattuppattu* ("Ten Idylls") is a set of ten longer poems, among them the *Tirumurukarruppatai*, *Maduraikkanci* and *Pattinappalai*.
Together they contain roughly two thousand five hundred poems, by several hundred named poets and many anonymous ones.
[@src.zvelebil-1992-companion-studies]

The grammar *Tolkappiyam* is often grouped with them, but it is a different kind of text.
It is the earliest surviving Tamil grammar, and it includes a section on poetics that describes the themes found in the poems.
It is a layered work, and its date is as debated as that of the anthologies, with some strata thought to be older than others.
[@src.zvelebil-1992-companion-studies]

## Akam and puram: the two worlds of the poems

Tamil poetics divides all poetry into *akam* ("inner") and *puram* ("outer").
*Akam* poems are about love, in which no real names are given: the lovers are simply "the man" and "the woman", so that the poem speaks to any reader.
*Puram* poems are about the public world: kings, battles, gifts to bards, and the ethics of rule.
[@src.ramanujan-1985-poems-love-war; @src.hart-1979-poets-tamil-anthologies]

Akam poems are organised by *tinai*, a set of five landscapes, each tied to a mood of love: hills (*kurinji*, union), forest (*mullai*, patient waiting), farmland (*marutam*, quarrels), seashore (*neytal*, anxious longing) and dry land (*palai*, separation).
The landscape supplies the imagery of the poem, so a reader who knows the code hears feelings in the flowers, birds and weather.
[@src.ramanujan-1985-poems-love-war]

## Why "Sangam"?

The name comes from later tradition, not from the poems.
Medieval commentators, notably in the commentary on the *Iraiyanar Akapporul*, told of three great assemblies (*cankam*, from Sanskrit *sangha*) of poets patronised by the Pandya kings at Madurai, said to have lasted for many thousands of years.
Modern scholars treat this account as legend: the surviving poems themselves do not describe such assemblies.
The label "Sangam" has stuck as a convenient name, and some scholars prefer to speak of "classical Tamil" or "early Tamil" poetry.
[@src.zvelebil-1992-companion-studies; @src.wilden-2014-manuscript-print-memory]

## How we know when they were written

The dating of Sangam literature rests on four kinds of evidence, which agree only in part.

**Epigraphy.**
Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions on cave beds in Tamil Nadu, from perhaps the last centuries BCE onwards, include names of chiefs and kings that are also found in the poems, such as the Chera rulers named in the Pugalur inscriptions.
This shows that the political world of the poems existed in the early centuries of Tamil writing.
[@src.mahadevan-2003-early-tamil-epigraphy]

**Foreign synchronisms.**
The poems speak of Yavanas, foreigners, who come with gold and wine and go home with pepper, in ships to ports such as Muciri (Muziris).
The Greek *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a first-century CE merchant's handbook, independently names Muziris as a great pepper port.
This links the poems to Indian Ocean trade in the early centuries CE.
[@src.casson-1989-periplus-maris-erythraei; @src.zvelebil-1992-companion-studies]

**Language and metre.**
Zvelebil studied the language, metres and conventions of the poems and placed the core of the corpus between about 100 BCE and 250 CE.
Within this range the poems are not all of one age.
Some, like the *Paripatal* and *Kalittokai*, are usually judged later than the rest of the anthologies.
[@src.zvelebil-1992-companion-studies]

**Transmission.**
The poems were preserved on palm leaf and in memory, and were rediscovered and printed only in the late nineteenth century and after, notably through the work of U. V. Swaminatha Aiyar.
No manuscript is anywhere near as old as the poems.
[@src.wilden-2014-manuscript-print-memory]

## Where scholars disagree

The usual view, set out by Zvelebil and widely held, places the core poems in the last centuries BCE and the early centuries CE.
Some scholars, however, ask for far more caution.
Herman Tieken has argued that the poems show strong features of Sanskrit court poetry, and that they were composed much later than the usual dating, in a period closer to the Pallava age, and edited into an "ancient" corpus.
Eva Wilden, whose work on the manuscripts and transmission is the most detailed available, treats the question of dates as still open in many details.
Most specialists, while allowing that individual poems may be later, still hold to the earlier range on the grounds of the inscriptions and trade evidence above.
[@src.tieken-2001-kavya-south-india; @src.wilden-2014-manuscript-print-memory; @src.zvelebil-1992-companion-studies]

The older textbook version, "the Sangam Age, 300 BCE to 300 CE", is therefore a wider bracket than the evidence directly supports.
