Imagine a priest in the first millennium BCE who has memorised thousands of lines of ritual instruction.
One line says that "one who desires heaven should sacrifice".
Who is "one"?
What exactly is he to do, with what, and in what order?
And if he does it faithfully, how can an act finished this afternoon produce a result in another world, or in another life?

Mimamsa (Sanskrit *mīmāṃsā*, from a root meaning "to ponder, to investigate") is the school of Indian thought that took these questions seriously, and answered them with unusual rigour.
It is also called Purva Mimamsa, "the earlier inquiry", because it deals with the earlier, ritual part of the Vedic corpus, while Vedanta, the "later inquiry", deals with the Upanishads [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism; @src.clooney-1990-thinking-ritually].
Its method of reading texts, its theory of how words mean, and its claim that moral action leaves an unseen residue all became shared furniture for Indian philosophy, law and theology.

## What Mimamsa is for

The founding text is the Mimamsa Sutra, attributed to Jaimini and arranged in twelve chapters.
Its first two aphorisms announce the whole programme: "Now, therefore, the inquiry into dharma" (MS 1.1.1), and "Dharma is the good that is marked out by the Vedic injunction (*codanā*)" (MS 1.1.2) [@src.clooney-1990-thinking-ritually].
Here *dharma* does not mean a vague "righteousness".
It means the specific, prescribed actions, above all the Vedic sacrifices, whose result is a lasting good.

The key claim is epistemological.
Dharma, in this view, cannot be seen with the eyes or deduced by reasoning, because it concerns what ought to be done and what a ritual will bring about in the future.
The only way to know it is through the Vedic injunction, so the whole enterprise rests on getting the reading of those injunctions right [@src.clooney-1990-thinking-ritually; @src.freschi-2012-duty-language-exegesis].

## A reader's toolkit

Mimamsa sorted Vedic sentences by what they do.
The main classes are injunctions (*vidhi*), which command; mantras, which are recited during the rite; names of rites; prohibitions; and explanatory or praising passages (*arthavāda*), such as myths and eulogies, which are not commands themselves but are read as supporting them [@src.clooney-1990-thinking-ritually].
This lets an interpreter say that a story about the gods in a Brahmana is not a rival teaching, but a motivational companion to a nearby command.

A famous stock example is the sentence "one who desires heaven should sacrifice" (*svargakāmo yajeta*).
Mimamsa analysed what such an optative verb does.
It does not merely describe a fact; it urges a person to bring something about.
Later authors, notably Kumarila Bhatta, spoke of this urging as *bhāvanā*, "bringing into being", and asked what is to be brought about, by what means, and by what procedure [@src.taber-2005-hindu-critique-buddhist-epistemology; @src.freschi-2012-duty-language-exegesis].

Because the rituals are complex, the school also wrote rules for conflicts.
When two signals in a text point to different ritual arrangements, a ranking of the strength of textual evidence decides which prevails; direct statement outranks inference from a mere word-sign, and context outranks position, and so on.
This ordered contest of pieces of evidence is the real core of Mimamsa method [@src.clooney-1990-thinking-ritually].
Each puzzle was worked through in a standard five-part form: the topic, the doubt, the opponent's view, the settled conclusion, and the connection to neighbouring topics [@src.clooney-1990-thinking-ritually].

A note on a popular summary: later manuals, especially in Vedanta, list "six marks" for finding a text's central purport (agreement of beginning and end, repetition, novelty, result, praise, and reasoning).
That list is real and useful, but it belongs to the later tradition of discerning a passage's purport; it is not the ranking of evidence found in the Mimamsa Sutra itself, and the two should not be confused.

## Apurva: the unseen link

If a sacrifice ends today and heaven comes later, something must carry the result across the gap.
Mimamsa's answer is *apūrva*, literally "the not-before-seen": a new potency produced by the act, which persists until it yields its fruit.
It is not an object anyone can perceive.
It is postulated because the alternative, a result with no cause, is unacceptable [@src.clooney-1990-thinking-ritually; @src.freschi-2012-duty-language-exegesis].

This is a nice piece of reasoning, and easy to misread.
Apurva is not a moral "battery stored in the soul" in a casual sense, and it is not the same as the later, broader doctrine of karma as it appears in other schools.
In Mimamsa it is tied to specific ritual acts and to the logic of getting an injunction to work.
Different Mimamsa authors disagreed about what exactly it is, a point we return to below.

## Language and the authority of the Veda

Mimamsa developed a full philosophy of language.
Its central claims are three.

- **The link between a word and its meaning is natural, not an agreed convention.** The sutra says the connection of a word with its sense is "originary" (MS 1.1.5) [@src.clooney-1990-thinking-ritually].
- **Sounds are eternal.** The letters of speech are held to be permanent, produced (manifested) by the speaker but not created by them. Grammarians who argued for a single indivisible *sphoṭa* disagreed.
- **The Veda is authorless (*apauruṣeya*).** Since no person composed it, no human fault (error, deceit, ignorance) can enter it.

The last point is tied to a theory of knowledge.
Mimamsa holds that cognitions are valid by themselves (*svataḥ-prāmāṇya*): a cognition is accepted as true when it arises, and is rejected only if a defect in its cause or a later contradicting cognition appears.
Nyaya, the school of logic, took the opposite view, that validity must be established by something further [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india; @src.taber-2005-hindu-critique-buddhist-epistemology].
An authorless text has no author to be defective, and nothing contradicts it, so it stands.
Critics, of course, replied that authorlessness cannot simply be assumed; the debate is a standing one in Indian philosophy [@src.ganeri-2001-philosophy-classical-india].

The idea of "never composed" should be kept distinct from the historian's picture of the Vedic corpus as texts composed and transmitted across centuries [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
Mimamsa is making a claim about the *source of authority* of the text, not offering an account of its prehistory, and the two questions are answered in different ways by different kinds of evidence.

## Two sub-schools, two answers

By roughly the seventh century CE the school had split into two main lines, named after Kumarila Bhatta and Prabhakara.
They disagreed on matters that look technical but run deep [@src.freschi-2012-duty-language-exegesis; @src.taber-2005-hindu-critique-buddhist-epistemology].

| Question | Bhatta line (Kumarila) | Prabhakara line |
|---|---|---|
| How do words combine into a sentence meaning? | Words first give their own meanings, which are then connected (*abhihitānvaya*) | Words give meanings already linked in a context of action (*anvitābhidhāna*) |
| What is the core of an injunction? | The "bringing about" (*bhāvanā*) | The binding command or duty (*niyoga*) |
| How many sources of knowledge are there? | Six, including absence (*abhāva*) | Five, without absence as a separate source |

This table simplifies debates that ran to book length, and the details inside each line varied by author [@src.freschi-2012-duty-language-exegesis].
The point for a general reader is that Mimamsa was not a monolith; it was a living argument.

## Is Mimamsa atheistic, and does it seek liberation?

The early sutras say nothing that requires a creator, and the commentators argue that the Veda needs none, since it is not composed.
Some modern writers therefore call Mimamsa atheistic.
Others say this is too strong: the early school is better described as *non-theistic*, because its interest was ritual and its sentences about gods treat them as the recipients named in mantras, not as authors or rulers of the cosmos [@src.clooney-1990-thinking-ritually].
Later authors did take up the question of release (*mokṣa*), which the early school largely ignores [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
Where the line falls between "silence" and "denial" in the sutras themselves is a matter of interpretation, and we should be careful not to read later positions back into them.

## Mimamsa and Vedanta

Vedanta took its opening from Mimamsa's: the Brahma Sutra begins "Now, therefore, the inquiry into Brahman", deliberately echoing Jaimini's "inquiry into dharma".
The two inquiries are in tension.
Mimamsa says that every Vedic sentence is ultimately about action.
Vedanta, and especially Advaita, argues that Upanishadic sentences about the Self are about something already real, and so do not command anything [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism; @src.ganeri-2001-philosophy-classical-india].
The question of whether knowing and doing are rivals, and which one frees a person, ran through the following centuries.

## What is secure, and what is not

The structure of Mimamsa thought, its method and its place in the later philosophical debates, is well documented in its commentaries.
What is far less secure is the date and authorship of the sutra: "Jaimini" names the tradition's author, and the text itself is best treated as the work of a school across time.
This article therefore gives a wide range rather than a single date, and treats tradition's own accounts of Jaimini as tradition.

A last caution.
Older popular summaries sometimes describe apurva as a general law of moral accounting, or Mimamsa as a doctrine of "cosmic alignment".
Neither phrase belongs to the school's own vocabulary; Mimamsa is much more precise, and much more interesting, than that.
