Imagine three readers meeting the Upanishadic sentence *tat tvam asi*, "you are that." One hears a teaching that the deepest self and Brahman are ultimately one. Another hears that the self belongs to God as a body's life belongs to its inner self. A third insists that the soul depends on God precisely because it is not God. These are simplified entry points to Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita, three influential Vedanta traditions. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.8.7; @src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta; @src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta]

All three traditions treat the Upanishads, the *Bhagavad Gītā* and the *Brahma Sūtras* as authoritative. Their differences arise from how they interpret the texts' language of oneness alongside passages that describe a creator, ruler, worshipper and worshipped. The core question is what kind of relation holds among Brahman, the individual self (*jīva*) and the world (*jagat*). [@src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta; @src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta]

| Question | Advaita (Shankara) | Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja) | Dvaita (Madhva) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is the world real? | Real in everyday life, dependent and not ultimately independent | Real, as God's body | Real and distinct from God |
| Is the self God? | Ultimately identical with Brahman | A real self inseparable from Brahman as body from its inner self | Always distinct from God and dependent on him |
| Liberation | Knowledge removes ignorance of non-duality | Devotional knowledge and God's grace; the self remains distinct | Devotion and God's grace; the self remains distinct |

The table compresses nuanced systems. It is a guide to the central contrast, not a substitute for each school's own arguments. [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta; @src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta]

## Ramanuja: unity with real distinctions

Ramanuja (Rāmānuja) was a theologian of the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition in South India. His *Śrībhāṣya* is a major commentary on the *Brahma Sūtras*. His dates remain uncertain: the community's traditional chronology gives 1017 to 1137 CE, an exceptionally long lifespan, while John Carman discusses a historical reconstruction of 1077 to 1157 CE. Both the traditional account and the modern reconstruction deserve to be identified as positions rather than reported as exact, settled dates. [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.lipner-1986-face-of-truth; @src.thibaut-1904-vedanta-sutras-ramanuja]

The term *viśiṣṭādvaita* means "qualified non-duality." Reality is one, but it is internally differentiated: selves and matter are real, while Brahman is their inner self and the support on which they depend. Ramanuja identifies this Brahman with Nārāyaṇa, a personal God endowed with auspicious qualities. The one reality is therefore not featureless; its unity includes genuine plurality. [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.lipner-1986-face-of-truth]

Ramanuja's central analogy is body and indwelling self. The world and conscious selves are the body of Brahman; Brahman is their inner controller. A body is distinct from the self that animates it, yet it exists in intimate dependence on that self. The analogy lets Ramanuja affirm both unity and difference without treating the world as a mistake. Anne Hunt Overzee's study traces how the body image works in Ramanuja's theology. [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.overzee-1992-body-divine]

This also shapes Ramanuja's reading of *tat tvam asi*. The sentence does not mean that the individual self is simply interchangeable with Brahman. It identifies the self with Brahman in a qualified sense: the self exists as God's body and depends on God as its inner self. Ramanuja therefore reads statements of identity alongside texts that distinguish the Lord from finite selves. [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.thibaut-1904-vedanta-sutras-ramanuja, commentary on 1.1.1; @src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.8.7]

Ramanuja also objects to Advaita's account of ignorance and a world whose reality is sublated by knowledge. In the *Śrībhāṣya*, he challenges where such ignorance could reside and how it could affect an unchanging Brahman or a self whose nature is knowledge. He reads scriptural descriptions of Brahman as without defects, not as lacking all qualities. The disagreement is not simply about whether devotion matters; it concerns the meaning of scripture, the reality of the world and what liberation changes. [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.lipner-1986-face-of-truth; @src.thibaut-1904-vedanta-sutras-ramanuja]

For Ramanuja, liberation is not the disappearance of the individual into an undifferentiated absolute. The liberated self knows and enjoys God directly while remaining dependent and distinct. Devotion (*bhakti*) is a sustained form of knowledge and loving contemplation; surrender (*prapatti*) became especially important in later Śrīvaiṣṇava teaching. The precise relation between disciplined practice and divine grace has itself been debated within that community. [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.lipner-1986-face-of-truth]

## Madhva: difference that never disappears

Madhva (Madhvācārya, also called Ānanda Tīrtha) taught in the thirteenth century in the region of present-day Karnataka. His dates are commonly given as 1238 to 1317 CE, although early biographical traditions vary. Deepak Sarma's introduction presents his philosophy and its debates with Advaita through Madhva sources and later interpretations. [@src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta]

Madhva's *dvaita* means "dualism," but his view is more than the claim that there are two things. He argues that difference is real and enduring. God, identified with Viṣṇu, is independent (*svatantra*); every soul and material thing is dependent (*paratantra*). Dependence does not erase a thing's reality or turn it into God. [@src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta]

The doctrine of five differences (*pañca-bheda*) names the relations that Advaita ultimately treats as provisional: between God and souls, God and matter, one soul and another, souls and matter, and one material thing and another. These distinctions are not errors to be overcome. They are part of the structure of reality and remain relevant in liberation. [@src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta]

Madhva reads the Upanishadic identity statements in a way that preserves dependence rather than numerical identity. His interpretation of *tat tvam asi* is especially distinctive: he construes the phrase so it denies that the self simply is Brahman, rather than making it an equation. Other Vedanta commentators reject this reading, and the long-running debate shows that the same brief Sanskrit sentence can bear sharply different interpretations within traditions that all claim the Upanishads as authority. [@src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta; @src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.8.7]

In Madhva's account, liberation is a state of direct knowledge and bliss in the presence of God, not identity with God. Souls differ in their capacities, and their relation to God remains one of dependence. Devotion and divine grace are central, but this should not be simplified into a contrast where Ramanuja has grace and Madhva does not: both traditions value devotion and grace while grounding them in different metaphysics. [@src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta; @src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja]

## Three answers to a shared problem

Advaita makes non-duality the final meaning of the Upanishads: knowledge removes the apparent separation of self and Brahman. Ramanuja argues that unity includes real selves and matter as God's body. Madhva holds that God, souls and the world remain truly distinct, even as all things depend on God. These are not three interchangeable ways of saying that everything is connected; they make incompatible claims about what exists and what liberation means. [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta; @src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta]

The comparison is most useful when it preserves each system's own logic. Ramanuja's body analogy explains how he can affirm one Brahman and real difference; Madhva's five differences explain why dependence does not imply identity; Shankara's account of superimposition explains why apparent difference can be experienced without being ultimate. Their shared scriptures did not produce one consensus, but a centuries-long philosophical argument about reality, knowledge, devotion and freedom. [@src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta; @src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.sarma-2003-introduction-madhva-vedanta]
