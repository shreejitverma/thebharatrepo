Most philosophical texts begin with an argument.
The Katha Upanishad begins with an angry child.
A father gives away cattle at a sacrifice, and the cattle are old, dry and past giving milk.
His young son Naciketas notices, and the text says that "faith" (*śraddhā*) entered him [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.1.1-2].
He asks his father, again and again, "To whom will you give me?"
Irritated, the father snaps: "To Death I give you" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.1.4].

## The setting and the text

The Katha belongs to the Kathaka school of the Black (Krishna) Yajurveda, and it is among the verse Upanishads.
It has two chapters, each with three sections (*vallī*).
Scholars including Patrick Olivelle place it after the earliest prose Upanishads, the Brihadaranyaka and Chandogya, and before or around the Mauryan period, though the dating rests on style and doctrine rather than any external record, so the range is wide [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
Some readers see two layers: a narrative inherited from Vedic ritual literature and a later, more abstract teaching built on it.
The ritual story of Naciketas and his fire is also told in a Brahmana, which supports that reading, but the layering remains a scholarly hypothesis, not a settled result [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].

## Three nights and three boons

Naciketas reaches the house of Yama, lord of the dead, who is away.
The boy waits three nights without food, and Yama, returning, grants three boons to make amends for the breach of hospitality [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.1.7-9].

| Boon | Request | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| First | That his father be calm and welcome him home | The story is rooted in family before metaphysics [KU 1.1.10] |
| Second | The fire that leads to heaven | A ritual question, answered with the "Nāciketa" fire, named after the boy [KU 1.1.13-19] |
| Third | What happens to a man who has died: "some say he exists, some say he does not" | The real subject of the text [KU 1.1.20] |

[@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads]

Yama resists the third request.
He says even the gods once doubted this and that the matter is subtle.
He offers sons and grandsons, cattle, gold, elephants, long life, chariots and beautiful women instead [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.1.21-25].
Naciketas answers that such things last only until tomorrow and wear out the senses, and that no one can be made happy by wealth when Death is in view [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.1.26-27].
The boy's refusal is itself the first lesson: the teaching can only be given to someone who wants it more than anything Yama can offer.

## The good and the pleasant

Pleased, Yama states the choice that underlies the whole text.
The good (*śreyas*) and the pleasant (*preyas*) both draw a person.
The wise examine them and choose the good, while the dull choose the pleasant for the sake of comfort and gain [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.2.1-2].
Yama says Naciketas has chosen the good, and adds that ignorance and knowledge are far apart and lead to different ends [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.2.4-5].

This is not a call to despise life.
It is a claim about ordering: what you can lose should not be the thing you stake yourself on.

## What Yama teaches about the self

Yama's answer to the third boon is that the knowing self is not born and does not die.
It "is not killed when the body is killed" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.2.18-19].
It is "smaller than the small, greater than the great", hidden in the heart of a creature [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.2.20].
One verse says the self cannot be gained by teaching, by intellect or by much learning, but only by the one whom it chooses [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.2.23].
Readers disagree about whether "chooses" points to grace, to inner readiness, or simply to the limits of argument.
Later devotional traditions lean on the first reading, and many modern interpreters on the last two; the text itself leaves the question open.

## The chariot

The most quoted passage comes in the third section of the first chapter.
Yama gives a picture of a person as a vehicle on a road [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.3.3-9].

| Image | Meaning in the text |
|---|---|
| Rider in the chariot | The self (*ātman*) |
| The chariot | The body |
| Charioteer | The intellect (*buddhi*) |
| Reins | The mind (*manas*) |
| Horses | The senses |
| The paths the horses run on | Sense objects |

[@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.3.3-4]

The logic is practical.
If the charioteer lacks understanding and the reins are loose, the senses run like unruly horses.
If the charioteer understands and the reins are firm, they behave like good horses.
The person with the first kind of equipment is caught in rebirth, and the one with the second reaches "the highest place of Viṣṇu" and does not return [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.3.5-9].
The phrase "place of Viṣṇu" is a goal word here, not evidence of a developed Vaishnava theology, which is a later development; the text does not teach one.
The "self" in the picture sits quietly in the chariot and is called the "enjoyer" only when joined to body, senses and mind [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.3.4].

A reader of fifteen can try it: the picture says that feeling a pull is not the same as obeying it, and that something in you can notice the pull.

## The ladder of the subtle

Two verses then climb a ladder.
Beyond the senses are their objects, beyond the objects the mind, beyond the mind the intellect, beyond the intellect the "great self" (*mahān ātmā*), beyond that the "unmanifest" (*avyakta*), and beyond the unmanifest the Person (*puruṣa*).
"Beyond the Person there is nothing; that is the end, the highest course" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.3.10-11].
The terms *mahat*, *avyakta* and *puruṣa* are also the vocabulary of the later Samkhya school.
Scholars disagree about the direction of influence: some read the Katha as a proto-Samkhya text, others as a source Samkhya drew on, and others as using words common to a wider milieu [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
In the second chapter, the steady restraint of the senses is called yoga [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 2.3.10-11], which makes the Katha an early witness to yoga as a practice of discipline.

## Awake, arise

The famous exhortation comes at the end of the first chapter: "Arise, awake, and understand" what is excellent; the path is "like the sharp edge of a razor, hard to cross, so the wise say" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 1.3.14].
The word *varān* can mean "boons" or "the best", so translators differ on whether the verse points to teachers or to what one has just been given.

The second chapter also gives an upside-down fig tree, with roots above and branches below, as an image of the world [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 2.3.1].
The Bhagavad Gita takes up the same image in its fifteenth chapter.
The text ends with Naciketas learning the teaching and the yoga procedure and becoming free of impurity and death [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, KU 2.3.18].

## Why it endures

The Katha's strength is that it is a drama.
Death is not an abstract problem but a host who owes a guest a gift, and a teacher who tests the student by offering everything except the answer.
Its picture of the divided person, with appetite, reins, judgement and a witness, is easy to carry and hard to improve on.

The text answers the question "what lies beyond death?" with an inner fact, not a place.
Whether one finds that answer persuasive is a matter for the reader, and the later schools (Advaita, Vishishtadvaita, Dvaita) read the same verses differently, as the archive's articles on those traditions show.
