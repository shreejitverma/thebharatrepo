The oldest Upanishads do not read like treatises.
They read like transcripts: a father quizzes his son, a husband tells his wife why he is leaving, a woman at a royal tournament asks questions until a famous teacher tells her to stop.
This article follows four of those exchanges and says what the text actually says, since popular summaries often smooth the details.

## Two long texts

The Chandogya belongs to the Samaveda tradition, and the Brihadaranyaka to the White (Shukla) Yajurveda.
They are the two longest of the principal Upanishads and are placed, with the Brahmanas and Aranyakas behind them, among the earliest Upanishadic prose [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
Neither can be dated exactly.
The usual scholarly range is roughly the 7th to 5th centuries BCE, with the caveat that each is a patchwork and some sections are later [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].

## A proud student: Uddalaka and Shvetaketu

Shvetaketu goes to a teacher at twelve and returns at twenty-four, "conceited, thinking himself learned, arrogant" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.1.1-2].
His father Uddalaka Aruni asks whether he ever asked for the teaching by which the unheard is heard, the unthought is thought and the unknown is known.
Shvetaketu admits he has not, and wonders whether his teachers knew it [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.1.3-7].

Uddalaka answers with examples of how many things can be one thing.
By knowing one lump of clay, one knows everything made of clay, since "the modification is a name that rests on speech; the clay alone is real" (*vācārambhaṇaṁ vikāro nāmadheyam, mṛttikety eva satyam*) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.1.4].
The same is said of a lump of gold and its ornaments, and of iron and the tools made from it [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.1.5-6].
Note that these are the first three examples, and they are about material and form.
The salt in water comes later, in a different lesson.

### Being, then the refrain

Uddalaka then says that in the beginning there was Being alone, one without a second.
Some say there was non-being, he reports, but how could being be born from non-being? [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.2.1-2].
From Being come fire, water and food, which the text uses to explain how the many arise [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.2-6.7].
Then, beginning at 6.8.7, comes a sentence that repeats nine times with a different illustration each time:

> *sa ya eṣo 'ṇimaitadātmyam idaṃ sarvaṃ tat satyaṃ sa ātmā tat tvam asi śvetaketo*

"That which is the finest essence, this whole world has it as its self. That is the real. That is the self. You are that, Shvetaketu" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.8.7].

The illustrations are ordinary: bees collecting nectar from many trees, rivers flowing to the sea and losing their names, a tree that lives when it still has life in a branch [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.9-11].
The banyan is next.
Break open a fig, and there are seeds; break a seed, and Shvetaketu sees nothing.
"The finest essence which you do not see, from that essence this great banyan stands. Believe me, my dear" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.12.1-2].
Then salt is dissolved in water overnight.
Shvetaketu cannot see it, but every sip tastes of it [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.13].
The point is that something real can be everywhere in a thing and still be invisible to the eye.

### What "tat tvam asi" means

The sentence is short and easy to quote, and it has been argued over for more than a thousand years.
Advaita Vedanta reads it as an identity statement: the individual self and the ground of the world are not two.
Other Vedanta schools read it differently, for example as a statement of dependence or of inseparability without identity.
The text supports the reading that Shvetaketu's own essence is the same as the essence of everything, but the grammar and the surrounding context do not settle the philosophical question that later schools raise.
A historian's safe summary is that Uddalaka teaches a single underlying reality and says the student shares in it [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.8-6.16].

### A teacher who is also taught

The same Shvetaketu appears elsewhere being humbled.
In Chandogya 5.3 and Brihadaranyaka 6.2 a king, Pravahana Jaivali, asks him questions about where beings go after death.
He cannot answer, and his father Uddalaka also has to be taught by the king [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 5.3.1-7 and BU 6.2.1-8].
Scholars have long debated whether this pattern, with kings instructing priests, reflects a kshatriya origin for some Upanishadic ideas.
Brian Black reads such passages mainly as literary devices that test and reshape the authority of the Brahmin teacher, and he urges caution about drawing social history from them [@src.black-2007-character-of-the-self].

## A wife's question: Yajnavalkya and Maitreyi

The Brihadaranyaka tells this story twice, in nearly the same words [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 2.4 and 4.5].
Yajnavalkya is about to leave household life and offers to divide his property between his two wives, Maitreyi and Katyayani.
Maitreyi asks whether she would be immortal if the whole earth full of wealth were hers.
"No," he says; "your life would be like that of rich people, but there is no hope of immortality through wealth."
She answers: "What would I do with that by which I will not be immortal? Tell me what you know" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 2.4.1-3].

His reply is not a doctrine but a reorientation.
A husband is dear not for the husband's sake but for the sake of the self, and the same is said of wife, sons, wealth, the gods and everything else.
So "the self is to be seen, heard, thought about and reflected upon" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 2.4.5].
Then he gives the image of salt thrown into the sea, which cannot be picked out again, though every drop tastes of it.
He adds that "there is no consciousness after death," and Maitreyi is bewildered.
He tells her that where there seems to be duality one sees another, but where everything has become one's own self, "by what and whom would one see?" and "by what would one know the knower?" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 2.4.12-14].
The text has Maitreyi confused for a moment, and it does not hurry to comfort her.

## A debater's challenge: Yajnavalkya and Gargi

King Janaka of Videha holds a great sacrifice and offers a thousand cows, with gold on their horns, to the wisest Brahmin.
Yajnavalkya tells his student to drive them away, and the other scholars rise to challenge him [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 3.1.1-2].
Gargi Vacaknavi is among them.

She asks: since this whole world is woven back and forth on water, on what is water woven?
On wind, he says.
On what is wind woven?
On the atmosphere, then the worlds of the Gandharvas, the sun, the moon, the stars, the gods, Indra, Prajapati, and finally the worlds of Brahma.
On what are the worlds of Brahma woven?
Here Yajnavalkya says, "Gargi, do not ask too much, or your head will fall off. You are asking about a divinity that cannot be asked about beyond this" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 3.6.1].
Gargi falls silent.

Note the chain: it ends at "the worlds of Brahma" and not at Brahman the ultimate reality, as popular retellings sometimes say.
The head-splitting warning is a standard rhetorical weapon in these debates; in the next chapter it is carried out on another challenger, Vidagdha Shakalya [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 3.9.26-27].

Gargi rises again, saying she will ask two questions, like a warrior with two arrows.
The ground of the sky, Yajnavalkya answers, is the Imperishable (*akṣara*).
It is not coarse or fine, not short or long, not red, not sticky, without shadow or darkness, without taste or smell [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 3.8.8].
By its command the sun and moon stand apart, and sky and earth hold their places [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 3.8.9].
It is "unseen but seeing, unheard but hearing, unthought but thinking, unknown but knowing" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 3.8.11].
Gargi then tells the assembly to count themselves lucky if they escape this man with a bow, for none of them will defeat him in a debate about Brahman [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 3.8.12].
The scene shows a woman of the period as a recognised and formidable participant in philosophical contest, and the text does not treat her as exceptional.

## Not this, not this

The method behind many of Yajnavalkya's answers is *neti neti*, "not this, not this".
It occurs at BU 2.3.6, where it is given as the teaching about Brahman, and again at 3.9.26, 4.2.4, 4.4.22 and 4.5.15 [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 2.3.6].
It does not say that the self is nothing.
It says that whatever can be pointed to, such as body, breath or thought, is not the final subject, since the subject is what does the pointing.
In 2.3.6 the formula is followed by a positive name for Brahman, "the truth of the truth" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 2.3.6].

## The "great sayings"

A common summary lists four "great sayings" (*mahāvākya*), one from each Veda: *prajñānaṃ brahma* (Aitareya Upanishad, Rigveda), *tat tvam asi* (Chandogya, Samaveda), *aham brahmāsmi* (Brihadaranyaka, White Yajurveda; BU 1.4.10) and *ayam ātmā brahma* (Mandukya, Atharvaveda; also BU 2.5.19) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
The Upanishads themselves never number or rank four sentences.
They contain many statements of similar force, and the neat set of four is a later way of organising them.

## Reading the dialogues well

Three habits help.
First, check the chain of examples against the verses, since later retellings tend to compress and reorder them.
Second, notice that the teachers are not always the final authority: a father is taught by a king and a husband puzzles his wife.
Third, keep text and tradition apart: Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita each draw on these verses, and the Upanishads were composed before any of them existed.
