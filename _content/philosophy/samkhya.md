Imagine sitting in a theatre.
The stage is lit, the dancer moves, the music changes, and you feel joy, fear and sorrow as the story unfolds.
Now ask: which part of this is *you*?
Samkhya (Sāṃkhya, सांख्य) gives a radical answer.
You are only the person in the seat, the one who watches.
Everything on the stage, including your thoughts, your feelings, your sense of "I", belongs to the dancer.

This article explains that idea, how the system counts the stages of the world, and where scholars disagree about its history.

## What Samkhya is, and what we actually have

Samkhya is one of the classical schools (*darśana*) of Indian philosophy.
The name is traditionally linked to *saṃkhyā*, "number" or "enumeration", because the system is known for counting the basic principles (*tattva*) of reality [@src.larson-1987-encyclopedia-samkhya; @src.burley-2007-classical-samkhya-yoga].

Tradition names the sage Kapila as its founder.
No work that can be securely traced to Kapila survives.
The earliest complete surviving statement of the system is the *Sāṃkhyakārikā* ("Verses on Samkhya") of Īśvarakṛṣṇa, seventy-odd verses in the āryā metre.
Scholars place it in roughly the fourth to fifth century CE, and a Chinese translation made in the sixth century gives a firm latest date [@src.larson-1987-encyclopedia-samkhya; @src.burley-2007-classical-samkhya-yoga].
When this article gives a verse number as "SK", it means this text, which can be read in English in the standard anthology [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy].

One caution about popular summaries.
The *Sāṃkhyasūtra* (or *Sāṃkhyapravacanasūtra*), the aphorism collection attributed to Kapila, is a much later work, usually placed in the later medieval period.
A line quoted from it is therefore evidence of how Samkhya was taught in that period, not of what Kapila said [@src.larson-1987-encyclopedia-samkhya].

Before the *Kārikā*, Samkhya-like ideas are visible in older texts.
The Katha Upanishad describes a ladder running from the senses to their objects, to mind, to intellect, to a "great self", to the "unmanifest" and finally to *puruṣa* (Kaṭha 1.3.10-11; 2.3.7-8) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
Scholars call this proto-Samkhya, and they disagree about where it came from.
One view, argued by Johannes Bronkhorst, is that Samkhya and yoga grew in the culture of the eastern Gangetic plain ("Greater Magadha"), among the same ascetic milieus that produced Buddhism and Jainism, and were later absorbed into Brahmanical thought [@src.bronkhorst-2007-greater-magadha].
Others stress the continuity with Upanishadic speculation [@src.larson-1987-encyclopedia-samkhya].
Neither view can be settled by the surviving evidence, so both are held as reasoned estimates.

## Two realities

Samkhya says that ultimately there are exactly two kinds of thing [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 3, 11].

**Puruṣa** is consciousness.
It is a pure witness: it does not act, does not change and has no parts.
There are many puruṣas, one for each living being.

**Prakṛti** (also called *pradhāna*) is everything else.
It is a single, unconscious, creative nature that is always changing.
Bodies, planets, emotions, memories and even the intellect are all prakṛti.

This is not the same as the mind-versus-body dualism of Descartes.
In Samkhya, the mind is *material*.
It is a very fine, subtle part of prakṛti.
Only the witnessing is consciousness [@src.burley-2007-classical-samkhya-yoga].

Why believe in a separate witness at all?
The *Kārikā* gives several arguments (SK 17-18).
Composite things exist for the sake of something other than themselves, as a bed exists for a sleeper.
Experience needs someone who experiences.
People strive for freedom, which needs someone to be freed.
And the way births and deaths are distributed among beings suggests many separate experiencers rather than one [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 17-18].
Notice that these are arguments from reasoning, not appeals to scripture, which is part of why Samkhya has been called the most rationalist of the classical systems.

## The three guṇas

Prakṛti is not a lump.
It is made of three strands (*guṇa*, literally "strand" or "quality"), which are present together in everything [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 12-13].

| Guṇa | Character | Typical effects |
|---|---|---|
| Sattva | light, clear, revealing | understanding, calm, pleasure |
| Rajas | energetic, moving, stimulating | activity, restlessness, pain |
| Tamas | heavy, obstructing, covering | inertia, dullness, confusion |

The three work against each other, yet together produce one result, as a wick, oil and flame (opposites in character) together make one lamp (SK 13) [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 13].
Before the world is "unfolded", the guṇas are in perfect balance and nothing manifest exists.
When the balance is disturbed, the unfolding begins.
How the disturbance starts is the hardest question for the system: the Kārikā says only that puruṣa and prakṛti come together, "like a lame man and a blind man" (SK 21), the one able to see but not move, the other able to move but not see [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 21].

## The unfolding: counting the principles

The *Kārikā* sorts all of existence into twenty-five principles, and the grouping is deliberately tidy (SK 3, 22) [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 3, 22].

| Stage | Principle | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Root | Mūla-prakṛti (unmanifest nature) | 1 |
| First evolute | Mahat or buddhi (intellect, the faculty of determination) | 1 |
| Second | Ahaṃkāra (the "I-maker", sense of self) | 1 |
| Sattvic branch | Manas (the coordinating mind), five sense capacities (hearing, touch, sight, taste, smell), five action capacities (speech, hands, feet, excretion, generation) | 11 |
| Tamasic branch | Five subtle elements (*tanmātra*: sound, touch, form, taste, smell) | 5 |
| From the subtle elements | Five gross elements (space, air, fire, water, earth) | 5 |
| Total of prakṛti and its products | | 24 |
| The witness | Puruṣa | 1 |

So the famous "twenty-four tattvas" are prakṛti and its products, and puruṣa is the twenty-fifth.
The system also classifies them by role (SK 3): one that is only a producer (prakṛti), seven that are both produced and producers (buddhi, ahaṃkāra, five subtle elements), sixteen that are only produced (the eleven capacities and five gross elements), and puruṣa, which neither produces nor is produced [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 3].

The order in which things emerge is also fixed.
Ahaṃkāra is the turning point: when sattva predominates in it, the eleven capacities arise, and when tamas predominates, the subtle elements arise, with rajas energising both (SK 24-25) [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 24-25].
This is a model of how a perceiving being is assembled: first a faculty of judgement, then a sense of self, then instruments for knowing and acting, and finally the world of objects those instruments meet.

## Causation: the effect is already in the cause

Behind this picture is a theory of causation called *satkāryavāda*, "the doctrine that the effect exists [beforehand]".
The *Kārikā* gives five reasons for it (SK 9): what does not exist cannot be produced; a particular effect needs a particular material cause; not everything can come from everything; a cause can produce only what it is capable of producing; and the effect is of the same nature as its cause [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 9].
Commentators illustrate it with oil and sesame: you can press oil out of sesame seeds but not out of sand, because the oil was latent in the seed.

For Samkhya, then, creation is a manifestation of what was already there in latent form, and dissolution is its return to latency.
The rival Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika school takes the opposite position, that an effect is genuinely new when it is produced (see the companion article on Nyaya and Vaisheshika) [@src.burley-2007-classical-samkhya-yoga].

## Liberation: kaivalya

If puruṣa is untouched by everything, why does anyone suffer?
Samkhya answers that suffering is a mistake of identification.
Puruṣa seems to be happy or sad only because the buddhi, lit by consciousness like a clear mirror, carries those states and consciousness seems to be inside them [@src.burley-2007-classical-samkhya-yoga].

The cure is knowledge: the discriminating insight that "I am not this, this is not mine, this is not I" (SK 64) [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 64].
The *Kārikā* compares prakṛti to a dancer who stops performing once the audience has seen her (SK 59), and it says the body can continue for a while after the insight, as a potter's wheel keeps turning after the push (SK 67), until at death comes complete and final isolation, *kaivalya* [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, SK 59, 67-68].

It is worth being clear about what this liberation is not.
It is not a heaven or a blissful union with a divine being.
Because experience of any kind belongs to prakṛti, kaivalya is the cessation of experience as such: puruṣa remains, simply as witness with nothing to witness.
Many readers find this austere, and the tradition itself spent centuries debating it [@src.larson-1987-encyclopedia-samkhya].

## Debates and legacy

**Does Samkhya deny God?**
The *Kārikā* does not mention a creator.
Later tradition therefore labelled classical Samkhya atheistic, while the closely related Yoga school adds a special "Lord" (*Īśvara*) to the same twenty-five principles [@src.burley-2007-classical-samkhya-yoga].
Scholars differ on whether the omission was a rejection or simply that the question was not one the system needed to answer.

**Can two such different things interact?**
This is the oldest objection, and it applies to any strict dualism.
Samkhya's answer, that their mere proximity triggers the unfolding, is deliberately minimal and has never satisfied everyone [@src.larson-1987-encyclopedia-samkhya].

**Many puruṣas or one?**
The Upanishadic Vedānta traditions hold that consciousness is one, and they argued against Samkhya's plurality and its independent, unconscious prakṛti [@src.larson-1987-encyclopedia-samkhya].

Whatever one makes of the metaphysics, Samkhya's categories became the shared vocabulary of Indian thought.
The three guṇas, the idea of prakṛti as a field of constant change, and the model of a layered mind-body feature in the Yoga tradition and in later Sanskrit philosophy, often re-interpreted by schools that rejected Samkhya's dualism [@src.larson-1987-encyclopedia-samkhya; @src.burley-2007-classical-samkhya-yoga].
