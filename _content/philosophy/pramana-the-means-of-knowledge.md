Walking at dusk, you see a coil on the path and jump back: a snake.
A moment later a torch shows it is a rope.
Your first judgment was vivid and sincere, and it was wrong.
How could you have known beforehand?
What does it mean to say the second judgment is better?

This humble scene is the favourite example of Indian epistemology, the study of *pramāṇa*, the "means of valid knowledge".
Its authors asked, with great technical care, what the sources of knowledge are, how much trust each deserves, and how mistakes come about [@src.matilal-1986-perception; @src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].

## The vocabulary

Indian theory separates four things.
The knower is the *pramātā*, the object known is the *prameya*, the result, a true cognition, is the *pramā*, and the instrument that produces it is the *pramāṇa* [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].
The question of the schools was: what are the legitimate pramanas?
A "source" counts only if it gives knowledge not obtained by another route, and if it is reliable when working normally.

The reason the topic mattered is not purely academic.
The Nyaya school opens its foundational sutras by listing the pramanas first among its sixteen topics, and promises that understanding the topics leads to the highest good [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india; @src.ganeri-2001-philosophy-classical-india].
For many schools, getting knowledge right and getting free of suffering were two sides of the same task.

## How many sources of knowledge?

The schools disagreed, and the disagreement is the main plot of the subject.

| School | Sources of knowledge accepted |
|---|---|
| Carvaka (materialists) | Perception only |
| Buddhist logicians (Dignaga, Dharmakirti) | Perception and inference |
| Vaisheshika | Perception and inference (traditionally) |
| Samkhya | Perception, inference, reliable testimony |
| Nyaya | Perception, inference, analogy (*upamāna*), testimony (*śabda*) |
| Prabhakara Mimamsa | The four of Nyaya plus postulation (*arthāpatti*) |
| Bhatta Mimamsa and Advaita Vedanta | Those five plus non-apprehension (*anupalabdhi*) |

This table is a generalisation, and sub-schools varied [@src.ganeri-2001-philosophy-classical-india; @src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].
The Carvaka position is known mainly through their opponents' reports, as no complete Carvaka text survives, so its details should be handled with care.

The extra sources are easy to grasp with examples.
If you know that Devadatta is fat and that he never eats by day, you conclude that he eats at night.
That is postulation: you add a fact to make the others fit.
Non-apprehension is how you know the jug is *not* on the table: you looked, it would have been seen if present, and you did not see it [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].

## Perception, inference and words

Perception was the foundation for almost everyone.
Many authors distinguished a first, bare registering of something from the later act of naming and classifying it, and argued over whether the first stage is knowledge at all [@src.matilal-1986-perception].

Inference was developed into a formal method.
In the Nyaya model, a standard argument has five steps, such as: there is fire on the hill; because there is smoke; wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen; this hill has smoke; therefore fire.
The hard part is the middle claim, the *vyāpti* or pervasion, whose justification is a long-running debate [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].

Testimony is where Indian philosophy looks most unlike a modern textbook, because most schools treated the words of a reliable speaker as a primary source of knowledge, not merely a convenient shortcut.
For Mimamsa, authoritative testimony is the only way to learn *dharma*, and so the unauthored Veda becomes a unique case, as described in the article on Mimamsa [@src.ganeri-2001-philosophy-classical-india].

## How errors arise

If the rope is mistaken for a snake, where did the snake come from?
Schools gave different answers, and later Indian surveys sorted them into a standard list of "theories of error" (*khyāti*) [@src.matilal-1986-perception].

- Nyaya: you really see the rope, but it appears as something else, a snake you remember from before.
- Prabhakara Mimamsa: there is no positive error at all, only a failure to tell apart what you perceive from what you remember.
- Advaita Vedanta: the snake is neither real nor entirely unreal, and is "indescribable" (*anirvacanīya*).
- Buddhist idealist and other schools gave still other analyses [@src.matilal-1986-perception].

These are not idle puzzles; each is a position in a larger picture of what the world is.
The Advaitin uses the rope-snake model to say something about the world as a whole, which is why this example recurs.

## Is a cognition true by itself?

Mimamsa and Nyaya split over this.
Mimamsa says validity is intrinsic: a cognition is taken as true unless a defect or a contradicting cognition appears.
Nyaya says validity must be established by something outside the cognition, such as successful action based on it [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india; @src.taber-2005-hindu-critique-buddhist-epistemology].
The choice has large consequences: an intrinsic-validity view makes a text like the Veda safe, whereas an extrinsic view makes every source answerable to a check.

## Hearing, reflecting, contemplating

Advaita Vedanta joined epistemology to a way of life.
A line in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, spoken by Yajnavalkya to his wife Maitreyi, says that the Self is to be "seen", and explains this as hearing about it, reflecting on it and contemplating it (*śrotavyo mantavyo nididhyāsitavyaḥ*, BU 2.4.5) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
Later Advaita writers read this as three stages:

1. **Śravaṇa**, hearing: the systematic study of Upanishadic teaching with a teacher.
2. **Manana**, reflection: resolving doubts by reasoning.
3. **Nididhyāsana**, contemplation: sustained meditation until the understanding is no longer overridden by habit.

The three-stage scheme is a later systematisation of a single line in an early text; the Upanishad itself does not lay out a curriculum [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
Later Advaita authors differed over how much the second and third stages are needed once a sentence such as "you are that" (*tat tvam asi*, ChU 6.8.7) is properly understood [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].

The role of words here is the point where epistemology and practice meet.
For the Advaitin, the Upanishadic sentence is the pramana for the Self, since the Self cannot be pointed to by the senses.
The Mimamsaka objects that sentences exist to direct action; the Advaitin replies that knowing what is already so is not an action [@src.ganeri-2001-philosophy-classical-india].

## What can cancel a knowing?

A famous Advaita criterion for the real is *bādha*, "sublation": something is real if no later cognition cancels it.
A dream is cancelled on waking; a snake is cancelled by the torch.
Later Advaita manuals arrange this into three orders: the apparent (*prātibhāsika*, like a dream or a rope-snake), the practical (*vyāvahārika*, the everyday world), and the ultimate (*pāramārthika*, Brahman) [@src.ganeri-2001-philosophy-classical-india].
This tidy threefold scheme is a later formalisation and not a simple summary of the Upanishads, and critics from other schools questioned whether an everyday world can be called unreal in any sense.

## Is this "Eastern" versus "Western"?

It is tempting to sum up the difference as "the West studies matter, India studies the Self".
That is too crude.
Indian authors wrote at length about perception of external things, and Western philosophers have written on testimony, on error and on the self.
What does stand out is the explicit, school-by-school catalogue of knowledge sources, the habit of arguing over how many there are, and the close link in many schools between knowledge and liberation [@src.matilal-1986-perception; @src.ganeri-2001-philosophy-classical-india].

## Limits of this account

Nearly every position above has many internal variants, and the dates of the key thinkers are estimated with wide margins.
The three-stage path of hearing, reflection and contemplation is a tradition of reading rather than a text-by-text proof.
Modern guided "self-inquiry" practices should be treated as living traditions with their own teachers; they are outside the scope of this article, which does not give a practice manual.
