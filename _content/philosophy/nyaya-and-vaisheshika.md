You see a thin line of smoke rising from behind a hill, and you say, "There is a fire over there."
You have not seen the fire.
How do you *know*?

Two classical Indian schools grew from taking that question seriously.
Nyāya (न्याय) asked how knowledge works and how to argue well.
Vaiśeṣika (वैशेषिक) asked what kinds of things the world contains.
Over the centuries they merged into a single tradition, usually called Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, which was realist: the world exists independently of us, and careful reasoning can know it [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy; @src.matilal-1986-perception].

## Texts and dates

Tradition attributes the *Nyāyasūtra* to Gautama (also called Akṣapāda) and the *Vaiśeṣikasūtra* to Kaṇāda.
Neither author can be dated, and both texts look like works that grew by accretion.
Scholars generally place their composition in the last centuries BCE and the first centuries CE, and some parts of the *Nyāyasūtra* are probably later still [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy; @src.ganeri-2001-indian-logic].
The range is wide because the evidence is internal: the texts' own arguments, and the earliest thinkers who respond to them.

The tradition then developed through commentary.
Vātsyāyana wrote the great commentary (*Bhāṣya*) on the Nyāya aphorisms, probably in the fifth century CE, and Uddyotakara defended Nyāya against Buddhist critics soon afterwards.
For Vaiśeṣika the key early work is Praśastapāda's *Padārthadharmasaṃgraha*, around the sixth century CE [@src.matilal-1986-perception].
Much later, Gaṅgeśa (about 1300 CE) founded "New Nyāya" (*Navya-Nyāya*), a highly technical style of analysis that dominated Sanskrit scholarship for centuries [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].
Tradition places Gautama and Kaṇāda in remote antiquity without giving dates, and this article does not turn that into a calendar year.

## Nyaya's project: knowing and arguing

The first aphorism lists sixteen topics for study, including the means of knowledge, the objects of knowledge, doubt, debate and the types of fallacy (NS 1.1.1).
The second states the aim: right understanding removes error, and with it the chain of faults, activity, birth and suffering, until liberation (NS 1.1.2) [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, NS 1.1.1-2].
So for Nyāya, logic is not an academic game.
Clear thinking is part of the path to freedom from suffering.

The central idea is *pramāṇa*, a "means of valid knowledge".
Nyāya accepts four (NS 1.1.3), and defines each in the next few aphorisms [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, NS 1.1.3-7].

| Pramāṇa | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Pratyakṣa | perception, knowledge arising from contact between a sense and its object | seeing a pot |
| Anumāna | inference, knowledge that follows from a prior sign | smoke, so fire |
| Upamāna | comparison, learning from a stated similarity | told "a gavaya (wild ox) is like a cow", you recognise one |
| Śabda | testimony of a trustworthy person | a reliable guide, or the Veda for those who accept it |

Other schools disagree about the count.
The materialist Cārvāka school accepts only perception, Buddhist logicians accept perception and inference, and Mīmāṃsā and Advaita add further sources such as presumption (*arthāpatti*) and non-perception (*anupalabdhi*) [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].
Later Nyāya also refined perception, distinguishing a first, undifferentiated grasp (*nirvikalpaka*) from the determinate cognition that applies a concept ("this is a pot") [@src.matilal-1986-perception].

Notice what Nyāya claims: knowledge is a real relation to a real thing.
Error is not a flawless cognition of an illusory object, but a mismatch between what we think and how things are.
This is the realist core of the school [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].

## The five-step argument

Nyāya's most famous contribution is its model of a well-formed inference, with five members (NS 1.1.32) [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, NS 1.1.32].

1. **Pratijñā** (thesis): the hill has fire.
2. **Hetu** (reason): because it has smoke.
3. **Udāharaṇa** (example with rule): whatever has smoke has fire, like a kitchen hearth.
4. **Upanaya** (application): this hill has smoke of that kind.
5. **Nigamana** (conclusion): therefore the hill has fire.

Many versions also add a negative example: "whatever lacks fire lacks smoke, like a lake".
The example, a concrete case in which the rule is already observed, is what distinguishes this form from a bare Aristotelian syllogism.
Because the argument was designed for debate, it states the thesis, the reason and the example in the order a persuasive speaker would use them [@src.matilal-1986-perception; @src.ganeri-2001-indian-logic].
Later Buddhist logicians, beginning with Dignāga, argued for a shorter form, and the exchange between the two sides sharpened both [@src.ganeri-2001-indian-logic].

The *Nyāyasūtra* also distinguishes three kinds of inference (cause to effect, effect to cause, and by general correlation, NS 1.1.5) and catalogues five kinds of faulty reason (NS 1.2.4), such as a reason that is also present where the conclusion is absent [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, NS 1.1.5, 1.2.4].

## The problem of the rule: vyāpti

The hard part is step 3.
How do we know that *every* smoke is accompanied by fire, including cases we have never seen?
Nyāya calls this universal link *vyāpti* ("pervasion").
It is a version of what Western philosophers call the problem of induction, and Indian thinkers gave it serious attention [@src.matilal-1986-perception].

The standard Nyāya answer is that repeated observation of the two together, with no observed counter-instance, is the basis, and that "hypothetical reasoning" (*tarka*) tests the link: suppose there were smoke without fire, what absurdity would follow? [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].
Notice that the rule only runs one way.
Wherever there is smoke there is fire, but not wherever there is fire is there smoke, as a red-hot iron ball shows.
Later Nyāya, especially Gaṅgeśa's school, analysed such cases with the idea of an *upādhi*, a hidden "limiting condition" that explains why a mere correlation fails to be a true pervasion [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].
This elaborate analysis of vyāpti and upādhi belongs to the later tradition, not to the aphorisms of Gautama.

## Vaisheshika: the categories of what exists

Kaṇāda's aphorisms begin from a different question: what is there?
The *Vaiśeṣikasūtra* sorts everything into six categories (*padārtha*, "the meaning of a word") (VS 1.1.4) [@src.radhakrishnan-1957-source-book-indian-philosophy, VS 1.1.4].

- **Dravya** (substance): the things that bear qualities. Nine are listed: earth, water, fire, air, ether (*ākāśa*), time, space, self and mind (VS 1.1.5).
- **Guṇa** (quality): colour, taste, smell, touch, number, size, pleasure, pain, desire and so on.
- **Karma** (motion): such as lifting, falling or contracting.
- **Sāmānya** (universal): what a class shares, for example "cow-ness" in every cow.
- **Viśeṣa** (particularity): what makes eternal things, such as atoms and souls, ultimately distinct from each other. The school is named after this category.
- **Samavāya** (inherence): the permanent tie by which a quality sits in a substance, or a cloth in its threads, as opposed to mere contact.

A seventh category, *abhāva* ("absence"), was added by later Vaiśeṣika writers.
It covers cases such as the absence of a pot on a table, and Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika recognised several kinds, including absence before something is made, after it is destroyed, and mutual difference [@src.matilal-1986-perception; @src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].
So the often-quoted "seven categories" are a later development, and Kaṇāda's own list has six.

The distinctive claim is that these categories are real, not mere words or mental labels.
When you say "the cloth is red", there is a substance, a quality, and a real relation of inherence between them.
This is a robust realism about universals and qualities, and it separates Vaiśeṣika from Buddhist schools that deny enduring substances [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].

## Atoms

Vaiśeṣika is best known for its atomic theory (*paramāṇuvāda*).
The four material elements (earth, water, fire, air) are each composed of invisible, indivisible, eternal atoms.
In the form transmitted by Praśastapāda and later authors, two atoms combine to form a dyad (*dvyaṇuka*) and three dyads form a triad (*tryaṇuka*), the smallest thing large enough to be seen, like a mote of dust in a sunbeam [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy; @src.matilal-1986-perception].

Why atoms?
The usual argument is a regress.
If matter could be divided forever, a mountain and a mustard seed would each have infinitely many parts and so would be the same size.
There must be a smallest, partless unit [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].
Atoms are inferred by reason; no one claims to see them.

Atoms do not set themselves in motion.
Vaiśeṣika appeals to *adṛṣṭa*, the "unseen" moral force produced by the actions of souls, to account for the initial motion and combination of atoms.
Later Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika joined this to the claim that a creator God, Īśvara, directs the process, and the philosopher Udayana, around the turn of the first millennium CE, offered formal arguments for God's existence [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].

It is worth being careful in comparing this with modern physics.
Vaiśeṣika atoms are qualitative (earth atoms have smell, for example), are eternal, and are a conclusion of philosophical reasoning rather than of measurement.
They have a family resemblance to Greek and modern atomism, but they are not the same theory, and the texts cannot settle questions of influence between traditions [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].

## Effects are new: a rival to Samkhya

Where Samkhya holds that the effect pre-exists in its cause, Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika holds that a new effect, such as a cloth, genuinely comes into being when threads are combined, a new whole that is more than the threads (*asatkāryavāda*) [@src.burley-2007-classical-samkhya-yoga; @src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].
This follows from their realism: wholes, qualities and universals are real, and so a new whole is a new real thing.

On the self, too, they part company with Samkhya and with Vedānta.
In Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika the self (*ātman*) is an eternal substance, and consciousness is not its essence but a quality that arises when the self is joined to a mind and a body.
In deep sleep, with no mind-body contact, there is no consciousness [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india].

## Why it matters

Nyāya's rules for debate, its catalogue of fallacies and its theory of evidence gave other Indian philosophers, whether or not they were Nyāya followers, a shared standard of argument.
Buddhist, Jaina and Vedānta writers all engaged with Nyāya's methods, and in the later period Navya-Nyāya's precise technical language entered grammar, law and literary theory as well [@src.matilal-1986-perception; @src.ganeri-2001-indian-logic].
Those who study it today often value it less for the answers than for the care: the habit of asking what, exactly, makes a belief justified.
