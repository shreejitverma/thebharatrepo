Every night you do something strange.
You stop experiencing the world, and in the morning you are sure you are the same person.
The Mandukya Upanishad treats that ordinary fact as a clue to what the self is.

## A very short text

The Mandukya is attached to the Atharvaveda and consists of twelve short prose sentences, the briefest of the principal Upanishads [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
Scholars usually count it among the later Upanishads, though no inscription or foreign record fixes its date, so a dating range spanning several centuries is the honest one [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
Its fame owes much to Gaudapada, whose verse Karika on the Upanishad is the earliest surviving systematic work of Advaita Vedanta, and to the commentary tradition that follows it [@src.king-1995-early-advaita-vedanta-buddhism].

## Om is everything

The text opens with a sound: "Om is this whole world."
The past, the present and the future are all just Om, and so is whatever lies beyond time [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, MaU 1].
Then it makes two statements that drive the rest: "All this is Brahman. This self is Brahman, and this self has four quarters" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, MaU 2].
The second sentence, *ayam ātmā brahma*, is one of the statements Vedanta later calls "great sayings".

## The four quarters

The four quarters are described in turn.

| Quarter | Text | Description |
|---|---|---|
| First: waking | MaU 3 | Outward-knowing, with seven limbs and nineteen mouths, enjoying gross things; called *Vaiśvānara* |
| Second: dreaming | MaU 4 | Inward-knowing, same limbs and mouths, enjoying subtle things; called *Taijasa*, "the luminous" |
| Third: deep sleep | MaU 5-6 | Where the sleeper desires nothing and sees no dream; unified, a mass of cognition, made of bliss and enjoying bliss; called *Prājña* |
| Fourth | MaU 7 | Not inward-knowing, not outward-knowing, not a mass of cognition; unseen, beyond ordinary dealings, beyond grasp and thought; the cessation of the world's multiplicity, peaceful, benign, non-dual |

[@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads]

Three details need care.
First, the "nineteen mouths" are not explained in the text itself; the commentators list them as five sense organs, five organs of action, five vital breaths and four inner faculties (mind, intellect, ego and thought).
Second, the Upanishad names the fourth simply as "the fourth" (*caturtha*); the word *turīya* is the later tradition's usage.
Third, the cosmic names the tradition attaches to the three quarters (Virāṭ for waking, Hiraṇyagarbha for dreaming, Īśvara for deep sleep) are commentarial. The Upanishad itself calls the third quarter "lord of all", "knower of all" and "inner controller", and "the source and the end of beings" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, MaU 6].

## Why the fourth is not a "fourth state"

A natural reading is that the fourth is simply one more state, to be reached after the other three.
The wording resists that: it is defined by negations, and it is the one "to be known", the self [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, MaU 7].
Later Advaita reads it as the background in which waking, dream and sleep appear and disappear, and not as a rival to them.
That reading is the tradition's interpretation and is persuasive on the wording, but the Upanishad does not argue it explicitly.

## The sound map

Verses 8 to 11 lay the quarters over the three parts of the syllable.
The sound A is the waking quarter, and the text explains it by "pervading" and "being first".
U is the dreaming quarter, linked with "exaltation" and "being in between".
M is the deep-sleep quarter, linked with "measuring" and "merging".
The part that is no part, the silence after Om, is the fourth [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, MaU 8-12].
Each verse also promises a reward to the one who knows this, for example that the knower of the first part obtains all desires and becomes first [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, MaU 8].
The final verse says the fourth is without parts, beyond dealings, the cessation of multiplicity, benign and non-dual, and that the knower "enters the self with the self" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, MaU 12].
The wordplay is a piece of Sanskrit etymology of the Upanishadic kind, not phonetic science.

## Where the idea came from

The Mandukya compresses older material.
The Brihadaranyaka already speaks of the person's two places and a third, the junction of dream [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 4.3.9].
The Chandogya tells of Indra's long apprenticeship under Prajapati, in which Indra rejects the self of the waking body and then of the dreamer, and is troubled even by deep sleep, where "one does not know oneself" [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 8.7-12].
The Mandukya's contribution is to organise these observations into a single scheme and attach it to Om.

## Gaudapada and the Karika

Gaudapada's Karika has four chapters.
The first is a verse commentary on the Upanishad, and the later ones argue for the unreality of duality (the "no-origination" doctrine, *ajātivāda*) and use the rope-and-snake image to show how something real can be misperceived [@src.king-1995-early-advaita-vedanta-buddhism].
The image is Gaudapada's and the later tradition's; it is not in the Upanishad, and it should not be read back into the text as though it were.

Gaudapada's date and affinities are contested.
Richard King argues that the Karika draws on Mahayana Buddhist philosophical vocabulary and arguments, especially from Madhyamaka and Yogacara, and places Gaudapada around the 6th century CE [@src.king-1995-early-advaita-vedanta-buddhism].
Traditional Advaita holds that Gaudapada is the teacher of Shankara's teacher and that the system is a restatement of the Upanishads, not an import.
The two positions can be partly reconciled by noting that the Karika can both quote the Upanishads and use the intellectual vocabulary of its time; where historians and tradition part ways is on the weight given to Buddhist influence.

## What the text gives us

The Mandukya does not prove that there is a witness behind the three states.
It points to the ordinary experience of sleep, notes that the "I" survives across the gaps, and asks the reader to consider what that implies.
Whether the answer is a single non-dual reality, as Advaita holds, or something else, as other Vedanta schools hold, is a matter on which the archive's articles on those traditions present the arguments.
