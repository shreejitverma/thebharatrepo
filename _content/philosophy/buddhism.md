Buddhism arose in the middle Ganges region of northern India in the age of the early cities and kingdoms, and for well over a thousand years it was one of India's major religions [@src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism; @src.thapar-2002-early-india].
It began with one teacher, Siddhartha Gautama, later called the Buddha ("the awakened one") and Shakyamuni ("sage of the Shakyas").

## When did the Buddha live?

Older books give 563 to 483 BCE.
That date is not an ancient record: it is a modern adjustment of Sri Lankan Buddhist chronology, which places the Buddha's death in 544 or 543 BCE [@src.cousins-1996-dating-historical-buddha; @src.gombrich-2006-theravada-buddhism].
A 1988 symposium in Germany, published by Heinz Bechert in 1991 and 1992, tested these chronologies against other evidence, and many contributors concluded that the death fell later, in the decades around 400 BCE [@src.cousins-1996-dating-historical-buddha].
Gombrich, for example, argued from the lists of teachers in the monastic tradition and from other synchronisms for a date within a few decades of 400 BCE [@src.gombrich-2006-theravada-buddhism].
Some scholars still defend earlier dates, so this article treats the death as "about 420 to 350 BCE" and keeps the traditional date as a separate entry [@src.cousins-1996-dating-historical-buddha].

## What we can show, and what is tradition

The strongest early evidence is an inscription of the emperor Ashoka, set up at Lumbini in about 249 BCE, which says that the Buddha Shakyamuni was born there [@src.hultzsch-1925-inscriptions-of-asoka].
Excavations at Lumbini reported a timber structure beneath the brick shrine, which the excavators dated to the sixth century BCE and took to be an early shrine; other scholars have called that interpretation speculative [@src.coningham-2013-earliest-buddhist-shrine].
Most of the life story, including the dates usually given for the birth at Lumbini, the awakening at Bodh Gaya and the death at Kushinagar, comes from texts written down centuries later [@src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism].
Those dates are traditional and are not given here as history.

## The teaching

The Buddha's core teaching is summarised in the Four Noble Truths: dukkha (unsatisfactoriness, often translated as "suffering"), its origin in craving, its cessation in nirvana, and a path leading to that cessation [@src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism].
The path has eight parts: right view, resolve, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration (samadhi).
It is commonly grouped into ethics, meditation and wisdom [@src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism].
Gombrich has argued that these ideas should be read as a response to the Brahmanical and ascetic thought of the Buddha's own time, including ideas about karma and rebirth that Buddhism reinterpreted ethically [@src.gombrich-1996-how-buddhism-began].
Buddhism is better described as a religion or path of practice than as "non-theistic philosophy": gods appear in its cosmology, but they are not creators and cannot grant liberation [@src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism].

## The community and the councils

The Buddha taught a community of monks and nuns, the sangha, whose rules are kept in the Vinaya texts [@src.gombrich-2006-theravada-buddhism].
Tradition tells of councils at Rajagriha shortly after the death, at Vaishali about a century later, and at Pataliputra under Ashoka.
The details come from the monastic texts and the Sri Lankan chronicles, and different schools tell them differently [@src.gombrich-2006-theravada-buddhism].
Ashoka's own inscriptions, which are contemporary, do not mention a council, though one of them addresses the sangha and recommends passages of the teaching [@src.hultzsch-1925-inscriptions-of-asoka; @src.thapar-2002-early-india].
Ashoka also promoted a public ethic he called dhamma and sent envoys to named Hellenistic kings; historians generally treat dhamma as a broader moral policy, not as a simple endorsement of one sect [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].

## Schools and the rise of the Mahayana

Within a few centuries the community had divided into several schools, among them the Sthaviras, from whom the Theravada descends, and the Mahasamghikas [@src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism].
The Mahayana ("great vehicle") emerged slowly from about the first century BCE to the first century CE as new texts and ideals, notably the bodhisattva path, appeared within existing monastic communities [@src.williams-2009-mahayana-buddhism].
It was not created by a council, and the term "Hinayana" ("lesser vehicle") was a polemical label used by its critics, so modern scholars prefer "Nikaya" or "mainstream" schools for the others [@src.williams-2009-mahayana-buddhism; @src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism].
A later council under King Kanishka, in the first or second century CE, is reported in some northern traditions, but it did not divide the community into two [@src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism].

## Decline in India

By about the thirteenth century Buddhism had largely faded from most of India, though it had by then spread across Asia [@src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism].
Historians point to several interacting causes, including shifts in royal and merchant patronage, the growth of devotional Hindu traditions and the destruction of major monasteries in the north, and they do not agree on a single explanation [@src.thapar-2002-early-india; @src.harvey-2013-introduction-to-buddhism].
