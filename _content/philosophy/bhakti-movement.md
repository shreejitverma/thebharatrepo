Bhakti means devotion, a personal and emotional relationship with a god.
From about the sixth century CE a body of devotional poetry in the regional languages of India grew up around gods such as Vishnu, Shiva, Krishna and Rama, and it shaped later religious life across the subcontinent [@src.hawley-2015-storm-of-songs; @src.hardy-1983-viraha-bhakti].

## Earlier roots

The word and the idea are older than the poets.
The Bhagavad Gita presents loving devotion to Krishna as a way to liberation [@src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts].
What is new in the early medieval period is the large body of vernacular song composed by individuals who describe their own experience of the god [@src.hawley-2015-storm-of-songs].

## The Tamil saints

The earliest of these bodies of poetry that survives in quantity is in Tamil.
The Alvars were Vaishnava poets; their hymns were later gathered in the Nalayira Divya Prabandham, and they include Nammalvar, whose poems have been translated by A. K. Ramanujan [@src.hardy-1983-viraha-bhakti; @src.ramanujan-1981-hymns-for-the-drowning].
The Nayanars were Shaiva saints, among them Sambandar, Appar and Sundarar, whose hymns formed the core of what became the Shaiva canon [@src.peterson-1989-poems-to-siva].
Their poems span roughly the sixth to ninth centuries CE.
Many describe longing for the god, which Hardy called viraha-bhakti, devotion shaped by the feeling of separation and love [@src.hardy-1983-viraha-bhakti].
Tradition counts twelve Alvars and sixty-three Nayanars, and the saints came from many backgrounds, including some from outside the higher castes [@src.peterson-1989-poems-to-siva; @src.hardy-1983-viraha-bhakti].

## Spread across India

Devotional poetry in other languages followed.
In Karnataka the Virashaiva (Lingayat) saints, from about the twelfth century, wrote short vernacular poems called vachanas that criticised temple ritual and birth-based hierarchy [@src.ramanujan-1973-speaking-of-siva].
In the north, from about the fifteenth century, poets such as Kabir, Ravidas, Surdas, Mirabai and later Tulsidas composed in Hindi dialects, and Guru Nanak composed in Punjabi in a milieu related to the north Indian Sants [@src.hawley-2005-three-bhakti-voices; @src.mcleod-1968-guru-nanak].
Philosophical teachers gave bhakti a theology: Ramanuja, a Vaishnava teacher of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, argued that the world and souls are real and dependent on a personal God [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja].

## The "movement" is a modern idea

Hawley's A Storm of Songs shows that the picture of a single bhakti movement that began in the south and travelled north is itself a modern construction, built in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries by scholars and writers [@src.hawley-2015-storm-of-songs].
He does not deny the poetry or its connections.
He argues that the older story of one movement, with a single origin and a single direction, hides the variety of the poets and their regional settings [@src.hawley-2015-storm-of-songs].
For this reason many historians now write about bhakti traditions, in the plural.

## Bhakti and caste

It is often said that bhakti rejected caste and ritual.
Some poets did sharply criticise them.
Kabir's verses mock priests and pundits, and the Virashaiva vachanas attack caste and temple worship [@src.hess-2015-bodies-of-song; @src.ramanujan-1973-speaking-of-siva].
Others worked within existing social order: Ramanuja's school preserved Vedic and temple practice, and many poets and communities accepted caste in daily life [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.hawley-2015-storm-of-songs].
A fair summary is that bhakti gave people of many backgrounds a voice and a direct route to the divine, while the effect on caste in practice varied by place and community [@src.hawley-2015-storm-of-songs].

## Dating the poets

The dates of individual poets are less certain than the surrounding story suggests.
Kabir is traditionally given as 1440 to 1518, and some accounts give a longer life, but the scholarship says only that he lived in the fifteenth century [@src.hess-2015-bodies-of-song; @src.hawley-2005-three-bhakti-voices].
Mirabai is associated with the first half of the sixteenth century. Her exact dates are uncertain, and many of the songs attributed to her are later compositions in her name [@src.martin-2023-mirabai; @src.hawley-2005-three-bhakti-voices].
The Shrivaishnava tradition dates Ramanuja to 1017 to 1137 CE. John Carman proposes about 1077 to 1157 CE [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja].
Guru Nanak (1469 to 1539) is better documented, and his followers later formed the distinct Sikh community [@src.mcleod-1968-guru-nanak].
Sufi saints such as Nizamuddin Auliya, who taught Islamic devotion in the same centuries, are sometimes listed beside the bhakti poets, but they belong to a different tradition that interacted with it.
