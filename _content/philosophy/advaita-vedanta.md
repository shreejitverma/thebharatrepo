At dusk, a coiled rope can look like a snake. The fear is real, and the snake seems present, but a lamp reveals that the mistake depended on not seeing the rope clearly. Advaita Vedanta uses examples of mistaken perception to ask a deeper question: could our experience of ourselves as separate from one another and from ultimate reality also rest on a misunderstanding? Its answer is that the self's deepest nature is not other than Brahman, the reality that is the ground of all things. [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta; @src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta]

The Sanskrit word *advaita* means "not two." It names a family of interpretations of the Upanishads, not a claim that ordinary experience is simply nothing. The philosopher most associated with its classical form is Shankara (Śaṅkara), whose commentaries read the Upanishads, the *Bhagavad Gītā* and the *Brahma Sūtras* as a coherent teaching. Vedanta schools share these texts but disagree about how their statements of unity and difference fit together. [@src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta; @src.isayeva-1993-shankara-indian-philosophy]

## Reading the Upanishads

The Chāndogya Upaniṣad tells of Uddālaka Āruṇi teaching his son Śvetaketu. He begins with the idea that, before the world took its varied forms, "being alone, one only, without a second" existed. Later, he explains how an unseen essence pervades things and repeatedly tells his son *tat tvam asi*, "you are that." The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad gives another compact statement: *aham brahmāsmi*, "I am Brahman." These are the textual starting points for Advaita's account of self and reality. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.2.1; @src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.8.7; @src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BrU 1.4.10]

Shankara takes such sentences as revealing an identity that is obscured in ordinary experience. The body, senses and thoughts change, but the awareness by which they are known is not just another changing object. Advaita calls the innermost self *ātman* and ultimate reality *Brahman*; at the deepest level they are one. This is not the personal ego becoming all-powerful. It is the claim that the ego's apparent separateness does not define what the self finally is. [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta; @src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta]

## Superimposition and ignorance

Shankara opens his commentary on the *Brahma Sūtras* with a discussion called the *adhyāsa-bhāṣya*, or "discussion of superimposition." *Adhyāsa* is the mistaken transfer of one thing's features onto another. His example is mother-of-pearl mistaken for silver: the perception is not sheer nothing, but what is seen is misidentified. In daily life, we similarly attach the body's conditions to the self when we say "I am old" or "I am ill"; we also treat the body as conscious in its own right, although awareness is what lets us know it. [@src.thibaut-1890-vedanta-sutras-shankara, introduction; @src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta]

The rope and snake became a familiar teaching example in Advaita, though Shankara's opening discussion uses mother-of-pearl and silver. The analogy helps explain how an error can be vivid without being ultimately true: a real basis is misperceived, and corrected understanding removes the error. It is a model for ignorance, not a literal account of how the physical universe was made. [@src.thibaut-1890-vedanta-sutras-shankara, introduction; @src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta]

Advaita uses *avidyā* (ignorance) for the failure to know the self as it is. Shankara also uses *māyā*, but later writers give the term a more systematic role in explaining how plurality appears. The details of ignorance, where it resides and how it relates to Brahman became topics of debate among Advaitins themselves. Sengaku Maeda's study traces important changes between Shankara and Sarvajñātman, so familiar later formulas should not automatically be read back as Shankara's own settled theory. [@src.maeda-1975-avidya-sankara-sarvajnatman; @src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta; @src.das-1952-brahman-maya-advaita]

## What does it mean to call the world dependent?

"The world is an illusion" is an easy slogan, but it can suggest that Advaita denies the existence of everyday life. Its more careful claim is that the world is not independent of Brahman and is not ultimate in the way Brahman is. Advaitin discussions use *mithyā* for what is experienced and functions in ordinary life but is not finally self-standing. It is neither absolutely real nor wholly nonexistent, like a square circle. [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta; @src.das-1952-brahman-maya-advaita]

Shankara's commentary on *Brahma Sūtra* 2.1.14 draws on an Upanishadic comparison: by knowing clay, one understands the clay objects made from it; their names and forms vary, while their material basis is clay. Advaita extends this kind of dependence to the relation between the experienced world and Brahman. Other Vedanta schools reject the extension, arguing that a real world and real distinctions cannot be reduced to ignorance. [@src.thibaut-1890-vedanta-sutras-shankara, BSB 2.1.14; @src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.1.4; @src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja]

Later Advaita teaching often distinguishes three orders of reality. The table is a useful map of that later systematisation, not a three-part scheme that should be attributed without qualification to Shankara himself. [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta; @src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta]

| Order | Sanskrit | Example | How it is corrected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apparent | *prātibhāsika* | A rope mistaken for a snake; a dream | Closer perception or waking |
| Everyday | *vyāvahārika* | Bodies, duties and shared experience | Knowledge of Brahman, according to Advaita |
| Ultimate | *pāramārthika* | Brahman | Never cancelled by a deeper knowledge |

The test is often described as sublation: a later, better-grounded understanding cancels an earlier one. Waking corrects a dream; seeing the rope corrects the snake. Advaita says knowledge of Brahman corrects the assumption that the everyday world and its perceiver are ultimately separate. This is a philosophical claim about levels of understanding, not a scientific claim that physical laws stop working. [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta; @src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta]

## Knowledge and liberation

Shankara's commentary on the first *Brahma Sūtra* says that inquiry into Brahman presupposes preparation: discernment between the lasting and the transient, dispassion toward rewards, qualities such as calm and self-control, and a desire for liberation. Later teaching commonly gathers these under the four qualifications, *sādhana-catuṣṭaya*. In its six qualities, the order varies in presentation, but includes calm (*śama*), restraint (*dama*), withdrawal (*uparati*), endurance (*titikṣā*), trust (*śraddhā*) and concentration (*samādhāna*). [@src.thibaut-1890-vedanta-sutras-shankara, BSB 1.1.1; @src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta]

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka describes the self as something to be heard about, reflected upon and contemplated. Advaita teachers develop these as *śravaṇa*, listening to the teaching; *manana*, reasoning through doubts; and *nididhyāsana*, sustained contemplation. The aim is not to manufacture a new self but to remove mistaken identification. In Shankara's account, liberating knowledge is decisive; ritual and ethical disciplines can prepare a person but do not produce Brahman as a new result. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BrU 2.4.5; @src.thibaut-1890-vedanta-sutras-shankara, BSB 1.1.1; @src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta]

## Questions from other Vedanta schools

Critics ask how beginningless ignorance can be explained if Brahman is changeless and there is no second reality outside it. Ramanuja argues that locating ignorance either in Brahman or in the individual self creates difficulties, and rejects the idea that the world is a mistaken appearance. Advaita authors answer that such objections assume distinctions that hold at the everyday level, while the inquiry concerns what is ultimately true. The dispute is philosophical and textual: the schools use many of the same scriptures but weigh and interpret their statements differently. [@src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja; @src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta]

Shankara's own dates are also uncertain. Modern scholars have placed him in different parts of the eighth century; Nakamura argues for around 700 to 750 CE, while the widely repeated 788 to 820 dates derive from later records. The Kanchi matha tradition places him much earlier, in 509 to 477 BCE, and other lineages give different chronologies. These should be presented as separate historical and traditional claims, not blended into one settled timeline. [@src.nakamura-1983-early-vedanta; @src.isayeva-1993-shankara-indian-philosophy]

Advaita's enduring question is whether the apparent division between knower, known and knowing is final. Its answer is that disciplined inquiry can show the deepest self to be Brahman. Later Advaita elaborated the language of *māyā* and orders of reality, while other Vedanta schools developed rival readings that preserve real difference. [@src.hirst-2005-samkaras-advaita-vedanta; @src.maeda-1975-avidya-sankara-sarvajnatman; @src.carman-1974-theology-of-ramanuja]
