Ask "what am I?" and you can answer in layers.
I am a body.
I am also breathing, feeling, thinking, deciding, and sometimes simply content.
Around the 6th to 4th century BCE, a teacher in the Taittirīya Upaniṣad turned this everyday observation into a method: peel the layers away one by one, and see what is left.
Later Vedānta named the layers *kośa*, "sheaths", and the scheme of five sheaths, *pañca-kośa*, became one of the best-known pictures of the person in Indian thought.

## Where it comes from

The scheme comes from the second chapter of the Taittirīya Upaniṣad, the *Brahmānandavallī* (TU 2.1-5) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
The Upanishad belongs to the earlier prose layer of the corpus; Patrick Olivelle places it before or around the rise of Buddhism, though all such datings are relative and debated [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].

The teaching goes like this.
The Upanishad starts from the body, a person "made of the essence of food" (*anna-rasa-maya*).
It then says that inside this one, and different from it, is a self made of breath, and inside that a self made of mind, then one made of understanding, then one made of bliss (TU 2.1-5) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
Each self fills the shape of the one before it, like a hand in a glove.
Each is pictured as a figure with a head, two sides and a base, which makes it something concrete to meditate on.

A point of precision: the Upanishad speaks of "selves" (*ātman*) made of food, breath and so on.
The word *kośa* as the label for the series is the later tradition's, and it is the vocabulary used in classical Advaita Vedānta [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta].

## The five layers

| Layer | Sanskrit | What it is | Everyday handle |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Annamaya* | The self of food: the physical body | "I am hungry, I am tired" |
| 2 | *Prāṇamaya* | The self of breath or vital energy | "I am energetic, I am short of breath" |
| 3 | *Manomaya* | The self of mind: sensing, wanting, doubting | "I am anxious, I am excited" |
| 4 | *Vijñānamaya* | The self of understanding: judgement and discernment | "I have decided, I know" |
| 5 | *Ānandamaya* | The self of bliss: contentment and joy | "I am at peace" |

The body is called the self of food because it is made from food and returns to the earth, to become food in turn [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].

*Prāṇa* here means more than breath.
Upanishadic texts speak of five functional "breaths" (*prāṇa*, *apāna*, *vyāna*, *udāna*, *samāna*), described, for example, in the Praśna Upaniṣad [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
Popular accounts add a system of subtle channels (*nāḍī*) and map them onto modern physiology, but that elaboration belongs to later yoga and tantric literature and is not found in the Taittirīya scheme.

The mind and understanding layers separate two jobs that English blurs.
*Manas* is the sensing, wanting, doubting mind.
*Vijñāna* is the faculty that discriminates and resolves.
Classical Advaita keeps this division of labour inside its account of the subtle body [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta].

## The bliss layer and a famous measuring scale

The bliss self is described with its own parts: pleasure (*priya*) is its head, delight (*moda*) its right side, and great delight (*pramoda*) its left (TU 2.5) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
The Upanishad then offers a "calculation of bliss" (*ānanda-mīmāṃsā*, TU 2.8).
It starts with a happy, healthy young man who owns the whole earth, calls that one unit of human joy, and multiplies it upward by a hundred at each step through the worlds of ancestors, gods and finally Brahmā [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
The point is not arithmetic.
It says that ordinary joy is a faint sample of something larger, and that a person who is not craving gains access to it.

## Three bodies, three states

Classical Advaita Vedānta later arranged the five sheaths inside a three-part scheme of bodies [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta]:

| Body | Contains | Linked state |
|---|---|---|
| Gross body (*sthūla-śarīra*) | Annamaya | Waking |
| Subtle body (*sūkṣma-śarīra*) | Prāṇamaya, manomaya, vijñānamaya | Dreaming |
| Causal body (*kāraṇa-śarīra*) | Ānandamaya | Deep sleep |

This mapping is a synthesis by later commentators, not something stated in the Taittirīya text itself.
Its logic is neat: in dreams the senses are closed but mind and memory still work, and in deep sleep even those cease, leaving a kind of unexamined peace.

## The self beyond the sheaths

Here the Upanishad does something unexpected.
After describing the bliss self, it adds a last line: *brahma puccham pratiṣṭhā*, "Brahman is the tail, the foundation" (TU 2.5) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
Its teaching is that the layers rest on something that is not itself a layer.

Advaita Vedānta reads this as follows: the five sheaths are all objects of experience, changing and composite, so the self cannot be any of them.
Even the bliss sheath, pleasant as it is, is a layer of experience, and the real self is the witnessing consciousness that is Brahman [@src.deutsch-1969-advaita-vedanta].
In this reading the sheaths are covers over, not parts of, the self, and the exercise of negating each one in turn is a method of self-inquiry.

This is an interpretation, and other readings exist.
The text's own wording makes Brahman the base of the bliss self but leaves room for debate about how the two relate, and commentators have read the passage differently [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
Treat the "witness beyond all sheaths" picture as the Advaita tradition's reading, not a neutral summary of the Upanishad.

## A model of experience, not an anatomy

It is tempting to treat the five koshas as a literal anatomy, with measurable layers and "imbalances" to correct.
Modern wellness writing often does this, adding diagnostic tables and practices assigned to each layer.
Those applications are modern; the Upanishad offers a contemplative argument about identity, not a medical theory [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].

A fair way to use the scheme is as the Upanishad does.
Notice how much of what you call "I" is body, energy, mood, thought or pleasure.
Ask whether any of these is the one who notices them.
The ancient answer, and the continuing disagreement about it, are the beginning of a long philosophical conversation.
