## A useful pair, with a history

Indian philosophy is often introduced through two words: *āstika* (आस्तिक) and *nāstika* (नास्तिक). The usual classroom shorthand calls the first group orthodox because it accepts Vedic authority, and the second heterodox because it does not. This is useful as a first map, but it can mislead if it is treated as a timeless or exhaustive definition. The words have been used in more than one way, and “orthodox” does not mean agreement on God, self, ritual, or liberation. [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism; @src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini]

The distinction is not simply between people who believe in a deity and people who do not. Classical Sāṃkhya is counted among the āstika systems although it does not require a creator God. Conversely, a school can affirm rebirth and moral consequence while rejecting the Veda as an authoritative source. The key question in the familiar school taxonomy is usually the status granted to the Veda, not theism in the modern sense. [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism; @src.collins-1982-selfless-persons]

## What the older wording suggests

Pāṇini’s *Aṣṭādhyāyī* includes a grammatical rule, 4.4.60, that forms words from *asti* (“it is” or “it exists”) and *nāsti* (“it is not” or “it does not exist”) for a person whose view is marked by one of those statements. Later grammatical commentators connect the contrast to belief in another world and the consequences of action. This gives an older semantic setting for the terms, but it does not by itself prove that every early use carried one fixed philosophical definition. [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini, A 4.4.60]

That older sense matters because Buddhists and Jains affirm forms of karma and rebirth. Under the interpretation centered on whether another world exists, they would not neatly fit the picture of nāstikas as people who deny post-mortem existence. The terms must therefore be read in their particular textual context rather than translated mechanically as “believer” and “unbeliever.” [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini; @src.dundas-2003-the-jains; @src.collins-1982-selfless-persons]

## The Veda as a line of classification

A different and influential use appears in the *Mānava-Dharmaśāstra*, often called the *Manusmṛti*. Its verse 2.11 describes as nāstika someone who disparages the Veda. Here the issue is scriptural authority. Later compendia commonly group Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, and Vedānta as six āstika *darśanas* (“viewpoints” or systems), with Buddhism, Jainism, and Cārvāka among the nāstika traditions. The sixfold scheme is a conventional classification, not a claim that each member shares one metaphysics. [@src.olivelle-2005-manus-code-of-law, Manu 2.11; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]

The differences within the six are substantial. Mīmāṃsā develops an account of ritual duty and Vedic sentences without relying on a creator God; Sāṃkhya explains experience through the distinction between *puruṣa* (conscious subject) and *prakṛti* (material nature); and Vedānta schools disagree over how self and ultimate reality relate. Their common classification reflects their acceptance of Vedic authority, while their arguments show that this authority can coexist with sharply different interpretations. [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]

## Shared questions, different answers

The labels can obscure how much debate crossed their boundary. Upanishadic authors ask what the self is and how release from ordinary existence might be possible. Buddhist analysis rejects a permanent self, while explaining continuity through conditioned processes rather than an enduring soul. Steven Collins emphasizes that early Buddhist teaching on not-self is at once an analysis of personhood and a practice aimed at loosening attachment; it should not be reduced to the slogan “nothing exists.” [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.collins-1982-selfless-persons]

Jain philosophy offers a different account. It treats living beings as enduring *jīvas* (souls) bound by karma, and develops *anekāntavāda*, the view that reality has many aspects, alongside qualified forms of predication. Jain thinkers used these ideas to make claims sensitive to standpoint; critics in other schools questioned whether apparently conflicting predicates could be reconciled. The dispute was philosophical, not merely a clash between groups that had no shared vocabulary. [@src.dundas-2003-the-jains]

Cārvāka or Lokāyata presents a special evidentiary problem. No complete early Cārvāka treatise survives, so much of the record comes through quotations and summaries by opponents. Those accounts associate the school with materialism and skepticism about inference, ritual authority, and life after death, but the surviving evidence does not warrant treating every hostile portrait as a transparent self-description. Pradeep Gokhale’s study stresses the need to separate recoverable arguments from polemical caricature. [@src.gokhale-2015-lokayata-carvaka]

## How traditions met

The setting in which these traditions developed is also debated. Johannes Bronkhorst argues that the eastern region he calls “Greater Magadha” had religious ideas and renunciant practices that differed from Vedic Brahmanism, and that these currents influenced later Indian thought. Other accounts emphasize interaction and development within a connected intellectual landscape rather than a clean division into separate cultures. The evidence supports taking regional difference seriously, but the boundaries and direction of influence remain questions for scholarship. [@src.bronkhorst-2007-greater-magadha; @src.thapar-2002-early-india; @src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads]

By the classical period, philosophers wrote against rival positions in increasingly formal ways. Buddhist logicians and Nyāya thinkers, for example, sharpened arguments about perception, inference, language, and the self in a long exchange. The traditions remained distinct, yet they also formed a shared arena of reasoning in which an opponent’s strongest argument had to be answered. [@src.siderits-2017-buddhism-as-philosophy; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]

## Reading the distinction carefully

Use *āstika* and *nāstika* as historically situated labels, then ask what a particular author means by them. In a grammatical discussion, the terms may concern whether something exists; in a Dharmaśāstra passage, they may mark rejection of Vedic authority; in a later survey, they may sort philosophical schools by their relationship to the Veda. None of these uses makes the map of Indian thought simple, and none tells us on its own whether a school believes in God, a soul, rebirth, or liberation. [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini; @src.olivelle-2005-manus-code-of-law; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]
