The Upanishads are a group of Sanskrit texts in which Vedic thought turns inward.
Where the earlier Vedic literature is about getting the sacrifice right, the Upanishads ask what the self is, what the world rests on, and what happens at death.
Their central teaching is that the self (atman) is in some way identical with, or deeply connected to, the ultimate reality of the cosmos (brahman) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.cohen-2018-upanishads-complete-guide].

## Where they sit in the Vedic corpus

Each Veda has a Samhita of mantras, then Brahmana prose on ritual, then Aranyakas and Upanishads.
The early Upanishads are usually embedded in Brahmanas or Aranyakas: the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, for example, is the last part of an Aranyaka attached to the Shatapatha Brahmana, and the Chandogya belongs to the Samaveda tradition [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.gonda-1975-vedic-literature].
That is why they are called Vedanta, "the end of the Veda", in two senses: they come last in the sequence, and later tradition treats them as its culmination.

The word upanishad is commonly explained as "sitting down near" a teacher, and many early passages are indeed records of teaching, often dialogues [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
The word also came to mean a "hidden connection" or secret teaching in the early texts.

## How many, and how old

Tradition counts 108 Upanishads, but they span more than a thousand years and very different religious worlds.
A smaller set of early texts is usually distinguished.
Patrick Olivelle, whose critical translation is the standard reference, places the oldest, the prose Brihadaranyaka and Chandogya, before or around the time of the Buddha, in roughly the 7th to 6th centuries BCE.
Others he places next: Taittiriya, Aitareya and Kaushitaki.
A later group of verse texts follows: Katha, Isha, Shvetashvatara and Mundaka, with texts like Prashna, Maitri and Mandukya after that [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.cohen-2018-upanishads-complete-guide].

All these dates are estimates based on language, style, and the way one text refers to another, with no absolute anchor, and they are graded C on this site.
Tradition gives no such sequence: Vedanta teachers regard the Upanishads as eternal revelation (shruti) rather than as human compositions in time [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
The claim sometimes made that the Upanishads are simply "the most recent part of the Vedas" is true only for the early texts, because other works called Upanishads were composed in the medieval period [@src.cohen-2018-upanishads-complete-guide].

## The main ideas

**Atman and brahman.** In the Brihadaranyaka the teacher Yajnavalkya describes the self as that which cannot itself be grasped, and the text offers the formula "neti, neti", "not this, not this", as a way of speaking about it.
In the Chandogya, the sage Uddalaka Aruni teaches his son Shvetaketu through a series of illustrations (salt dissolved in water, the seed of a banyan tree) and repeats a refrain, "tat tvam asi", usually translated "that you are" (Chandogya 6.8.7).
A line in the Brihadaranyaka has the creator becoming aware of himself and saying "I am Brahman" (aham brahmasmi, 1.4.10) [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].

**Rebirth and action.** The doctrine that actions have consequences carried into future lives, and that one may be released from the cycle, appears prominently in the Brihadaranyaka and Chandogya.
It is not found in the Rigveda hymns. Where the idea came from is discussed below [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.black-2007-character-of-the-self].

**Knowledge as liberation.** Several texts present liberation (moksha) as coming from knowledge of the self, not from performing rituals [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].

## Who speaks

The Upanishads are not only by priests.
Brian Black has shown that these texts stage conversations among Brahmin priests, kings and women, and that the kings and women are sometimes in the position of teacher.
Gargi and Maitreyi take part in debates and teaching with Yajnavalkya in the Brihadaranyaka, and kings such as Ajatashatru of Kashi and Pravahana Jaivali of the Panchalas teach doctrines to Brahmin visitors [@src.black-2007-character-of-the-self].
Black reads this as reflecting a contest over who holds authority, but the texts are literary compositions and we cannot take their scenes as transcripts of real conversations.

## Where scholars disagree

**Origins.** Scholars have long debated how Upanishadic ideas arose.
One view sees them as growing from within Vedic ritual speculation, in which the sacrifice and its parts were already identified with cosmic powers.
Another stresses influence from, or parallel development with, the wandering ascetic (shramana) movements of the Ganga plain that also gave rise to Buddhism and Jainism, especially for rebirth and renunciation.
Many scholars see both currents at work [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.cohen-2018-upanishads-complete-guide; @src.black-2007-character-of-the-self].

**Geography.** The early Upanishads place their scenes in the Kuru-Panchala country and further east, in Kosala and Videha, which fits a Late Vedic date [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru; @src.black-2007-character-of-the-self].

**Date.** As noted, the dating here is mainstream scholarship; Indigenist and traditional positions place the Vedic corpus earlier [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].

## The Upanishads after the Vedic age

The commentators of Vedanta gave the Upanishads a central role.
Shankara, whom most modern scholars place in the 8th century CE, wrote commentaries on a core group of ten, including the Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya, Katha and Mandukya, and presented them as teaching a single non-dual truth [@src.mayeda-1992-thousand-teachings-sankara].
Other Vedanta teachers read the same texts as teaching a qualified non-dualism or a dualism, and the different schools are treated in the article on the six darshanas [@src.king-1999-indian-philosophy].

In 1656 to 1657 the Mughal prince Dara Shukoh had about fifty Upanishads translated into Persian.
That version was the source for the Latin translation by Anquetil-Duperron in 1801 to 1802, which brought the texts to European readers, among them Schopenhauer [@src.cohen-2018-upanishads-complete-guide].
