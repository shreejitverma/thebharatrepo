Jainism is an Indian religion whose followers, the Jains, hold that the universe has no creator and that every living thing has a soul (jiva) that can win release from the cycle of rebirth by discipline and non-violence [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains; @src.jaini-1979-jaina-path-of-purification].
It has been continuously practised for more than two thousand years and remains a living tradition today, especially in western and southern India [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains; @src.long-2009-jainism-introduction].

## The tradition of twenty-four Tirthankaras

Jains teach that in each half of the cosmic cycle twenty-four Tirthankaras ("ford-makers") appear, who find the way across the ocean of rebirth and re-teach the path [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains].
The first of this age is Rishabha, and the last is Vardhamana Mahavira, known to tradition as the twenty-fourth.
The earlier figures are known only from tradition and have no independent historical record [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains].
The twenty-third, Parshva, is different: the Jain canon says he lived about 250 years before Mahavira, and many scholars accept him as a historical teacher whose community Mahavira's followers joined or inherited [@src.jacobi-1884-jaina-sutras-part-1; @src.dundas-2002-the-jains].

## Mahavira and his time

Mahavira was a teacher of the same region and period as the Buddha, in the Ganges plain of eastern India.
Buddhist texts mention a rival teacher called Nigantha Nataputta, usually identified with Mahavira, and this gives an outside confirmation that he lived [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains; @src.bronkhorst-2007-greater-magadha].
Traditional Jain chronology puts his birth in 599 BCE and his liberation in 527 BCE, and the Jain calendar counts from it [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains].
Modern scholars place him in the fifth century BCE, and since the dating of the Buddha is debated, the exact decades are too [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains; @src.bronkhorst-2007-greater-magadha].
Bronkhorst has argued that Jainism, Buddhism and related ascetic movements arose in a "Greater Magadha" culture of eastern India that was distinct from, and for a time outside, the Vedic ritual world; other scholars see more continuity with Vedic and Upanishadic thought [@src.bronkhorst-2007-greater-magadha].

## Core ideas

- **Ahimsa (non-violence).** The central ethical principle is to avoid harming any living being, in act, speech and thought, which is why Jain monks sweep the ground before them and why many Jains avoid certain foods and occupations [@src.jaini-1979-jaina-path-of-purification; @src.dundas-2002-the-jains].
- **Karma as a kind of matter.** Jains understand karma as subtle matter that binds to the soul because of passion and action, and that is shed by self-restraint and austerity until the soul is liberated (moksha) [@src.jaini-1979-jaina-path-of-purification].
- **Anekantavada.** The doctrine of "many-sidedness" holds that reality has many aspects, so any single statement is true only from one standpoint; it is often called non-absolutism [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains; @src.long-2009-jainism-introduction].
- **Aparigraha (non-possession).** Detachment from possessions is the third pillar often cited alongside the other two [@src.long-2009-jainism-introduction].

Monks and nuns take five great vows: non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, celibacy and non-possession.
According to the canon, Parshva taught four vows and Mahavira added the fifth, celibacy; lay Jains take lesser versions of the same vows, so for them the fourth means chastity within marriage rather than celibacy [@src.jacobi-1884-jaina-sutras-part-1; @src.jaini-1979-jaina-path-of-purification].

## Texts, councils and the two traditions

The Svetambara ("white-clad") tradition holds that the canon, the Agamas, was kept orally and gathered in stages, with a council at Pataliputra, traditionally placed around 300 BCE, and a final redaction at Valabhi in the fifth century CE under Devarddhigani [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains].
The Digambara ("sky-clad") tradition holds that the old canon was lost and relies on other texts [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains; @src.jaini-1979-jaina-path-of-purification].
The dates of the early councils come from Jain tradition and are not independently attested; the written Agamas as we have them are later than the teaching they preserve [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains].
Hermann Jacobi's translations of the Acaranga, Kalpa and other canonical sutras, made in the 1880s, were an early step in the scholarly study of these texts [@src.jacobi-1884-jaina-sutras-part-1].

## How the two traditions divided

Svetambara tradition dates the split to the end of the first century CE; the Digambara tell a different story of how it came about.
Scholars regard the division as gradual, not as a single event: distinct groups, practices and images are visible over several centuries, and the two communities were clearly established by the middle of the first millennium CE [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains].
Evidence from the Kushan-period sculptures of Mathura, in the first and second centuries CE, shows Jain art and worship in a form already recognisable, with images of the Tirthankaras [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains; @src.cort-2010-framing-the-jina].
The two groups differ on points such as monastic nudity and whether women can attain liberation in that lifetime [@src.dundas-2002-the-jains; @src.jaini-1979-jaina-path-of-purification].
