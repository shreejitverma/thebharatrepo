## Two questions

What is the deepest reality behind the changing world? And what, beneath the roles, memories, and sensations that make up a life, is the self? The early Upanishads return to these questions through dialogues, images, and acts of close attention. Two Sanskrit words become central: *brahman* (ब्रह्मन्), the ultimate or sacred ground, and *ātman* (आत्मन्), the self. Several passages bring them together, but the Upanishads do not present one uniform philosophical system. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads]

The word *Vedānta* means “end of the Veda” and later names traditions that interpret the Upanishads as authoritative teaching. Those traditions developed systematic answers from a body of texts whose voices are varied. Reading the passages before the later conclusions helps show why the same sentence could support more than one account of reality and selfhood. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]

## Brahman: what cannot be left out

In the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad*, the sage Yājñavalkya uses the expression *neti neti*, “not this, not this,” when describing what exceeds ordinary predicates. This is not simply a denial that anything is real. It cautions that the ultimate cannot be captured by naming one visible or conceptual thing as the whole. The passage invites a disciplined refusal to confuse a description with what it describes. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 2.3.6]

Other passages describe Brahman affirmatively. The *Taittirīya Upaniṣad* calls it *satyaṃ jñānam anantaṃ brahma*: “Brahman is truth, knowledge, infinity.” In the teaching that follows, the student Bhṛgu investigates through contemplation and comes to understand Brahman through successive accounts, culminating in bliss. The text presents an inquiry, not just a definition to memorize. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads]

Later Vedānta often summarizes ultimate reality with the expression *sat-cit-ānanda* (“being, consciousness, bliss”). It is a useful later formula, but it should not be mistaken for the exact three-word phrase in the Taittirīya passage just cited. The difference matters: later schools systematized compact readings of older texts, and their terminology carries the history of interpretation with it. [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism; @src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads]

## Ātman: who is the one who knows?

In a dialogue with Maitreyī, Yājñavalkya asks what makes anything dear. He answers that things are dear in relation to the self, then urges her to hear, reflect on, and contemplate the self. The passage does not reduce the self to an individual’s preferences. It turns the apparent knower into the subject of inquiry: what is the self that makes experience and value possible? [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 2.4.5]

The *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* stages a related investigation through Indra and Virocana, who seek instruction from Prajāpati. Virocana accepts the reflected body as the self and leaves. Indra returns with questions, finding that the waking body, dream experience, and dreamless sleep each fail to provide an adequate account. The story tests proposed answers against what they can explain; it does not ask the reader to accept the first image as final. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 8.7-12]

## When self and ultimate are joined

One of the best-known teachings comes in the *Chāndogya*. Uddālaka teaches his son Śvetaketu with examples drawn from ordinary life: a seed too small to see, and salt dissolved in water. The salt is invisible but present throughout the water. Uddālaka uses the image to point toward a subtle essence underlying the world and tells Śvetaketu, *tat tvam asi* - “you are that.” The line occurs within a repeated teaching sequence, so it is not merely a slogan detached from its argument. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, ChU 6.8.7]

The *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* offers another striking formulation. It says that, in the beginning, Brahman knew only itself and declared *ahaṃ brahmāsmi* (“I am Brahman”), after which it became all. A related statement, *ayam ātmā brahma* (“this self is Brahman”), appears in the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*. These passages make identity between self and ultimate reality a powerful Upanishadic possibility, though their meaning and scope remain matters of interpretation. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, BU 1.4.10]

Later Advaita Vedānta traditions gather four such passages into the *mahāvākyas*, or “great sayings,” associating one with each Veda. This four-part arrangement is a later teaching convention. It helps explain Advaita’s central claim that the deepest self is not ultimately separate from Brahman, but it is not a list that the Upanishads themselves provide as a unified set. [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]

## A layered account of the person

The *Taittirīya Upaniṣad* describes a sequence of human dimensions associated with food, breath, mind, understanding, and bliss. Each is presented through the image of one self nested within another. Later Vedānta writers call these the five *kośas*, or sheaths, and use them to describe layers that can be mistaken for the deepest self. The text’s own progression is a contemplative teaching, while the familiar “sheath” framework reflects its subsequent interpretation. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, TU 2.1-5; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]

The short *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* approaches the self through waking, dreaming, and deep sleep, then speaks of a “fourth” (*turīya*) that is not an ordinary object of awareness. It associates the syllable *Oṃ* with these states and their sound-symbolism. This compressed account became important to later Advaita interpretation, but the text should be distinguished from the expanded systems built on it centuries afterward. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads, MaU 3-12; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]

## Later schools disagree

Advaita Vedānta reads the identity passages as teaching that Ātman and Brahman are ultimately one; apparent separation belongs to ignorance. Viśiṣṭādvaita, associated with Rāmānuja, understands selves as real and inseparable from Brahman while retaining meaningful distinction. Dvaita, associated with Madhva, argues for a lasting difference between the individual self and God. Each tradition claims continuity with the Upanishadic texts, but gives different weight to their identity and difference statements. [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]

Not every Indian school accepts the premise of a single Brahman. Sāṃkhya and Yoga describe many enduring *puruṣas* (conscious selves) distinct from material nature, while Buddhist traditions reject an eternal, independent self. These positions do not make the Upanishadic inquiry irrelevant; they show that the question “what is the self?” became a site of sustained disagreement across Indian philosophy. [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism; @src.collins-1982-selfless-persons]

The careful conclusion is modest. The Upanishads preserve influential claims that the self and ultimate reality are deeply connected, sometimes expressed as identity. The claim is neither a single uncontested doctrine shared by every Indian tradition nor a proposition whose later meanings can be read back into every early passage without argument. [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]
