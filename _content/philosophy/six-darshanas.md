Six schools of Indian philosophy are traditionally grouped together as the "orthodox" systems: Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa and Vedanta.
They disagree with one another about almost everything that matters: what exists, how we know, whether God exists, what a self is, and what liberation consists of [@src.king-1999-indian-philosophy; @src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].
What they share is a family resemblance and, in most cases, some relation to the Vedas.

## A later scheme, not an original one

The word darshana means "view" or "way of seeing".
The tidy list of six, and the division into astika (orthodox) and nastika (heterodox) schools, did not come from the schools themselves.
Andrew Nicholson has shown that medieval writers who surveyed Indian thought did not agree on a list of six.
Haribhadra's *Saddarshanasamuccaya*, traditionally dated to the 8th century CE, includes Buddhism and Jainism among its six, and leaves out Yoga and Vedanta.
The familiar set of six, treated as a unified "Hindu" philosophy with six branches, took shape across later centuries and was consolidated further in the modern period [@src.nicholson-2010-unifying-hinduism].

The orthodox/heterodox division is also less simple than it sounds.
It is usually explained by acceptance of the authority of the Veda, and Buddhists, Jains and the materialist Carvakas are counted as nastika [@src.king-1999-indian-philosophy].
But classical Samkhya does not build on Vedic authority, and it does not accept a creator god [@src.larson-1979-classical-samkhya].
It is best treated as a label of later tradition, not a precise test.

## The six schools

Tradition attributes each school to a founder.
In every case the founder is the author or reputed author of a foundational sutra, a compact work of aphorisms that the school's later commentators interpret.
The ideas in each school are usually older than its sutra text, and the dates of most of the sutras are debated [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy; @src.king-1999-indian-philosophy].

### Nyaya: logic and the sources of knowledge

The Nyaya Sutra is attributed to Gautama (Akshapada).
Nyaya is a realist system of epistemology and debate.
It holds that there are four means of valid knowledge (pramanas): perception, inference, comparison and reliable testimony.
It developed a theory of inference with a multi-step argument form and analysed fallacies in reasoning.
It also gave arguments for the existence of God and the self [@src.phillips-2012-epistemology-classical-india; @src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].

### Vaisheshika: categories and atoms

The Vaisheshika Sutra is attributed to Kanada.
It classifies reality into categories (padarthas) such as substance, quality and action, and holds that the physical world is composed of eternal atoms of earth, water, fire and air that combine into larger things.
This is an atomism arrived at by philosophical argument, not by experiment.
Nyaya and Vaisheshika came to be treated as a pair [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy; @src.king-1999-indian-philosophy].

### Samkhya: purusha and prakriti

Samkhya's tradition names Kapila as founder, but the earliest complete surviving text is the *Samkhya Karika* of Ishvarakrishna, which is usually dated to about the 4th or 5th century CE, though its ideas are older [@src.larson-1979-classical-samkhya].
It is a dualism of two ultimate principles: purusha, conscious witnesses that do nothing, and prakriti, the unconscious matter or nature that evolves into mind and body through three qualities, the gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas).
Liberation is the discriminating knowledge that purusha is separate from prakriti.
Gerald Larson reads classical Samkhya as an atheistic, rationalising system [@src.larson-1979-classical-samkhya].

### Yoga: discipline

The Yoga Sutras are attributed to Patanjali and set out a path of eight limbs, from ethical restraints and bodily posture to meditative absorption (samadhi).
The metaphysics is Samkhyan, with the addition of a special kind of being, Ishvara, as an object of meditation [@src.bryant-2009-yoga-sutras-patanjali].
The original page dated the work to "c. 200 CE".
That is one possibility among several: scholars have proposed dates from the last centuries BCE to the 5th century CE, and the tradition that the author is also the grammarian Patanjali is not accepted by all. Edwin Bryant's edition surveys the question [@src.bryant-2009-yoga-sutras-patanjali].
Modern postural yoga is a later development and is not described in the sutras.

### Mimamsa: ritual and the interpretation of the Veda

The Mimamsa Sutra is attributed to Jaimini.
Mimamsa (also called Purva Mimamsa, "prior inquiry") is the investigation of dharma, understood as what the Vedic injunctions command, above all sacrifice.
It developed rigorous rules for reading texts and the doctrine that the Veda is authorless (apaurusheya) and self-validating.
It is not interested in a creator god [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy; @src.king-1999-indian-philosophy].

### Vedanta: the Upanishads

Vedanta ("end of the Veda") is the interpretation of the Upanishads, together with the Brahma Sutra attributed to Badarayana and, in later tradition, the Bhagavad Gita.
It is not a single doctrine but a family of schools.
Advaita (non-dualism) is associated with Shankara, whom most scholars place in the 8th century CE: the self is identical with brahman and the world's plurality is not ultimately real [@src.mayeda-1992-thousand-teachings-sankara].
Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism) is associated with Ramanuja of the 11th and 12th centuries, and Dvaita (dualism) with Madhva of the 13th century [@src.king-1999-indian-philosophy; @src.ganeri-2017-oxford-handbook-indian-philosophy].
The earlier article on the Upanishads covers their own content.

## How we date the sutras

The original article gave "c. 600 to 200 BCE" for the formation of the sutras of all schools.
That is too early for the texts as we have them.
Most scholarship puts the surviving foundational sutras of Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa, Vedanta and Yoga somewhere between roughly the 2nd century BCE and the 5th century CE, with the Samkhya Karika at the later end of that range and with considerable disagreement about individual texts [@src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy; @src.king-1999-indian-philosophy; @src.larson-1979-classical-samkhya].
The schools' ideas and debates are older than their texts, and several of the sutras were revised by later hands, so a single date for each would be false precision.
Traditional accounts regard the founders as ancient sages, and this site treats those accounts as tradition [@src.nicholson-2010-unifying-hinduism].

## Debate between schools

These schools did not simply sit alongside each other.
They argued with one another and with Buddhists and Jains for centuries, and these exchanges sharpened their arguments about the nature of knowledge, the existence of a self, causation, and God [@src.ganeri-2017-oxford-handbook-indian-philosophy; @src.perrett-2016-introduction-indian-philosophy].
That debate is a better guide to Indian philosophy than the tidy list of six.
