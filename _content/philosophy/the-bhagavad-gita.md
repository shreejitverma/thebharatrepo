The Bhagavad Gita ("the Song of the Lord") is a Sanskrit poem of about 700 verses in eighteen chapters, and it sits inside the sixth book of the Mahabharata [@src.vanbuitenen-1981-bhagavadgita-mahabharata; @src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts].
It is among the most widely read and commented-on texts of Indian religious thought, and it has been read well beyond India since the late eighteenth century [@src.davis-2015-bhagavad-gita-biography].

## The story

The scene is a battlefield.
Two armies of one extended family, the Pandavas and the Kauravas, face each other at Kurukshetra.
The Pandava warrior Arjuna asks his charioteer Krishna to drive between the lines, sees teachers and cousins on the other side, and collapses in despair: he will not fight [@src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts].
The rest of the poem is Krishna's answer.
The whole exchange is reported by Sanjaya, the king's counsellor, to the blind king Dhritarashtra, so the Gita reaches us as a story within a story [@src.vanbuitenen-1981-bhagavadgita-mahabharata].

## What Krishna says

Krishna's reply is not one argument but several, and readers have long disagreed about how they fit together [@src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts].
The main strands are these.

- **The self is not killed in battle.** The embodied self is described as unborn and lasting, so grief over its death is misplaced (chapter 2) [@src.vanbuitenen-1981-bhagavadgita-mahabharata].
- **Action without clinging to its fruits.** Arjuna is told to do his duty (dharma) as a warrior but to give up attachment to success or failure. This is often called karma yoga [@src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts].
- **Knowledge.** The poem draws on ideas about the self and the world that are also found in the Upanishads, which scholars date to the first millennium BCE [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
- **Devotion.** Krishna presents himself as the supreme lord, shows Arjuna a vision of his cosmic form (chapter 11), and says that loving devotion (bhakti) to him is the surest path (chapter 12) [@src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts].

The Gita is therefore a meeting point of older sacrificial duty, Upanishadic knowledge and personal devotion to one god, which is one reason it was later read so differently by different schools [@src.davis-2015-bhagavad-gita-biography].

## How we know when it was composed

No manuscript or inscription dates the Gita.
Scholars place it by working out the history of the Mahabharata, which grew over several centuries, with the usual estimate running from roughly the fourth century BCE to the fourth century CE [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
Within that span most specialists put the Gita at about 200 BCE to 200 CE, though the range is a reasoned estimate and not a measurement [@src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts; @src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].

A second long-running question is whether the Gita was composed for the epic or inserted later.
Some earlier scholars proposed that it was built up in layers from an older core.
Malinar argues instead that it is a coherent composition whose teaching responds to the epic's central problem, the moral cost of a war between kinsmen and the duties of a king [@src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts].
Other scholars still accept that the text as we have it contains older and younger material, so this remains a live debate.

## The traditional dating

Tradition holds that Krishna spoke these words at the start of the great war, and places that war at the turn from the Dvapara to the Kali age, which the usual reckoning puts at 3102 BCE [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].
This is the framework of the traditional calendar and of devotional history.
It is not supported by archaeological evidence for a single war on that date, and it is treated here as tradition, a separate question from the composition of the text [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics; @src.davis-2015-bhagavad-gita-biography].
Attempts to fix the war astronomically from epic references give widely different results and have not won general acceptance among historians [@src.brockington-1998-sanskrit-epics].

## A text that kept being reread

The Gita was not always the centre of attention it is today.
It was commented on by the great Vedanta teachers, including Shankara, Ramanuja and Madhva, each drawing out a different reading: knowledge, devotion or a qualified union of the two [@src.davis-2015-bhagavad-gita-biography].
Its first English translation, by Charles Wilkins, appeared in 1785, and in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries it became a touchstone for reformers, nationalists and philosophers, who again read it in conflicting ways, for example on whether it justifies or limits violence [@src.davis-2015-bhagavad-gita-biography].
Reading it well means keeping two things in view: what the text most plausibly meant when it was composed, and the long history of how communities have used it [@src.davis-2015-bhagavad-gita-biography; @src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts].
