## A map of human concerns

Imagine trying to make a life that is ethical, materially secure, pleasurable, and spiritually free. Classical Sanskrit texts discuss all of these concerns, often with the words *dharma* (धर्म), *artha* (अर्थ), *kāma* (काम), and *mokṣa* (मोक्ष). A familiar later framework calls these the four *puruṣārthas*, or aims associated with human life. It is a useful map, as long as we remember that the map itself developed over time. [@src.sharma-2001-classical-hindu-thought; @src.davis-2004-purusharthas]

The first three are often grouped as the *trivarga*, the “threefold set”: dharma, artha, and kāma. The fourth, mokṣa, concerns release from the conditions that bind a person to ordinary existence and rebirth. One influential account describes the four as a gradual integration of the pursuits of social life with the renunciant goal of liberation. A significant scholarly counterargument asks whether early authors understood the first three as “goals” at all, rather than as broad domains of activity. [@src.sharma-2001-classical-hindu-thought; @src.davis-2004-purusharthas]

## Dharma: conduct, duty, and order

*Dharma* has no single English equivalent. Depending on its context, it can refer to law, duty, right conduct, custom, or an order that sustains social and moral life. In the *Mānava-Dharmaśāstra*, duties are discussed in relation to social role and life-stage, alongside norms that the text presents as applying more generally. These are prescriptions in a historical law text, not a neutral description of how every person lived. [@src.hacker-2006-dharma-in-hinduism; @src.olivelle-2005-manus-code-of-law]

The breadth of the word is a feature rather than a translation problem to be solved with one English substitute. Dharma can name what is owed in a particular role and can also frame questions about conduct more broadly. Paul Hacker’s study traces this range in Hindu usage; the specific meaning in a sentence needs to be established from that sentence’s genre and context. [@src.hacker-2006-dharma-in-hinduism]

## Artha: means and material security

*Artha* can mean purpose, advantage, or material means; in political and ethical discussions it includes livelihood, property, prosperity, and state power. The *Arthaśāstra*, a treatise on statecraft attributed to Kauṭilya, treats material security as a necessary concern of government. Manu and Kauṭilya do not rank the human pursuits in exactly the same way: the law text discusses dharma, artha, and kāma together, while the political treatise gives artha special weight as the basis that supports the others. [@src.olivelle-2013-king-governance-law; @src.olivelle-2005-manus-code-of-law]

This makes artha more than greed or private wealth. It includes the practical conditions under which households and political communities function. Nor do the texts make it an unlimited good: its pursuit is placed within arguments about lawful action and the responsibilities of rulers and subjects. The tension between ethical constraint and the needs of material life is part of the framework, not an accidental footnote. [@src.olivelle-2013-king-governance-law; @src.davis-2004-purusharthas]

## Kāma: desire and enjoyment

*Kāma* means desire and can refer to pleasure, affection, aesthetic enjoyment, and erotic life. The *Kāmasūtra* treats it as a field of learned practice, while the broader classical scheme places it alongside social duty and material means. The point is not that every desire is automatically good; the texts ask how desire is pursued and how it relates to other concerns. [@src.sharma-2001-classical-hindu-thought; @src.davis-2004-purusharthas]

The word also appears in a cosmic setting in the *Ṛgveda*’s creation hymn, where desire arises in the beginning as an impulse associated with mind. That poetic use should not be collapsed into the later ethical category: one word can operate differently across genres and periods. The later system’s attention to pleasure makes space for enjoyment as part of human life, while debates about dharma ask how it should be guided. [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda, RV 10.129.4; @src.sharma-2001-classical-hindu-thought]

## Mokṣa: release

*Mokṣa* is release or liberation, though traditions differ over what is released from and what liberation consists in. In Vedānta, accounts may connect liberation with knowledge of Brahman; Sāṃkhya and Yoga describe freedom as the isolation of *puruṣa*, conscious self, from material nature. Buddhist traditions speak of *nirvāṇa* and reject an eternal self, while Jain traditions describe liberation of the soul from karmic bondage. These terms belong to distinct doctrines and should not be treated as interchangeable translations. [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism; @src.collins-1982-selfless-persons; @src.dundas-2003-the-jains]

Mokṣa changes the shape of the whole scheme. Dharma, artha, and kāma can describe forms of purposeful life in society; liberation raises the possibility that a person’s deepest good lies beyond ordinary social and worldly aims. Some texts and traditions try to coordinate household life and renunciation, while others preserve the tension between participation and withdrawal. [@src.sharma-2001-classical-hindu-thought; @src.bronkhorst-2007-greater-magadha]

## One hierarchy, or a set of domains?

Modern diagrams often show dharma at the base, artha and kāma in the middle, and mokṣa at the top. This can be a helpful teaching image, but it makes the relationship look more settled than the sources show. Manu considers how the three worldly pursuits should be weighed and favors their coordinated pursuit; Kauṭilya gives material security priority. Other religious and philosophical texts place liberation above worldly aims, or ask how liberation can be pursued without abandoning action. [@src.olivelle-2005-manus-code-of-law; @src.olivelle-2013-king-governance-law; @src.malinar-2007-bhagavadgita-doctrines-contexts]

The historical interpretation of *puruṣārtha* itself is contested. Arvind Sharma describes the fourfold framework as the gradual acceptance of all four pursuits as legitimate human ends. Donald R. Davis Jr. challenges the idea that the early *trivarga* straightforwardly meant “three goals of human life”: he notes that the expression *puruṣārtha* is rare in early literature in that sense and reads the three as major domains of beneficial human activity. These approaches need not erase the textual evidence for the fourfold scheme; they caution against projecting its familiar modern form unchanged into every earlier text. [@src.sharma-2001-classical-hindu-thought; @src.davis-2004-purusharthas]

## A framework, not a formula

The four terms are most useful when they keep a reader’s attention on the questions that texts actually ask. What does a person owe others? What material conditions make life possible? Which forms of pleasure are worth seeking? Is release from ordinary existence possible, and what would it mean? Different authors answer differently, and the fourfold list does not make those disagreements disappear. [@src.sharma-2001-classical-hindu-thought; @src.davis-2004-purusharthas]

The framework therefore offers neither a single ladder that everyone must climb nor a timeless checklist with one fixed ranking. It is a historically layered way of discussing meaningful life, responsibility, prosperity, pleasure, and liberation. Reading its terms in their texts preserves both their practical force and the real debate over how they belong together. [@src.davis-2004-purusharthas; @src.olivelle-2005-manus-code-of-law; @src.olivelle-2013-king-governance-law]
