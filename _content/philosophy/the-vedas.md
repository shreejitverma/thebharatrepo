The Vedas are the earliest surviving literature of South Asia and the foundation text of the Vedic and later Brahmanical traditions.
They were composed in Vedic Sanskrit, an older form of the language that Panini later described, and for most of their history they were not written down at all [@src.gonda-1975-vedic-literature; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].

## Four collections and four layers

The word veda means "knowledge".
There are four Vedas, each preserved as a collection of mantras called a Samhita [@src.gonda-1975-vedic-literature].

- The **Rigveda** is a collection of 1,028 hymns arranged in ten books, mostly praising gods such as Agni, Indra and Soma. Books 2 to 7 are the "family books", each attributed to a particular lineage of poets; Books 1 and 10 are generally taken to be later additions [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].
- The **Samaveda** reworks verses, mostly from the Rigveda, into melodies for singing at ritual.
- The **Yajurveda** holds the prose and verse formulas spoken by the priest who performs the physical work of sacrifice. It survives in a "black" and a "white" (Shukla) tradition [@src.keith-1914-veda-black-yajus-school; @src.gonda-1975-vedic-literature].
- The **Atharvaveda** contains charms, healing spells and speculative hymns, and was accepted into the Vedic canon somewhat later than the other three [@src.gonda-1975-vedic-literature].

Around each Samhita grew further layers of text.
The **Brahmanas** are prose manuals explaining what the ritual acts mean and how to perform them [@src.keith-1920-rigveda-brahmanas; @src.gonda-1980-vedic-ritual].
The **Aranyakas** ("forest texts") and the **Upanishads** carry the reflection further, turning from the outer sacrifice to questions about the self and the cosmos [@src.olivelle-1998-early-upanisads].
Because the Upanishads come at the end of this sequence they are also called Vedanta, "the end of the Veda".

## The Vedic world

The hymns do not describe a city civilisation.
They describe a pastoral and ritual society of clans led by chieftains and priests, with cattle, horses and chariots central to its imagery, and with a ritual religion built around fire offerings and the pressing of the soma plant [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda; @src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
Later Vedic texts show the centre of gravity shifting east to the Kuru and Panchala lands of the upper Ganga plain, where the Samhitas were collected and the elaborate "shrauta" rituals took shape [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
The Shatapatha Brahmana, for instance, describes the building of the great fire altar in detail, and Frits Staal's fieldwork recorded this ritual still being performed in Kerala in 1975 [@src.eggeling-1882-shatapatha-brahmana; @src.staal-1983-agni-fire-altar].

## How the texts were kept

Memorising the Vedas was an exact discipline, not loose recitation.
Pupils learned each text by ear from a teacher, with prescribed accents, and used multiple recitation patterns, including word-by-word, and interlocking "braided" forms that re-weave the words of a verse in set orders [@src.staal-2008-discovering-the-vedas].
These patterns work like error-correcting checksums: any slip breaks the pattern.
Staal argued that the recitation of mantras was valued for its sound and form rather than for conveying a meaning, which helps explain the care taken over preservation [@src.staal-2008-discovering-the-vedas].
UNESCO proclaimed the tradition of Vedic chanting a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2003 [@src.unesco-2003-tradition-vedic-chanting].

One consequence is that the surviving manuscripts, which are medieval, tell us little about the age of the texts.
What the recitation tradition preserves is a text that scholars regard as remarkably stable, but its antiquity has to be inferred from other evidence.

## How we date the Vedas

No inscription, coin or excavated document carries a Vedic text, so every date is an inference.
The mainstream scholarly position (Michael Witzel, Stephanie Jamison and Joel Brereton, and others) dates the Rigveda hymns to roughly 1500 to 1000 BCE and places the Brahmanas and earliest Upanishads in the following centuries, before the rise of Buddhism [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru; @src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].
The reasons are:

- **Language.** Rigvedic Sanskrit is very close to the oldest Iranian (Avestan), and the Vedic corpus shows steady grammatical change from Rigveda to Brahmana to Sutra [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
- **Material culture.** The Rigveda has no clear mention of iron, which appears in later Vedic texts, and the geography moves from the northwest to the Ganga plain [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
- **External evidence.** A treaty of about 1380 BCE between the Hittite king Suppiluliuma I and Shattiwaza of Mitanni in Syria calls on Mitra, Varuna, Indra and the Nasatyas as witnesses. Those are Vedic god-names, which shows Indo-Aryan religious vocabulary was in use in the Near East by then [@src.thieme-1960-aryan-gods-mitanni-treaties; @src.beckman-1999-hittite-diplomatic-texts].

This is a reasoned estimate with a wide margin, not a measurement.

## Where scholars disagree

The origin of the Vedic people is contested, and so is the date of the Rigveda.

- **Migration view.** Most historical linguists, and many archaeologists, hold that Indo-Aryan speakers entered South Asia from Central Asia during the second millennium BCE, bringing the language and the earliest hymns. Recent ancient-DNA studies report steppe-related ancestry reaching South Asia in the second millennium BCE [@src.narasimhan-2019-formation-human-populations], and a genome from the Indus city of Rakhigarhi shows none [@src.shinde-2019-ancient-harappan-genome]. Proponents read these results as consistent with a later arrival of the Vedic language. [@src.kuzmina-2007-origin-indo-iranians; @src.anthony-2007-horse-wheel-language]
- **Indigenist view.** Scholars such as B. B. Lal and Shrikant Talageri hold that Vedic culture is indigenous to the northwest and older than the mainstream dating, and that the Rigvedic Sarasvati is the Ghaggar-Hakra river of the Harappan period [@src.lal-2002-saraswati-flows-on; @src.talageri-2000-rigveda-historical-analysis]. Edwin Bryant's survey sets out both sides with their evidence [@src.bryant-2001-quest-origins-vedic-culture].
- **Tradition.** The sampradaya regards the Vedas as eternal and not composed by any person, and Puranic chronology puts Vyasa's arrangement of the four Vedas near 3102 BCE [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism]. This is a statement of faith and tradition, and is graded as such on this site.

The word "Aryan" in the Rigveda (arya) is a self-description of a cultural and ritual community. Modern scholarship does not use it as a racial category, and the Vedas contain no such theory [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].

## What the Vedas are not

It is common to call the Vedas "the oldest scriptures of Hinduism".
That is true only with qualification: they are the earliest surviving texts, but the Vedic religion of sacrifice differs in important ways from later Hinduism, and the gods, practices and ideas of later Puranic and devotional traditions are largely absent from the Samhitas [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
The later traditions nevertheless claim the Vedas as their authority, and the Upanishads, the Dharmashastras and the six philosophical schools all define their position in relation to them.
