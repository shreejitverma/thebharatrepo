Reconstructing a damaged nose or ear with skin taken from elsewhere on the face is one of the oldest operations in plastic surgery.
The earliest known detailed description of such an operation in Sanskrit is in the Sushruta Samhita.
That is a claim about a text.
It is not the same as saying that a named surgeon performed these operations on a known date, and the difference matters.

## The text and its layers

The Sushruta Samhita is a compendium of surgery and medicine.
Scholars regard it as a compilation, built up in layers by several hands, rather than the work of a single physician [@src.wujastyk-2003-roots-of-ayurveda; @src.wujastyk-2011-indian-medicine-obo].
Its oldest strata may be pre-Common Era, its final section is a later addition, and the version that survives was reworked and commented on in the first millennium CE and afterwards.
The person Sushruta, who tradition makes a student of the healer-king Divodasa, cannot be dated from the evidence, so "Sushruta performed the first plastic surgery" is not a statement history can back.
What can be said is that the compilation contains these descriptions and is among the early surviving Indian medical texts [@src.wujastyk-2011-indian-medicine-obo].

## What the passage describes

The best-known passage appears in the early, general part of the work (*Sutrasthana*), in the chapter on piercing and repairing the ear-lobes.
In Kunja Lal Bhishagratna's 1907 English translation, the instructions for a severed nose run as follows [@src.bhishagratna-1907-sushruta-samhita-vol1, Chapter XVI]:

- A leaf of a creeper, large enough to cover the whole missing part, is first gathered, to serve as a template.
- A patch of "living flesh" of the same size is cut from the region of the cheek and, after the edges are scarified, is swiftly adhered to the severed nose.
- The physician then bandages the part carefully, checks that it has taken, and inserts two small pipes into the nostrils, to help the patient breathe and to stop the new flesh from sagging.
- Finally the part is dusted with powders.

The same chapter describes the repair of torn ear-lobes by taking a flap of flesh from the cheek, with named techniques for lobes of different lengths [@src.bhishagratna-1907-sushruta-samhita-vol1, Chapter XVI].

## How far to take it

Several cautions apply.

- This is a procedure described in a manual, and we have no contemporary account of a named patient being operated on. Textbook descriptions and clinical practice are not the same thing.
- The wording is terse. Many modern surgeons read the passage as describing a flap that stays attached to the cheek by a stalk that carries blood supply, but the translated text does not spell this out, and the interpretation rests on later commentary and on surgical reasoning [@src.wujastyk-2003-roots-of-ayurveda].
- The translation cited here is more than a century old, and in it the nose passage sits in the middle of the ear-lobe material; anyone relying on a detail should check a modern critical edition.

## Other traditions of nose reconstruction

Nasal reconstruction was developed in more than one place.
In sixteenth-century Italy, Gaspare Tagliacozzi described a different method, based on a flap from the upper arm, and published it in print [@src.miller-1985-tagliacozzi-flap].
How, and whether, the Indian and European traditions are connected is a separate historical question, and this article does not settle it.

## Why "father of plastic surgery" is the wrong frame

The label is popular, and Sushruta's name is well attached to the Samhita.
But "father of" suggests a single inventor at a datable moment, and neither of those is available to us.
A fair statement is that the Sushruta Samhita preserves an early, detailed, and surprisingly specific account of reconstructive technique, and that this is a real achievement of South Asian medical writing.
