Ayurveda, "knowledge of long life", is the name given to the classical medicine of South Asia.
It is a learned tradition with its own texts, theory of the body, diagnostics, drug-lore and surgery.
It is still practised today, but this article is about its history and how we know it.

## Before the classical texts

Medical ideas are older than the texts that systematise them.
The Atharvaveda, one of the four Vedic collections, contains charms and spells against fever, wounds and other ailments, together with plant lore [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
Scholars usually date the Atharvaveda to the early first millennium BCE or the late second, which is why popular summaries speak of Ayurveda being "3,000 years old".
That is a fair description of Vedic healing; it is not a fair description of Ayurveda as a systematic medicine, whose surviving classical texts are considerably later [@src.wujastyk-2003-roots-of-ayurveda].
Early Buddhist and other literature also records physicians and medical practice, so the classical works draw on a wider medical culture [@src.wujastyk-2011-indian-medicine-obo].

## The classical compendia

Three works are usually called the "great triad" of Ayurveda.

### Charaka Samhita

The Caraka Samhita is the foundational work on internal medicine.
It was not written by one person at one time.
Its tradition names a teacher Agnivesha, a redactor Charaka, and a later reviser, Dridhabala, who is said to have completed the sections that had been lost.
The English-language scholarship treats the text as layered, with the work of Charaka often placed around the early centuries CE and the final recension later [@src.wujastyk-2003-roots-of-ayurveda; @src.hoernle-1909-caraka-samhita-bower-manuscript].
Early twentieth-century philologist Rudolf Hoernle used an early medical manuscript found in Central Asia, the Bower Manuscript, to argue about how the Caraka was assembled [@src.hoernle-1909-caraka-samhita-bower-manuscript].

### Sushruta Samhita

The Sushruta Samhita concentrates on surgery (*shalya-tantra*) and describes instruments, operations and training.
It is also layered.
Its core is thought by many scholars to be older than its present form, the closing section (*Uttaratantra*) is a later addition, and tradition credits an author named Nagarjuna with a revision (an attribution that need not point to the famous philosopher of that name) [@src.wujastyk-2003-roots-of-ayurveda].
Because the layers are unresolved, the often-repeated date "c. 600 BCE" for "Sushruta" is a guess rather than a result, and this article does not use it.

### Vagbhata

Vagbhata's Ashtangahridaya and its companion work are later syntheses of the earlier material, usually dated to roughly the seventh century CE [@src.wujastyk-2003-roots-of-ayurveda; @src.wujastyk-2011-indian-medicine-obo].
They became the standard textbooks in many regions, and later commentators built on them.

## Core ideas

Ayurvedic theory describes the body in terms of three regulating principles, the *doshas*: *vata*, *pitta* and *kapha*.
They are often glossed as "wind", "bile" and "phlegm", or translated as "humours", though that term can mislead.
Health is understood as a dynamic balance of the doshas, and disease as disturbance of it, which is treated through diet, regimen, drugs and, where needed, surgery [@src.wujastyk-2003-roots-of-ayurveda].
The *panchamahabhuta*, the five great elements (earth, water, fire, air and space), provide the underlying vocabulary of both body and cosmos.
These are conceptual frameworks of a pre-modern medicine, to be read as such; they are not the categories of modern biomedicine.

## Medicine and its setting

The Charaka and Sushruta compendia describe a physician's ethics, the conditions of a patient's household, and relations with royal and religious authority.
Dominik Wujastyk has discussed how medical writers positioned their discipline within wider Brahminical and Buddhist ideas of duty and learning [@src.wujastyk-2004-medicine-and-dharma].

## A note on dates

Three points keep the chronology honest.

- The Vedic healing material is much older than the classical texts.
- The Charaka and Sushruta compendia were built up over centuries, so a single date misleads; their received forms belong to the first millennium CE.
- Tradition ascribes Ayurveda to divine origin, taught from the gods through sages to physicians. That is a traditional account, and it is not dated history [@src.wujastyk-2003-roots-of-ayurveda].
