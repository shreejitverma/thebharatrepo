Our number system writes any quantity with ten digits, and the position of a digit gives its value: in 305 the 3 means three hundreds because two places lie to its right.
Such a system only works if there is a way to mark an empty place, which is what a zero symbol does.
The system we use today comes to us from India through the Islamic world.
The question this article takes up is how much more than that can be said honestly, because the topic attracts the words "invented" and "first" far more often than the evidence allows.

## What was new, and what was not

Place-value notation with a marker for empty places was not unique to India.
Babylonian scribes wrote numbers in base 60 with place value and eventually used a sign for an empty place, and the Maya used a base-20 system with a zero sign [@src.joseph-2011-crest-of-the-peacock].
Both developed independently of India, so the claim "India invented the idea of a placeholder" cannot stand [@src.joseph-2011-crest-of-the-peacock].

What the Indian evidence shows is a different and still remarkable combination.
In Sanskrit texts from the middle of the first millennium CE, numbers were written with ten-based place value, and zero (shunya, "empty") became a full member of arithmetic rather than just a spacer [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
Plofker's survey describes Sanskrit number words used with place value in verse, so that a figure can be spoken as a list of words for 1, 2, 3 and so on, with shunya for the empty places [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
Aryabhata's *Aryabhatiya* (499 CE, by its own date) states a rule that each place is ten times the one before [@src.clark-1930-aryabhatiya; @src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
Plofker also discusses the Jain cosmological work *Lokavibhaga*, whose Sanskrit version is dated 458 CE by its colophon, as an early text using place-value words; how much of its wording goes back beyond that version is a matter of scholarly discussion [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].

## Zero as a number

Placing a zero in a column and treating zero as something you can add, subtract and multiply are different steps.
The earliest surviving text that states rules for arithmetic with zero is Brahmagupta's *Brahmasphutasiddhanta*, composed in 628 CE [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
Brahmagupta explains how zero behaves in sums, differences and products, along with negative numbers as "debts" [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
His treatment of division by zero is not correct by modern standards, and later authors such as Bhaskara II discuss it differently [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
Describing Brahmagupta's as the "earliest surviving" text is the accurate wording; "first ever" cannot be shown, since earlier works are lost [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].

## The oldest zeros we can date

Written zeros are best dated when they appear in an inscription with a date.
Two stand out.
A stele from Sambor in Cambodia, called K-127, carries a date in the Shaka era, usually read as 605 and so 683 CE, with a dot used as zero [@src.aczel-2024-khmer-stele-k127].
A 9th-century inscription on a temple wall at Gwalior in Madhya Pradesh was long considered the oldest recorded example of a zero used as a placeholder in India [@src.bodleian-2017-bakhshali-carbon-dating].
So the earliest securely dated written zero is the Cambodian one, from the Indianised world of Southeast Asia, and the Indian inscription is about two centuries later [@src.aczel-2024-khmer-stele-k127; @src.bodleian-2017-bakhshali-carbon-dating].
Their dates are firm; what they cannot show is when zero first appeared, because a symbol must exist before it is carved.

## The Bakhshali manuscript

The Bakhshali manuscript is the main source of the dispute.
It is a set of birch-bark leaves, found in 1881 in the village of Bakhshali near Peshawar, in what is now Pakistan, and given to the Bodleian Library in 1902 [@src.bodleian-2017-bakhshali-carbon-dating].
It contains arithmetical and algebraic rules and worked problems, and its numbers use a dot as a placeholder, with hundreds of zeros in all [@src.bodleian-2017-bakhshali-carbon-dating].

Before 2017 the most authoritative study, by Takao Hayashi, dated it on palaeography, content and style to between the 8th and 12th centuries [@src.bodleian-2017-bakhshali-carbon-dating].
In September 2017 the Bodleian Libraries announced the first radiocarbon dating of the manuscript.
It found that the leaves come from at least three different periods, with the earliest as early as the 3rd or 4th century, and presented this as the world's oldest recorded origin of the zero symbol [@src.bodleian-2017-bakhshali-carbon-dating].

That reading was challenged within weeks by a group of historians of Indian mathematics: Dominik Wujastyk, Takao Hayashi, Agathe Keller, Clemency Montelle and Kim Plofker.
They argue that the earliest dated folio is unlikely to be the date of the whole text, and that the latest dateable folio is logically the date of the scribal activity, which fits the earlier estimates [@src.plofker-2017-bakhshali-radiocarbon].
They also argue that the manuscript uses written zeros as arithmetical operators, meaning as numbers in their own right, and not just as placeholders, which is another point of disagreement with the Bodleian announcement [@src.plofker-2017-bakhshali-radiocarbon].
They regret that the results were announced by press release, without peer review [@src.plofker-2017-bakhshali-radiocarbon].

A careful summary is therefore:
the Bakhshali leaves contain some material that is old, but how and when the text as a whole was composed and copied is unsettled, and the manuscript cannot yet serve as a firm date for the origin of zero.

## From India to the world

The Indian numerals later spread west and east.
Arabic-language writers adopted them, and through Latin translations and later treatises they entered European use; Plofker and Joseph both trace this transmission [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india; @src.joseph-2011-crest-of-the-peacock].

## How we know

For the earliest centuries we depend on a few datable inscriptions, a handful of verse texts whose dates rest on their own statements and on quotation, and one manuscript with a disputed date.
Where those disagree, this article reports the disagreement.
