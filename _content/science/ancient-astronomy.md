Astronomy in India (jyotisha, "science of the luminaries") began as a practical servant of ritual and grew into a mathematical science with its own models, tables and instruments.
It also took in ideas from outside India, and it contained arguments among its own authors.
This article follows the evidence in order, and corrects some popular claims.

## Timekeeping for ritual

Vedic ritual had to be performed on the right day of the lunar month, at the right season, and under the right nakshatra (lunar mansion), which pushed priests to track the Moon and Sun [@src.pingree-1981-jyotihsastra].
The earliest surviving Indian astronomical text is the *Vedanga Jyotisha*, attributed to Lagadha, which describes a five-year cycle of 1,830 days and 62 lunar months, and a daylight length that varies linearly between solstices [@src.pingree-1981-jyotihsastra; @src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].

Its date is disputed.
The text puts the winter solstice at the start of the nakshatra Dhanishtha, which on a literal reading points to the 14th to 12th century BCE.
Pingree and Plofker, however, treat the surviving verses as a later redaction, in the 5th or 4th century BCE, which may preserve an older tradition [@src.pingree-1981-jyotihsastra; @src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
The older article gave "c. 1400 BCE" without that qualification.

## Foreign contact and the Siddhantas

Indian astronomy did not develop in isolation.
David Pingree argued that Babylonian methods reached India in the last centuries BCE or early centuries CE, and that Greek astronomy and astrology followed, including through Sanskrit works such as the *Yavanajataka* [@src.pingree-1963-astronomy-astrology-india-iran; @src.pingree-1981-jyotihsastra].
Plofker's survey presents the same picture of exchange, and an Indian tradition that reworked what it took in [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
From about the 5th century CE, astronomy was set out in the Siddhantas, verse treatises that give planetary models and the rules to compute positions, eclipses and calendars [@src.sarma-2008-astronomy-in-india; @src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].

## Aryabhata: a rotating Earth, not a heliocentric one

Aryabhata wrote the *Aryabhatiya* in 499 CE [@src.clark-1930-aryabhatiya].
He holds that the apparent daily motion of the stars comes from the Earth turning on its axis, and he compares it to a person in a boat who sees stationary things moving backwards [@src.clark-1930-aryabhatiya; @src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
That is a claim of rotation, and the old article's headline "Heliocentrism" was wrong.
His planetary models are geocentric, with the planets moving on circles around the Earth, and the Sun and Moon are among those bodies [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
Later Indian authors disputed the rotation: critics argued that a turning Earth would leave birds and clouds behind [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].

Aryabhata also explains eclipses as geometry.
A lunar eclipse is the Moon entering the Earth's shadow, and a solar eclipse is the Moon covering the Sun [@src.clark-1930-aryabhatiya].
The older article said this replaced "demons (Rahu/Ketu)", but the history is less tidy: the older mythic view persisted in religious contexts, and Brahmagupta, a century later, criticised Aryabhata's account and defended the Rahu tradition, even though calculation in Siddhantic texts treats eclipses as a matter of shadows [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].

## Brahmagupta and gravity

Brahmagupta's *Brahmasphutasiddhanta* (628 CE) says that heavy bodies fall to the Earth because it is the nature of the Earth to attract them, as it is the nature of water to flow [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
That is an early statement of attraction toward the Earth.
It is not a law of gravitation: it says nothing about attraction between other bodies, and no formula follows from it [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
"Recognised gravity as an attractive force" therefore overstates the case.

## Kerala and a partly Sun-centred model

The Kerala tradition refined planetary theory with observation.
Nilakantha Somayaji's *Tantrasamgraha* (1501) revised the models of the inner planets so that Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn move around the Sun, which itself moves around the Earth [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
That is a geo-heliocentric arrangement of the kind Tycho Brahe later proposed.
It is not the Sun-centred system of Copernicus, and it belongs to the 16th century, not to Aryabhata's time [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].

## The Jantar Mantar observatories

Sawai Jai Singh II of Jaipur built five observatories, at Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi and Mathura, starting with Delhi in 1724 and continuing into the 1730s [@src.sharma-1987-jai-singh-astronomical-efforts].
They use large masonry instruments to measure time and the positions of the Sun, Moon and stars [@src.sharma-1987-jai-singh-astronomical-efforts].
The old article mentioned only Jaipur and Delhi, and gave 1724 to 1735 as a single span, which compresses a longer building programme.
These are late instruments, built long after the Siddhantic period, and they belong to a living tradition that also drew on Islamic and European astronomical tables [@src.sharma-1987-jai-singh-astronomical-efforts].

## How we know

Almost everything above comes from Sanskrit verse texts, so each date depends on their own statements, on quotation by later authors, and on computing back from the astronomical data they contain [@src.pingree-1981-jyotihsastra; @src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
Where scholars disagree, as over the date of the *Vedanga Jyotisha*, this article gives both positions.
