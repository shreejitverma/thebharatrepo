"Indian cuisine" is a convenient phrase for something that is really a family of regional cooking traditions. K. T. Achaya, whose *Indian Food: A Historical Companion* is the standard survey, treats the food of the subcontinent as the product of local crops, religious dietary rules, the movement of plants and peoples, and the tastes of courts and households [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food]. This article follows that approach.

## Early evidence

The oldest direct evidence of how people in the Indus region cooked is archaeological. Arunima Kashyap and Steven Weber extracted starch grains from pottery, stone tools and human dental calculus at Farmana, a Mature Harappan site in Haryana. They reported starch grains that they identified as resembling ginger and turmeric on the inside of a cooking pot, together with grains resembling aubergine, alongside wheat, barley, millets and pulses [@src.kashyap-2010-farmana-starch]. This is evidence that spices were used in cooking in the third millennium BCE. It is a study of one site, and starch identification is a probabilistic method, so the identifications are best treated as strong but not final [@src.kashyap-2010-farmana-starch].

Popular phrases such as "the world's oldest curry" come from press coverage rather than from the scientific paper, and should be read as shorthand.

## Crops that made regional cuisines

Cereals divide the subcontinent into broad food zones. Wheat and barley are old crops of the north-west, rice is prominent in the east and south, and millets such as sorghum and several small millets are important in drier regions [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food]. Pulses supply protein across the country, and religious traditions that discourage meat, such as Jain and many Hindu communities, have long shaped vegetarian cooking [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food].

A regional sketch, with the caveat that every region contains many sub-regions:

- **North and north-west:** wheat breads (roti, naan, paratha) and dishes cooked in the clay tandoor. Lizzie Collingham links much of the rich meat-and-gravy cooking of the north to the Persianate cuisine of the Mughal courts [@src.collingham-2006-curry].
- **South:** rice, lentils, coconut, tamarind and curry leaves, with fermented rice-and-lentil batters for dosa and idli [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food].
- **East:** fish and rice are central in Bengal and Odisha, and mustard oil is widely used [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food].
- **West:** Gujarati vegetarian meals, Maharashtrian dishes and the Goan tradition of pork and seafood cooking, which carries a strong Portuguese imprint [@src.collingham-2006-curry].

These are generalisations. They are not a substitute for knowing that a Kerala fish curry, a Kashmiri wazwan feast and a Rajasthani meal of millet bread and lentils are very different things.

## Spices and the trade

The subcontinent has long been a major source of spices. Black pepper is native to the Western Ghats and the Malabar coast, and Indian pepper reached markets far beyond India in antiquity [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food; @src.collingham-2006-curry]. Cardamom also grows in the southern hills [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food].

A common misconception is that cinnamon is an Indian spice. True cinnamon (*Cinnamomum verum*) is native to Sri Lanka, and other cinnamon-like barks such as cassia come from elsewhere in Asia. They were traded through Indian ports, but India was not their source [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food]. India was a centre of the spice trade, and of the Indian Ocean trade more broadly, but the claim that it was "the" centre needs qualifying: Indian ports redistributed goods from Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia and beyond, as well as exporting local produce [@src.collingham-2006-curry].

## What changed after 1500

The arrival of Europeans in the sixteenth century brought American crops into Indian kitchens. Chilli peppers, potatoes and tomatoes all came from the Americas, carried across the world after 1492 [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food; @src.collingham-2006-curry]. Before chillies, heat in Indian food came mainly from black pepper and long pepper. Chilli was adopted quickly and is now so closely associated with Indian food that it surprises many people to learn it is a relatively recent arrival.

It is sometimes said that the Portuguese introduced potato, tomato and chilli in the sixteenth century and that all three were quickly adopted. That is too compressed. Chilli spread rapidly, but potatoes and tomatoes became everyday foods across the subcontinent much later, over the following centuries, with the British period playing a large part in popularising them [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food; @src.collingham-2006-curry]. The Portuguese were an important channel for the new plants, but they were not the only one.

Goa shows the mixing clearly. The dish known as vindaloo descends from a Portuguese wine-and-garlic marinade for meat, adapted in Goa with local spices and vinegar [@src.collingham-2006-curry]. Later, British, Mughal and regional traditions combined again in the restaurant and household dishes that are now called "curry" around the world, a word that Collingham shows has been used in many different ways [@src.collingham-2006-curry].

## How we know

Evidence comes from several kinds of source. Archaeobotany (seeds, charred grain and microscopic starch grains) tells us what people grew and cooked in the distant past [@src.kashyap-2010-farmana-starch]. Recipes and food descriptions in Sanskrit and regional-language texts, and later European and Persian accounts, take the story forward [@src.achaya-1994-indian-food; @src.collingham-2006-curry]. For many dishes the written record is thin, and origin stories attached to a particular dish are often later inventions. Where a dish's history cannot be traced to a text, this article does not claim one.
