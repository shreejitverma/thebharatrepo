For much of recorded history, India was the world's leading maker of fine cotton cloth. Its textiles were traded across the Indian Ocean, and for several centuries they clothed people from West Africa to Japan [@src.riello-2009-how-india-clothed-the-world; @src.guy-1998-woven-cargoes]. The craft traditions behind them are still alive.

## The earliest cotton

The earliest known cotton thread from the Indian subcontinent was found at Mehrgarh in Balochistan. Christophe Moulherat and colleagues examined minerals preserved inside a copper bead from a Neolithic burial and found mineralised fibres they identified as cotton. Moulherat and colleagues placed the burial in the first half of the sixth millennium BCE. A newer radiocarbon study places its cemetery context around 5250 to 4650 BCE [@src.moulherat-2002-mehrgarh-cotton; @src.mutin-2025-mehrgarh-radiocarbon]. This is evidence that cotton fibre was used in this region very early, though it is a single find, and it does not by itself show that cotton cloth was being woven at scale.

A common summary dates Indus cotton to about 2500 BCE, but the Mehrgarh find shows use of cotton well before that. The Harappan cities of the third millennium BCE do provide more secure evidence of textile use: cotton cloth fragments were reported from Mohenjo-daro in the excavations directed by John Marshall, and the city's archaeology includes spindle whorls and evidence of dyeing [@src.marshall-1931-mohenjo-daro; @src.kenoyer-1998-ancient-cities-indus]. The Indus civilisation therefore appears to have had a developed cotton textile craft [@src.wright-2010-ancient-indus].

## A global industry

Textiles came to dominate India's overseas trade in the early modern period. Indian cottons, plain, woven with patterns, or printed and painted, were exported on a large scale to Southeast Asia, East Africa, the Middle East and then Europe [@src.riello-2009-how-india-clothed-the-world]. Printed and painted cloth known in Europe as "calico" was so popular that several European states restricted its import or sale to protect their own wool and silk industries [@src.riello-2013-cotton]. John Guy's *Woven Cargoes* shows that cloth made on India's Coromandel coast was in demand in the spice-producing islands of Southeast Asia, where it was traded for spices [@src.guy-1998-woven-cargoes].

It would be an overstatement to say that Indian textiles dominated all global trade in the seventeenth century; the supported claim is that Indian cotton cloth was among the most widely traded manufactured goods of the period, and that India's producers were a leading supplier to markets across Asia, Africa and Europe [@src.riello-2009-how-india-clothed-the-world]. Later, British industrialisation and policy changed this picture, and Indian handloom production declined in many areas [@src.riello-2013-cotton].

## Famous fabrics

- **Muslin:** Very fine cotton cloth, especially associated with Bengal and the region around Dhaka. The poetic name "woven air" is a common translation of descriptions found in later accounts, not a measured fact, and the exact technical sophistication of the cloth is still studied [@src.riello-2009-how-india-clothed-the-world].
- **Silk:** India has several silk weaving traditions, including Banarasi brocades from Varanasi, Kanchipuram silks from Tamil Nadu and Mysore silk from Karnataka [@src.gillow-2008-indian-textiles]. Their present form owes much to historical patronage, though the dates at which each tradition took its modern shape differ by region.
- **Wool:** Fine goat wool from the Himalayan region, marketed as pashmina and associated with Kashmir, has long been woven into shawls [@src.gillow-2008-indian-textiles].
- **Khadi:** Hand-spun and hand-woven cloth. In the twentieth century it became a political symbol. Lisa Trivedi shows how Gandhi and the nationalist movement used homespun cloth and the spinning wheel to argue against dependence on imported mill cloth and to give the freedom movement a visible uniform [@src.trivedi-2007-clothing-gandhis-nation].

## Techniques

Indian textile traditions are as notable for decoration as for weaving [@src.gillow-2008-indian-textiles].

- **Ikat:** Threads are tied and dyed before weaving, so the pattern appears as the cloth is woven. Pochampally in Telangana is a well-known centre, and related techniques are found elsewhere in India and Asia [@src.gillow-2008-indian-textiles].
- **Block printing and resist dyeing:** Wooden blocks are used to print patterns, often with mordants and resists, as in Ajrakh from Sindh and Kutch and the block-printed cloth of Bagh in Madhya Pradesh [@src.gillow-2008-indian-textiles].
- **Embroidery:** Chikankari from Lucknow and Phulkari from Punjab are among many regional embroidery styles [@src.gillow-2008-indian-textiles].

## How we know

For prehistory, evidence comes from archaeology: fibres, spindle whorls, and impressions of cloth [@src.moulherat-2002-mehrgarh-cotton; @src.kenoyer-1998-ancient-cities-indus]. Textiles rarely survive in the Indian climate, so surviving early cloth is scarce. The great Indian trade cloths of the early modern period are known partly from fragments found in distant places such as Egypt and Indonesia, which preserved them better than India itself, and partly from the records of trading companies [@src.guy-1998-woven-cargoes; @src.riello-2009-how-india-clothed-the-world].
