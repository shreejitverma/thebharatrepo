Bharatanatyam is a solo classical dance of South India, built on rhythmic footwork, precise hand gestures and facial expression that tells stories.
Most people say it comes from the temples of Tamil Nadu, and that is partly true.
The fuller story is more interesting: the dance as we see it today was shaped by court musicians, hereditary women performers, social reformers and a few determined artists, mostly in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

## What the dance is

A Bharatanatyam recital is usually built from two kinds of dance.
One is pure movement, called *nritta*, in which the dancer shapes patterns of footwork and posture to a rhythm and no story is told.
The other is expressive dance, in which the dancer acts out the words of a song through gesture and face, a skill called *abhinaya*.
These ideas, along with the theory of *rasa* (the emotional flavour a performance aims to awaken in the audience), come from Sanskrit writing on theatre, above all the *Natyashastra* [@src.ghosh-1951-natyasastra; @src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader].

The *Natyashastra* is a treatise attributed to Bharata.
Scholars cannot date it precisely and usually place it somewhere between about 200 BCE and 300 CE, with some suggesting earlier or later [@src.ghosh-1951-natyasastra; @src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader].
It is a manual for theatre as a whole, not a handbook for one regional dance, so the claim that Bharatanatyam simply descends from it is a simplification.
Modern dancers and teachers draw on it selectively, and the link has often been stressed because it gives the dance an ancient and respectable pedigree [@src.allen-1997-rewriting-script-south-indian-dance].

## Before the name: sadir

Before the 1930s the art was not called Bharatanatyam.
In South India it went by several names, including *sadir* (also *dasiattam*), and it was performed by hereditary women performers known as devadasis, accompanied by male teachers and musicians called *nattuvanars* [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].
Devadasis were attached to temples and also performed at royal courts and other gatherings.
Their art passed down in families through the female line, with the nattuvanar families supplying training, rhythm and music [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].

The nineteenth-century court of Thanjavur is central to the story of the present-day repertoire.
Four brothers of a nattuvanar family, Chinnayya, Ponnayya, Sivanandam and Vadivelu, known together as the Tanjore Quartet, are traditionally credited with arranging the sequence of items that a recital still follows today [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].
How much of this arrangement is due to them, and how much was gradually fixed by later teachers, is a matter that historians treat with caution, because the evidence is largely family memory and later accounts.

## Decline, reform and revival

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the dance came under attack.
Reformers, including Indian social reformers and Christian missionaries, campaigned against the dedication of girls to temples and often described the dancers as immoral, in what is usually called the anti-nautch movement [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures; @src.allen-1997-rewriting-script-south-indian-dance].
The Madras Devadasis (Prevention of Dedication) Act of 1947 made dedication to a temple illegal in Madras Presidency [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].
Many of the families who had kept the art alive lost their standing and their income.

At the same moment, a cultural revival movement in the 1930s took up the dance, particularly in Madras (now Chennai).
E. Krishna Iyer, a lawyer and amateur performer, and Rukmini Devi Arundale, a Theosophist who had trained in the sadir style, were among its leaders.
Matthew Harp Allen's study of the period argues that this revival recast the art: it was given a new name, Bharatanatyam, was presented on the concert stage rather than in temples and courts, and was framed as a spiritual and national art with ancient Sanskrit roots [@src.allen-1997-rewriting-script-south-indian-dance].
Rukmini Devi founded the Kalakshetra institution in Madras in the 1930s, which trained a new generation of mostly middle-class dancers [@src.allen-1997-rewriting-script-south-indian-dance].
Allen calls this a rewriting of the script, and not simply a rescue of something unchanged.

## Whose dance is it? A debate

Historians read this episode in different ways.
One reading, found in older accounts and in the revivalists' own writings, says that a fading art was saved and purified.
Another, argued by Soneji and by Allen in different ways, says that the revival took the art from the community of hereditary performers and reworked it to suit new, middle-class tastes, so that the origins of the dance are partly overlooked [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures; @src.allen-1997-rewriting-script-south-indian-dance].
Soneji's work also records how devadasi families themselves remember the period and how some of them continued to teach and perform [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].
Both views agree on the basic facts: the modern form, name and stage setting are mostly a twentieth-century creation, built on older practice.

## How we know

For the older period, historians work from the dancers' own repertoire and family histories, from the compositions that nattuvanars and musicians handed down, and from colonial-era writing and court records [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].
For the 1930s revival, there are contemporary newspapers, programmes and the writings of the revivalists, analysed by Allen [@src.allen-1997-rewriting-script-south-indian-dance].
Claims that the dance is "thousands of years old" are usually based on its connection to the *Natyashastra* and to temple sculpture, but those show that dance was a part of Indian culture, not that this particular form existed unchanged [@src.allen-1997-rewriting-script-south-indian-dance].

## Today

Bharatanatyam is now taught worldwide and is performed by women and men of all backgrounds.
It is one of the dance forms that the Sangeet Natak Akademi, India's national academy for the performing arts, recognises as classical.
It is also the subject of continuing scholarship and debate about caste, gender and the proper way to remember the hereditary performers who first practised it [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].
