Indian cinema is not one industry but many. Films are made in Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Malayalam, Kannada, Marathi and a long list of other languages, each with its own studios, stars and audiences. By number of feature films made, India is usually counted among the most prolific film-producing countries in the world, though exact totals depend on how "film" is counted [@src.gokulsing-2013-routledge-handbook-indian-cinemas; @src.rajadhyaksha-1999-encyclopaedia-indian-cinema].

## Arrival and the first films

Moving pictures reached India within months of their invention in Europe. The Lumiere brothers' programme was shown to a paying audience in Bombay in July 1896 [@src.rajadhyaksha-1999-encyclopaedia-indian-cinema]. Early Indian cameramen soon began filming local subjects as well, and short actualities of the kind often credited to Harishchandra Sakharam Bhatavdekar in 1899 belong to this first phase [@src.rajadhyaksha-1999-encyclopaedia-indian-cinema].

The story-telling feature film took shape in the 1910s. Dhundiraj Govind Phalke, known as Dadasaheb Phalke, made *Raja Harishchandra* (1913), a mythological drama drawing on the story of the truthful king. It is officially and widely treated as the first Indian feature film [@src.rajadhyaksha-1999-encyclopaedia-indian-cinema]. The claim is not unchallenged. *Shree Pundalik*, a filmed version of a Marathi stage play by R. G. Torne, was shown in Bombay in 1912, and some historians and Torne's family argue that it deserves the title. Defenders of Phalke reply that *Pundalik* was a recording of a stage play and was processed abroad, so it is better described as the first filmed play than the first Indian-made feature [@src.rajadhyaksha-1999-encyclopaedia-indian-cinema]. A careful statement is therefore that *Raja Harishchandra* is the first Indian feature film by the conventional reckoning, with *Pundalik* as a disputed earlier candidate.

## Sound and the studio era

The coming of sound transformed the industry. *Alam Ara* (1931), directed by Ardeshir Irani, is usually described as the first Hindi-Urdu sound feature [@src.rajadhyaksha-1999-encyclopaedia-indian-cinema]. It was not the only "first": talkies in South Indian languages, such as the Tamil-Telugu *Kalidas*, appeared in the same year, so "the first Indian sound film" is better qualified by language [@src.rajadhyaksha-1999-encyclopaedia-indian-cinema]. Songs became central to Indian film from this moment, a feature that still distinguishes the popular tradition [@src.ganti-2004-bollywood].

During the 1930s and 1940s studios such as Bombay Talkies, Prabhat and New Theatres built reputations for social dramas and literary adaptations. After independence in 1947, film-makers took up themes of nation-building, poverty, class and tradition. Sumita Chakravarty's study of popular cinema from 1947 to 1987 shows how the figure of the nation and the problem of national identity run through the big hits of these decades [@src.chakravarty-1993-national-identity].

## The popular classics

The 1950s are often called a golden age of Hindi cinema, associated with Raj Kapoor, Guru Dutt and Mehboob Khan [@src.ganti-2004-bollywood]. Mehboob's *Mother India* (1957), the story of a peasant woman who holds her family together through hardship, became one of the best-known Indian films of any era and was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film. It did not win [@src.chatterjee-2002-mother-india]. Gayatri Chatterjee's monograph discusses how the film's image of the heroic mother became entangled with ideas of the nation [@src.chatterjee-2002-mother-india].

## Art cinema and the Apu films

In Bengal, Satyajit Ray began a different tradition. His first film, *Pather Panchali* (1955), based on a novel by Bibhutibhushan Bandyopadhyay, was followed by *Aparajito* (1956) and *Apur Sansar* (1959), together known as the Apu Trilogy. The films used non-professional actors and real locations, and they brought Indian cinema to festivals and critics abroad [@src.robinson-1989-satyajit-ray]. Ray was not alone: Ritwik Ghatak and Mrinal Sen in Bengal, and later film-makers such as Shyam Benegal and Adoor Gopalakrishnan, built what is often called parallel or New Indian cinema, which sat alongside, and sometimes borrowed from, the commercial mainstream [@src.rajadhyaksha-1999-encyclopaedia-indian-cinema]. In 1992 the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences gave Ray an Honorary Award for lifetime achievement, which he received while ill in Calcutta [@src.academy-1992-honorary-award-ray].

## What "Bollywood" means

"Bollywood" is a nickname for the Hindi-language film industry based in Bombay (now Mumbai). Tejaswini Ganti, who studied the industry from the inside, notes that many people in it dislike the label, and that it covers only part of Indian cinema [@src.ganti-2004-bollywood]. Telugu, Tamil, Malayalam, Kannada and Bengali industries have their own histories and audiences. Nicknames such as "Tollywood" and "Kollywood" are also used, but they refer to specific regional industries and are not synonyms for pan-Indian cinema [@src.gokulsing-2013-routledge-handbook-indian-cinemas]. In recent years some big-budget films have been made and released in several languages at once to reach audiences across the country and abroad.

## Recent recognition

One example is *RRR* (2022), a Telugu-language film directed by S. S. Rajamouli. Its song "Naatu Naatu" won the Academy Award for Best Original Song at the 95th Academy Awards in March 2023 [@src.academy-2023-95th-awards]. The award went to the song, not to the film as a whole, and the film is a Telugu production rather than a Hindi one.

## How we know

The early history of Indian film is hard to reconstruct because most silent films have been lost. Dates for the earliest screenings come from newspaper advertisements and trade records gathered in reference works such as the *Encyclopaedia of Indian Cinema* [@src.rajadhyaksha-1999-encyclopaedia-indian-cinema]. Recent ceremonies and awards are documented by the awarding bodies themselves [@src.academy-1992-honorary-award-ray; @src.academy-2023-95th-awards].
