Ajanta and Ellora are often named together, but they are two different sites with different stories.
They lie in the Deccan plateau of Maharashtra, roughly 100 km apart, and both were cut by hand into basalt cliffs.
Ajanta is a Buddhist monastic site; Ellora holds Buddhist, Hindu and Jain monuments side by side.
Both are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, inscribed in 1983 [@src.unesco-1983-ajanta-caves; @src.unesco-1983-ellora-caves].

## Ajanta: a Buddhist monastery in a river gorge

Ajanta consists of about thirty rock-cut Buddhist caves arranged in a horseshoe curve above the Waghora river.
Some are viharas (residences for monks, with cells around a central hall) and some are chaitya halls (prayer halls with a stupa at the far end).
UNESCO describes two broad phases: the earliest caves date from the second and first centuries BCE, and many more, richly decorated, were added in the fifth and sixth centuries CE [@src.unesco-1983-ajanta-caves].
Between the two phases there seems to have been a long pause in building [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].

The later caves are where Ajanta becomes extraordinary.
Their walls and ceilings were covered with painted scenes, and their pillars and doorways with carved Buddhas, bodhisattvas and ornament.
The paintings illustrate the life of the Buddha and the Jataka tales, stories of the Buddha's earlier births, and they include courtly scenes with a wide range of people, clothing and architecture [@src.huntington-1985-art-ancient-india; @src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].
Ajanta is one of the main places where Indian painting from the first millennium CE survives in quantity, because most painting of that age elsewhere has been lost [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].

### A dating debate

When exactly were the later caves made?
The traditional, longer view spreads the work across much of the fifth and into the sixth century, and the UNESCO text reflects that outline [@src.unesco-1983-ajanta-caves].
Walter Spink, who spent decades on the site, argued for a much shorter and more precise picture.
In his reading, the great campaign took place in a brief span of about twenty years, roughly 460 to 480 CE, under patrons connected to the Vakataka king Harisena, and it ended abruptly [@src.spink-2006-ajanta-cave-by-cave].
His chronology rests on stylistic sequence, on the unfinished state of many caves and on historical reasoning about royal patronage.
Not all specialists accept it, so the honest statement is that the later phase belongs to the fifth century CE and perhaps beyond, and that its internal order is still argued.

## Ellora: three religions on one escarpment

Ellora has 34 excavated monasteries and temples along a cliff about two kilometres long.
UNESCO counts 12 Buddhist, 17 Hindu and 5 Jain caves, made between roughly 600 and 1000 CE [@src.unesco-1983-ellora-caves].
The Buddhist caves come first in the sequence, the Hindu caves are mostly later, and the Jain caves close the series [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].
Among the Buddhist caves is a tall chaitya hall whose facade imitates timber construction, and among the Jain caves is the Indra Sabha, which has richly carved interiors [@src.huntington-1985-art-ancient-india].

The centrepiece is the Kailasa temple, Cave 16.
It is not a cave in the ordinary sense.
Workers cut a deep trench into the hillside and shaped the temple out of the remaining rock, in the form of a freestanding Shaiva temple complete with gateway, assembly hall, shrine tower and courtyard, carved from the top downward [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].
It is usually attributed to the Rashtrakuta dynasty, and most often to the reign of Krishna I in the middle of the eighth century CE.
The attribution is an inference from the style and from the dynasty's rule over the region, not a direct statement in a dated inscription naming the temple, and some scholars suggest work continued under later kings [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent; @src.huntington-1985-art-ancient-india].

## Why so many religions in one place?

Ellora's mix of faiths is often described as harmony.
The sequence of caves does show that Buddhist, Hindu and Jain communities were active in the same landscape over several centuries.
But how they related to each other, and who paid for what, is a question for historians rather than something the caves answer on their own.
Studies of the nearby Buddhist caves at Aurangabad, for example, trace how Buddhist art and practice in this region changed as patrons and religious fashions shifted [@src.brancaccio-2011-buddhist-caves-aurangabad].

## How we know

Rock-cut monuments are hard to date, because there is no building phase to excavate.
Scholars rely on a few kinds of evidence: dedicatory inscriptions where they exist, the style of sculpture and painting compared with dated works elsewhere, the order in which neighbouring caves cut into one another, and what we know of the ruling dynasties.
That is why Ajanta's chronology can be argued in decades, while Ellora's general span (about 600 to 1000 CE) is firmer than the date of any single cave [@src.spink-2006-ajanta-cave-by-cave; @src.unesco-1983-ellora-caves].
