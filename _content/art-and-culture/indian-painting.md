Painting in India is one of the longest continuous traditions in the world, but its evidence is uneven.
Painted rock surfaces survive from deep prehistory, a few wall paintings from the first millennium CE, and then, from the later medieval period, large numbers of paintings on palm leaf, paper and cloth.
Much of what was painted in between has vanished.

## Rock art

India has thousands of painted rock shelters.
One of the richest groups is at Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh, where hunting scenes, animals, dancers and later figures are painted in layers on the walls of sandstone shelters [@src.unesco-2003-bhimbetka].
The shelters were lived in for a very long time, and excavation of their floors shows a sequence of stone tool traditions from the Lower Palaeolithic through the Mesolithic [@src.unesco-2003-bhimbetka].

It is tempting to give the paintings a single date such as "10,000 BCE".
The evidence does not support that.
Most rock art cannot be measured directly, so it is dated indirectly, by which images lie over which, by style and subject, and by comparison with excavated material [@src.neumayer-2013-prehistoric-rock-art-india].
On that basis the Bhimbetka paintings are usually divided into several phases, from hunter-gatherer scenes, thought to belong in the Mesolithic, up through later farming-period and early historic paintings.
So the safe description is a long span, with the earliest phase uncertain and the later phases better anchored [@src.unesco-2003-bhimbetka; @src.neumayer-2013-prehistoric-rock-art-india].

## Murals: Ajanta and others

The best-preserved early Indian wall paintings are in the Buddhist caves at Ajanta.
A few patches survive in the oldest caves, from the first centuries BCE and CE, while the finest paintings are in the later caves, dated to the fifth century CE (with a tighter dating by Walter Spink to the 460s to 480s CE, which is debated) [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent; @src.spink-2006-ajanta-cave-by-cave].
They show the Buddha's life and the Jataka stories in crowded scenes, with elegant figures, jewellery, textiles and architecture, and many viewers have admired their expressive faces and gestures [@src.huntington-1985-art-ancient-india].
The separate article on the Ajanta and Ellora caves discusses the site and its chronology.

The painted tradition continued in temples and palaces in later centuries, but fewer examples survive, and every new discovery is therefore important to the story [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].

## Manuscript painting before the Mughals

Between about the eleventh and fifteenth centuries, much of the surviving painting is in books.
In eastern India, Buddhist palm-leaf manuscripts carried small painted panels.
In western India, Jain texts were copied and illustrated, first on palm leaf and later on paper, in a distinctive style with sharply drawn faces and bold colour [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent; @src.beach-1992-mughal-rajput-painting].
These manuscripts matter because they connect the world of Ajanta with the court schools that came later.

## The Mughal school

Mughal painting began in the sixteenth century at the imperial court.
The emperor Humayun, who spent years in Safavid Iran, brought Persian artists back to India, and under his son Akbar a large studio produced illustrated histories and epics, among them the illustrated Hamzanama [@src.beach-1992-mughal-rajput-painting].
Mughal artists blended Persian technique with Indian subjects and with an interest in observation.
Under Jahangir, and later Shah Jahan, painters produced portraits, natural history studies and formal court scenes [@src.beach-1992-mughal-rajput-painting].

"Realism" is a fair shorthand for the portraits and the animal and plant studies, but not for everything: Mughal painting also used idealised, symbolic and allegorical conventions, so realism is a tendency rather than the rule.

## Rajput and Pahari painting

"Rajput" painting refers to the work of Hindu courts in Rajasthan and central India, such as Mewar, Bundi, Kota and Kishangarh, from the late sixteenth century on.
"Pahari" painting refers to the hill courts of the Himalayan foothills, such as Basohli, Guler and Kangra, mostly from the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries [@src.beach-1992-mughal-rajput-painting].
Their subjects include the stories of Krishna and Radha, the Ramayana and the Bhagavata Purana, musical modes illustrated as paintings (ragamala), and portraits of rulers.
Devotion and romance are major themes, but so are court life and royal portraiture, and the Mughal studio influenced many of these courts [@src.beach-1992-mughal-rajput-painting].

## The Deccan schools

In the Deccan, the sultanates of Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda patronised their own painting from the sixteenth century.
It combines Persian and Turkish elements with local Deccani and Hindu traditions, and in the best examples has rich colour, fantasy and an unusual sensitivity to landscape and atmosphere [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].

## What this article leaves out

Folk and community traditions, including Madhubani, Warli and Pattachitra, are living practices with their own scholarship, and this article does not try to summarise them.
The Mughal, Rajput, Pahari and Deccan "schools" are also convenient modern groupings: artists moved between courts, so the boundaries were never fixed [@src.beach-1992-mughal-rajput-painting].
