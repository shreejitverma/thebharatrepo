Fighting with weapons and bare hands has been taught in India for a very long time, and several traditions are still practised.
They are often presented as ancient, mystical and unbroken.
The documented picture is more modest and in some ways more interesting: these arts were shaped by warfare, village teaching, healing knowledge and, in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, by colonial rule and revival movements.

## Kalaripayattu (Kerala)

Kalaripayattu is the most carefully studied Indian martial art, thanks largely to the anthropologist and theatre scholar Phillip Zarrilli, who trained in it and wrote its main scholarly account [@src.zarrilli-1998-when-the-body-becomes-all-eyes].
A *kalari* is a training hall, traditionally a sunken earthen pit.
Students learn bodily exercises, then fighting sequences with wooden and metal weapons, then bare-hand techniques, and a system of massage and medicine.
Zarrilli describes how the art draws on Ayurvedic and yogic ideas of the body, which is why it is often described as both a fighting art and a discipline of health [@src.zarrilli-1998-when-the-body-becomes-all-eyes].
He also describes regional styles, usually grouped as northern, southern and central [@src.zarrilli-1998-when-the-body-becomes-all-eyes].

### How old is it?

The original article called Kalaripayattu the "Mother of all Martial Arts".
This is a slogan, not a finding of historical research.
The scholarly account of the art by Zarrilli is built on documented practice and does not rest on such a claim, and no source checked for this article demonstrates it [@src.zarrilli-1998-when-the-body-becomes-all-eyes].
A related popular story, that the art travelled to China and gave rise to other martial arts, is likewise not demonstrated by documented sources.

What can be said is more limited.
The Tamil Sangam poems, usually placed in the early centuries CE, describe warriors, weapons and battle, and the word *kalari* appears there in the sense of a battlefield, not a school [@src.thapar-2002-early-india].
The original article placed such references in the 3rd century BCE, which is earlier than the usual dating of those poems.
Later, Kerala's own oral ballads, the Vadakkan Pattukal, celebrate warrior heroes trained in kalaris, and training manuals are written in Malayalam.
Many accounts link the rise of organised martial schools in Kerala to the long wars of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, and treat Kalaripayattu as clearly established by the later medieval period [@src.zarrilli-1998-when-the-body-becomes-all-eyes].
The original article's "golden age during the Chola-Chera wars" is an interpretation of this setting, not a documented event, and it is better described as a reasoned estimate (grade C) than as established fact.

### Colonial decline and revival

In the early nineteenth century the British authorities in Kerala restricted martial training, after uprisings against colonial rule; accounts differ on the exact date and on whether it was a formal ban [@src.zarrilli-1998-when-the-body-becomes-all-eyes].
The art declined as warfare changed and as the old militia systems ended.
It was revived from the 1920s, first at Thalassery, in the context of a wider movement to value traditional arts, and the first printed manuals followed [@src.zarrilli-1998-when-the-body-becomes-all-eyes].
Kerala's dance-drama Kathakali also draws on its exercises, which shows how closely the two arts are connected in training [@src.zarrilli-2000-kathakali-dance-drama].

## Silambam (Tamil Nadu)

Silambam is a Tamil staff-fighting art, practised with a long bamboo staff, and also with other weapons.
Its traditional lineage is Tamil and the name is Tamil.
Popular accounts date it to remote antiquity, but I could not find a scholarly source that can support a specific early date, so none is given here.
The staff is among the oldest and most widespread of fighting tools anywhere, which makes any claim about the origin of a particular style hard to prove.

## Gatka (Punjab)

Gatka is a weapons art associated with the Sikhs, in which wooden sticks stand in for swords during training.
Its roots lie in the Sikh martial tradition that took shape in the 1600s, when the sixth Guru, Hargobind, is traditionally said to have taken up two swords to represent spiritual and worldly authority, and the community became increasingly armed [@src.singh-2004-history-of-the-sikhs-vol-1].
Khushwant Singh's history places this militarisation in the context of Mughal persecution of the Sikhs [@src.singh-2004-history-of-the-sikhs-vol-1].
The training form called Gatka, and the broader practice of *shastar vidya* (weapons skills), are today taught as both sport and heritage.

## Mallakhamb (Maharashtra)

Mallakhamb is a discipline in which gymnasts perform holds and turns on a vertical wooden pole, or on a hanging rope.
The original article called it "traditional aerial yoga".
That is a modern comparison, since the art is better described as athletic training for wrestlers.
The best-known account says it was revived in the early nineteenth century by a trainer named Balambhatta Deodhar at the court of the Peshwa Bajirao II (r. 1796 to 1818).
That story is repeated in popular and institutional sources, and the details vary; I could not verify it in scholarly literature, so it should be read as tradition.

## What the evidence allows

Taken together, these traditions show some common themes: training halls and teachers' lineages, weapons practice for warriors, and a link with physical culture and healing.
They also show the limits of the record.
For most of them, there are few written sources before the later medieval period, and many popular histories repeat claims of great antiquity that cannot be tested.
Where scholars such as Zarrilli have worked in depth, the result is a history that is rich and tied to specific places, people and periods, and does not require a claim to be the "first" or "oldest" of anything.
