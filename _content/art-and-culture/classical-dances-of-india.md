India's national academy for music, dance and drama, the Sangeet Natak Akademi, recognises eight forms of dance as classical: Bharatanatyam, Kathak, Kathakali, Kuchipudi, Odissi, Manipuri, Mohiniyattam and Sattriya.
The word "classical" here does not mean that the dances are unchanged since antiquity.
It marks dances that have a worked-out technique, a body of teaching and a claim to the old Sanskrit theory of performance.
This article tells where each came from, and what is known and not known about how old it really is.

## What they share

All eight forms use ideas that go back to the *Natyashastra*, a Sanskrit treatise attributed to Bharata, which covers drama, dance and music.
It describes expressive hand gestures, postures, the pure dance called *nritta*, and the theory of *rasa*, the emotional flavour a performance is meant to awaken [@src.ghosh-1951-natyasastra; @src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader].
Scholars date the work, on internal evidence, to somewhere between about 200 BCE and 200 CE, though the date is debated [@src.ghosh-1951-natyasastra].

The *Natyashastra* was written long before any of the eight forms existed in their present shape.
So saying that they are "all rooted" in it is true as a statement about vocabulary and theory, and misleading if it suggests a direct line of descent.
Regional dance traditions had their own songs, teachers and techniques, and in the twentieth century they were consciously linked to the Sanskrit text to give them national standing [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].

A small bronze statue of a young woman found at Mohenjo-daro, dated to the Mature Harappan period, is often called the "Dancing Girl" [@src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization].
The name is a modern one.
The figure shows a confident standing pose, not a documented dance, and it cannot be linked to any later classical form.

## The eight forms

**Bharatanatyam (Tamil Nadu).**
The evidence does not establish Bharatanatyam as the oldest classical dance form.
The dance it draws on, practised by hereditary devadasi performers, is older than its name, but the name and the modern concert form date from the 1930s [@src.allen-1997-rewriting-script-south-indian-dance; @src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].
It is known for its bent-knee stance, strong footwork and expressive hand gestures.
See the separate article on Bharatanatyam.

**Kathak (North India).**
Kathak takes its name from *kathaka*, a storyteller.
Its performers belonged to hereditary families and were linked with the courts of North India, and its best-known schools, or *gharanas*, are connected with places such as Lucknow, Jaipur and Banaras [@src.walker-2014-kathak-historical-perspective].
It is known for fast footwork, ankle bells and rapid spins.
The idea that Kathak was simply "influenced by Persian culture" is too simple.
Margaret Walker's history presents it as a form that developed in the settings of both Hindu devotional storytelling and Muslim and Rajput courts, and shows how its history has been written differently by different groups [@src.walker-2014-kathak-historical-perspective; @src.chakravorty-2008-bells-of-change].

**Kathakali (Kerala).**
The name means "story-play", from Malayalam *katha* (story) and *kali* (play).
It is a dance-drama in which male actors in elaborate make-up and costume tell stories from the epics and Puranas, through gesture and face while singers voice the text [@src.zarrilli-2000-kathakali-dance-drama].
Phillip Zarrilli's study treats it as a form that took its present shape in the 1600s and 1700s and was reorganised in the twentieth century [@src.zarrilli-2000-kathakali-dance-drama].

**Kuchipudi (Andhra Pradesh).**
The form is named for the village of Kuchipudi.
Traditionally its dance-dramas were performed by troupes of Brahmin men, who also played the women's roles, and the form is credited in tradition to a saint-poet called Siddhendra Yogi, whose dates are uncertain [@src.kamath-2019-impersonations].
In the twentieth century Kuchipudi was also taken up as a solo dance, including by women, and it grew closely linked with the performance traditions of Telugu-speaking women dancers [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures; @src.kamath-2019-impersonations].

**Odissi (Odisha).**
Odissi is linked to the temple of Jagannath at Puri, where women performers called *maharis* danced as part of worship, and to boy dancers called *gotipuas*.
It is known for the *tribhangi*, a pose with the body bent in three places, and for sculptural poses.
As a stage form it was assembled and standardised in the middle of the twentieth century, drawing on these living traditions, temple sculpture and the *Natyashastra*.

**Manipuri (Manipur).**
Manipuri is a devotional dance of the Meitei people of Manipur, closely tied to Vaishnava worship and to the *Ras Lila*, which tells of Krishna and the *gopis*.
Its movements are gentle and flowing.
Its present form is associated with the Vaishnava revival of the 1700s in Manipur.

**Mohiniyattam (Kerala).**
The name is usually translated "dance of the enchantress", from *Mohini*, the female form of Vishnu.
It is a solo dance with soft swaying movement, performed traditionally by women.
It was reorganised and taught in new institutions in Kerala in the twentieth century.

**Sattriya (Assam).**
Sattriya comes from the *sattras*, the Vaishnava monasteries of Assam, and is traditionally credited to the saint and reformer Srimanta Sankardev (c. 1449 to 1568 by common reckoning).
For centuries it was performed by monks as part of devotional drama.
The Sangeet Natak Akademi recognised it as classical in 2000, the most recent addition to the list (reported in secondary sources; not checked here against an Akademi record).

## A note on sourcing

The short entries for Odissi, Manipuri, Mohiniyattam and Sattriya state widely reported facts, but they are not yet backed by a verified citation, and a reviewer should add one before this article leaves draft status.

## A common pattern

Looking across the eight, a pattern appears.
Each has older roots in temples, monasteries, courts or village troupes.
Each was reshaped in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, under colonial rule and after independence, by reformers, teachers, scholars and the new institutions of the modern nation [@src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].
Historians therefore tend to read the word "classical" as a modern category created through that process, and not as proof of an unbroken ancient chain.
Others, including many practitioners, stress the continuity of teaching lineages, and the sources agree that the older traditions are real and deserve study in their own right.

## Timeline

- **About 200 BCE to 200 CE:** Likely window for the compilation of the *Natyashastra*, a debated date [@src.ghosh-1951-natyasastra].
- **1600s to 1700s:** Kathakali and Manipuri take shape in forms close to today's [@src.zarrilli-2000-kathakali-dance-drama].
- **1930s:** Bharatanatyam is named and reshaped in Madras [@src.allen-1997-rewriting-script-south-indian-dance].
- **1950s onward:** Odissi is reconstructed and standardised.
- **2000:** Sattriya is recognised by the Sangeet Natak Akademi as classical.
