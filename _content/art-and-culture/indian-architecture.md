Indian architecture is not one style but many regional traditions, built in brick, stone and timber for different faiths and rulers.
This article follows the main threads in rough time order and says what each rests on.

## Cities before temples: the Indus civilisation

The earliest large-scale planned architecture in South Asia belongs to the Mature Harappan phase, about 2600 to 1900 BCE.
At sites such as Mohenjo-daro, Harappa and Dholavira, builders laid out walled settlements, often with a raised citadel and a lower town, and used kiln-fired bricks of consistent proportions [@src.kenoyer-1998-ancient-cities-indus; @src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization].
Houses were connected to covered brick drains running beneath the streets, and the Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro is a carefully waterproofed brick tank [@src.kenoyer-1998-ancient-cities-indus].
No building at these cities is securely identified as a temple or a palace, which is one reason the civilisation's religion and politics remain debated [@src.possehl-2002-indus-civilization].

## Stupas, pillars and rock-cut halls

After a long gap in the surviving record, monumental architecture reappears in the early historic period.
The stupa, a solid dome that holds relics or marks a sacred place, is the basic Buddhist monument.
At Sanchi, in central India, the Great Stupa was founded in the third century BCE, in the time of the Maurya emperor Ashoka according to the usual account, and was later enlarged and surrounded by stone gateways carved with scenes from the Buddha's life and former births [@src.unesco-1989-sanchi; @src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].
Around the same period Buddhist, Jain and other communities began cutting halls and monasteries directly into rock, imitating timber buildings in stone, a practice that reached its peak at Ajanta and Ellora (see the separate article on those caves) [@src.huntington-1985-art-ancient-india].

## The Hindu temple

The structural stone temple emerges in the Gupta period and the centuries that follow.
The core of nearly every Hindu temple is a small, dark shrine room, the garbhagriha ("womb chamber"), housing the main image, and over it rises a tower that marks the sacred centre [@src.michell-1988-hindu-temple].
Around that core, builders added porches, halls for worshippers and surrounding walls, and over time the plan grew more elaborate.

Scholars group temples into broad families, often called Nagara (north) and Dravida (south).
These are modern, regional labels that overlap in practice; they are useful shorthand and no more than that [@src.michell-1988-hindu-temple; @src.hardy-1995-indian-temple-architecture].

### Nagara and its regional versions

In the Nagara tradition the tower, called a shikhara, curves inward as it rises, ending in a rounded crown.
The temples of Khajuraho, built under the Chandela dynasty between about 950 and 1050 CE, are a famous example, with clustered towers rising in steps toward the main summit [@src.unesco-1986-khajuraho].
In Odisha the tradition took its own form, and the Sun Temple at Konark, of the thirteenth century, was designed as a colossal chariot of the sun god [@src.unesco-1984-konark-sun-temple].

### Dravida

In the Dravida tradition the tower, called a vimana, is built in rising storeys, each a smaller version of the one below, and capped by a dome-shaped finial.
The Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur, completed in the early eleventh century under the Chola king Rajaraja I, has one of the tallest such towers [@src.unesco-1987-great-living-chola-temples].
Over later centuries, tall gateway towers, gopuras, came to dominate the outlines of large temple complexes in Tamil Nadu [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].

### The Deccan and the problem of "Vesara"

Between north and south lies the Deccan, where the Chalukyas of Badami and Aihole experimented with both northern and southern forms in the sixth to eighth centuries [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].
The name "Vesara" is often applied to this middle zone, and to the later Hoysala temples at Belur and Halebidu, as a "hybrid" of Nagara and Dravida.
That summary is a simplification.
Specialists describe Deccan temples by dynasty and region, for example the Karnata Dravida tradition, and treat the Hoysala temples, with their star-shaped plans and dense carving, as a distinctive achievement in their own right rather than a mere blend [@src.hardy-1995-indian-temple-architecture].

## Indo-Islamic architecture

From about 1200 CE, with the rise of the Delhi Sultanate, new building types and techniques entered the subcontinent: mosques, tombs, minarets, and the true arch and true dome built with wedge-shaped stones.
Indian builders already knew arches and domes made of corbelled courses, which are laid in overlapping layers, so the novelty was the structural method more than the shapes [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].
The Qutb Minar complex in Delhi, begun in about 1199, is among the earliest major monuments of the new order, and its carving shows Indian craft skills at work within it [@src.unesco-1993-qutb-minar].

The Mughal period brought a further blending of Persian and Indian traditions.
Under Akbar (reigned 1556 to 1605), red sandstone forts and palaces drew on regional building habits, and under Shah Jahan (reigned 1628 to 1658) white marble, precise geometry and garden settings reached their most refined form [@src.asher-1992-architecture-of-mughal-india].
The Taj Mahal at Agra, begun in the early 1630s as a tomb for Mumtaz Mahal and finished by the early 1650s, belongs to this tradition, and is a mausoleum within a walled garden [@src.unesco-1983-taj-mahal; @src.koch-2006-complete-taj-mahal].
In the Deccan, the sultanates of Bijapur and Golconda developed their own tomb and mosque architecture, including the domed mausoleum known as the Gol Gumbaz at Bijapur, built in the seventeenth century [@src.harle-1994-art-architecture-indian-subcontinent].

## A note on "golden ages"

Phrases such as "golden age of temple architecture" are judgements, not dates.
Temple building continued, with changing forms, from the Gupta period to the present day, and no single century was its peak.
The dates here are therefore given as the period of a monument's construction, not as a ranking.
