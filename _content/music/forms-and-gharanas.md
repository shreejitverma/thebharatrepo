Imagine two great rivers that rise in the same mountains, take different courses, and water different plains.
Indian classical music is usually described this way: the Hindustani tradition of the north and the Karnatak tradition of the south share ideas of raga (melodic mode) and tala (rhythmic cycle), but have developed their own repertoires, instruments, and concert shapes. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music; @src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
This article walks through the main forms in each tradition and then looks at the gharanas, the "houses" of teaching that carry Hindustani styles through the generations.

## Dhrupad: the austere elder

Dhrupad is the oldest form of Hindustani vocal music that is still widely performed.
It is accompanied by the tanpura drone and the barrel drum called pakhawaj, and it avoids light ornament in favour of slow, weighty movement between notes. [@src.widdess-2001-dhrupad-grove; @src.sanyal-widdess-2004-dhrupad]
A dhrupad performance opens with an unmetered alap that uses syllables such as "nom" and "tom" rather than words, then moves into pulsed sections, and only then reaches the composition itself. [@src.sanyal-widdess-2004-dhrupad]
Singers and teachers often give these syllables a devotional gloss, but musically their job is to carry the melody without the distraction of a text.

The fixed composition traditionally has four parts, named sthayi, antara, sanchari and abhoga, which are usually described as moving from the lower and middle register, up to the higher register, through a development section, and finally to a conclusion. [@src.widdess-2001-dhrupad-grove]
Tradition often credits the Gwalior court of Raja Man Singh Tomar (reigned late fifteenth to early sixteenth century) with a great flowering of dhrupad. [@src.widdess-2001-dhrupad-grove]
Treat that as the traditional account of a form whose full early history is still being worked out.

## Khayal: the form of imagination

The word khayal is Persian and Arabic in origin and means "imagination" or "idea".
Khayal is the dominant form of Hindustani vocal concerts today, typically accompanied by tabla and a melodic instrument such as sarangi or harmonium. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]
A khayal performance usually has a slow composition (bada khayal) that is elaborated at length, followed by a fast one (chhota khayal) in which the singer unleashes rapid runs of notes called taans. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]

Where did it come from?
There are several positions, and the evidence does not settle the matter.

- **The Amir Khusrau tradition.** Popular tradition credits the poet and musician Amir Khusrau (1253 to 1325) with inventing khayal.
- **The Jaunpur tradition.** Another account points to the Sharqi sultans of Jaunpur in the fifteenth century as patrons of an early khayal.
- **The eighteenth-century Delhi consolidation.** The form is firmly documented at the Mughal court in the eighteenth century, where the musicians Sadarang and Adarang are remembered as composers of khayal texts and as teachers who shaped its style. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]

The standard reference account treats the earliest attributions as tradition rather than demonstrated history, and places the clearest evidence in the later period. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]
A careful reader can hold all three in mind: the name and ideas may have older roots, but what we recognise as khayal today took shape across several centuries.

## Thumri and the lighter forms

Thumri is a "semi-classical" form whose texts are mostly about romantic and devotional longing, often the love of Radha and Krishna.
Its signature technique is bol banao, in which a singer repeats a single line of poetry again and again, colouring it each time with a different melodic or emotional shade. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]
Thumri and related genres such as dadra, hori, chaiti and kajri are usually built on lighter ragas and allow more freedom with the raga's boundaries than khayal does.
Two other genres sit nearby: tarana, which uses rhythmic syllables in place of words, and tappa, a quick, twisting song style from the north-west. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]

## The Karnatak concert

A Karnatak concert (kutcheri) is a carefully paced programme.
Many concerts open with a varnam, a composition that exercises the voice and displays the raga's skeleton, and they are built around the kriti, the central song form. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music; @src.widdess-2001-kriti-grove]
A kriti has three sections: the pallavi (the opening theme, to which the singer returns), the anupallavi (a second section, usually lying higher), and the charanam (a concluding stanza). [@src.widdess-2001-kriti-grove]
Singers also decorate a kriti line with fresh melodic variations. These improvisatory arts are described in a companion article on manodharma, the creative improvisation of Indian music.

The most elaborate item in a major concert is the ragam tanam pallavi (RTP), in which a free exploration of the raga, a rhythmic exploration called tanam, and a single line of text set to a complex tala are developed in turn. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
The concert often ends with a lively thillana or a short devotional piece.

### The Trinity

The late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries are remembered as a golden age of Karnatak composition, and three composers born in or near the town of Tiruvarur in the Kaveri delta are called the Trinity.

| Composer | Dates | Commonly noted for |
|---|---|---|
| Syama Sastri | 1762 to 1827 | Compositions in Telugu, and the intricate swarajati |
| Tyagaraja | 1767 to 1847 | Devotional kritis in Telugu, including the five "Pancharatna" kritis |
| Muthuswami Dikshitar | 1775 to 1835 | Kritis in Sanskrit, many in slow tempo, with deep attention to the raga |

[@src.lord-2001-tyagaraja-grove; @src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
These characterisations are generalisations, and each composer wrote across moods and tempos.

## What is a gharana?

The word gharana comes from the Hindi and Urdu word for "house" or "family".
A gharana is a lineage of teachers and pupils that carries a recognisable style, usually named after the place where it consolidated or the family that founded it. [@src.widdess-2001-gharana-grove]
The gharana system matured largely in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, as court patronage changed and musicians settled in new cities. [@src.widdess-2001-gharana-grove; @src.vandermeer-1980-gharana-system]

The table below lists the best-known Hindustani vocal lineages and the figures usually associated with them.
Stylistic summaries are the shorthand that musicians and critics use, not rules, and individual artists often draw on more than one house.

| Gharana | Associated founders or early masters | Often-cited hallmark | Well-known later artists |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gwalior | Nathan Pir Bakhsh, Haddu Khan, Hassu Khan | Broad open-voiced singing, clear compositions | V. D. Paluskar and Krishnarao Shankar Pandit |
| Agra | Ghagge Khudabaksh; later Faiyaz Khan | Resonant voice, rhythmic play with the text | Faiyaz Khan |
| Kirana | Abdul Karim Khan and Abdul Wahid Khan | Careful intonation, slow note-by-note unfolding | Bhimsen Joshi, Gangubai Hangal |
| Jaipur-Atrauli | Alladiya Khan | Rarer ragas, intricate melodic patterns | Kesarbai Kerkar, Mogubai Kurdikar, Kishori Amonkar |
| Patiala | Ali Baksh Khan and Fateh Ali Khan | Agile fast taans, thumri flavour | Bade Ghulam Ali Khan |

[@src.widdess-2001-gharana-grove; @src.vandermeer-1980-gharana-system]
Instrumental music has its own lines of teaching.
A well-known example is the style developed by Allauddin Khan (1862 or thereabouts to 1972) at Maihar, where he taught sitar, sarod, flute, and other instruments to a remarkable group of students, including Ravi Shankar and his own son Ali Akbar Khan. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]

## Caveats worth keeping

Lineage trees are tidy, but real musical lives are not.
Many artists studied with teachers of several houses, founding dates are approximate, and the stylistic labels are looser than the table suggests. [@src.widdess-2001-gharana-grove]
Some scholars have argued that the old gharana system has faded as radio, recordings, and concert halls replaced the court. [@src.vandermeer-1980-gharana-system]
The living tradition is still taught person to person, which is why guru and shishya (teacher and pupil) remain at the heart of how forms are passed on.
