## What a raga is, and what it is not

Imagine two people humming the same five notes. One makes you think of a quiet dawn, the other of a lamp being lit at dusk.
The notes are the same, so what differs is the way the notes are approached, stressed, bent and left.
That difference is the heart of a **raga** (rāga, राग).
A raga is a melodic framework with a characteristic set of notes, typical phrases, preferred resting notes and a typical way of ornamenting them. [@src.powers-2001-mode-in-south-asia-raga]

Because of this, listing the notes of a raga is only the first step.
Two ragas can share every note and still be completely different.
Scholars therefore describe a raga with both a scale and a set of conventions: which notes are emphasised, which are weak, and which phrases announce the raga. [@src.powers-widdess-2001-india-theory-practice]

## Two systems, one family of ideas

North Indian (Hindustani) and South Indian (Carnatic) musicians share the idea of raga but organise ragas differently.

- Hindustani musicians commonly use the **thaat** (ṭhāṭ) system, a set of ten parent scales popularised in the early twentieth century by V. N. Bhatkhande. A raga is assigned to the thaat whose notes it most closely matches, even when the fit is imperfect. [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga; @src.powers-2001-mode-in-south-asia-raga]
- Carnatic musicians use the **melakarta** scheme, a systematic table of 72 parent scales, each with seven notes. Other ragas are described as **janya** (derived) from a parent. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music; @src.powers-2001-mode-in-south-asia-raga]

Many Hindustani ragas have a Carnatic counterpart with the same or nearly the same notes, though the two traditions sing them in quite different ways. The table shows how the seven ragas in this article line up.
The notes are described with the usual abbreviations: S, R, G, M, P, D, N for the seven degrees, with "komal" meaning flattened and "tivra" meaning sharpened (for M).

| Raga | Hindustani thaat | Carnatic counterpart | Usual time of performance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yaman | Kalyan | Kalyani (Mechakalyani, no. 65) | Early evening to night |
| Bhairav | Bhairav | Mayamalavagowla (no. 15) | Early morning |
| Darbari Kanada | Asavari | No exact scale match is claimed here | Late night |
| Miyan ki Todi | Todi | Shubhapantuvarali (no. 45) | Late morning |
| Malkauns | Bhairavi (in Bhatkhande's scheme) | Hindolam, a five-note derived raga | Around midnight |
| Bhimpalasi | Kafi | Kharaharapriya (no. 22) is the parent scale | Late afternoon |
| Kalyani | (Carnatic raga; its Hindustani cousin is Yaman) | Mechakalyani (no. 65) | Concert, any time |

The "time of performance" column is a convention of Hindustani practice, which assigns each raga a part of the day or night. [@src.powers-2001-mode-in-south-asia-raga] It is a strong custom, not a physical law, and concerts today often ignore it.
The melakarta numbers are the standard numbering of the 72-scale table. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]

## Yaman and Kalyani: one scale, two worlds

Yaman is one of the most frequently performed Hindustani ragas.
It uses all seven notes with a sharp (tivra) fourth, M, which gives it a bright, rising feel. It belongs to the Kalyan thaat. [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga]
A well-known convention is that the ascent often begins from the low N, as in "N R G, M D N S'", rather than marching straight up from S, which is one reason Yaman does not sound like a plain scale. [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga]

Its Carnatic counterpart Kalyani (the 65th melakarta, Mechakalyani) has the same seven degrees, but the Carnatic treatment relies much more on oscillating ornaments called **gamakas** on individual notes. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
It is tempting to say that Carnatic Kalyani allows plain linear runs while Hindustani Yaman does not, but both traditions have their own phrasing conventions, so that is too simple to state as a rule.

## Bhairav and Todi: dawn and the long morning

Bhairav belongs to the Bhairav thaat, which has a flat second (R) and flat sixth (D) with the other notes natural; it matches the Carnatic parent Mayamalavagowla. [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga; @src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
Performers commonly describe a slow, wide oscillation on the flat R and flat D. It is a widely taught trait, though how wide the oscillation is differs from school to school.

Miyan ki Todi is assigned to the Todi thaat, with flat R, flat G, sharp M and flat D. [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga]
Beware of a naming trap: in Carnatic music "Todi" usually means Hanumatodi, which is a different scale from Hindustani Todi. The Hindustani Todi scale corresponds to the Carnatic parent Shubhapantuvarali. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
Many accounts say that in Todi the P is left out or lightly handled in the ascent, but the practice varies between lineages.

## Darbari Kanada: the legend and the evidence

Darbari Kanada is a slow, grave raga of the late night, classed under the Asavari thaat, and singers aim for a deep, slowly oscillating treatment of the flat G and flat D, approached from above. [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga]
Its name means "of the court", and a famous tradition says the singer Miyan Tansen created it for the Mughal emperor Akbar.
That is a hereditary tradition, and it should be read as one. No contemporary source documenting the creation is cited here. [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga]
Embellishments of the story, such as that the raga was meant to soothe the emperor's insomnia, are likewise undocumented.

A common teaching is that the flat G and D of Darbari are lowered beyond the standard flat notes ("ati-komal"). This is best read as a performers' description of the effect, since no measured study is cited here.

## Malkauns: five notes

Malkauns uses only five notes, S, flat G, M, flat D and flat N, omitting both R and P. Bhatkhande placed it in the Bhairavi thaat. [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga]
Its pentatonic shape and its strong emphasis on M make it sound spacious and still.
It corresponds to the Carnatic five-note raga Hindolam. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
Claims about an ancient etymology and a link to Shiva's dance are not documented, and are not asserted here.

## Bhimpalasi: a different count up and down

Bhimpalasi belongs to the Kafi thaat, whose notes match the Carnatic parent Kharaharapriya. [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga; @src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
It shows an important idea: a raga can have a different number of notes in ascent and descent.
In Bhimpalasi the ascent leaves out R and D and so has five notes, while the descent uses all seven.
The ascent often begins from the low flat N: "N S, flat G, M, P, flat N, S'". [@src.ruckert-widdess-2017-hindustani-raga]

## Where to go next

The same raga can sound different in the voice of a Hindustani vocalist and a Carnatic violinist, which is a reminder that a raga lives in performance more than on paper. [@src.powers-widdess-2001-india-theory-practice]
