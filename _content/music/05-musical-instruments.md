India has an extremely rich and varied set of instruments, from village drums and reed pipes to the instruments of the classical concert.
For historians, the difficulty is that musical instruments are made of perishable materials, so we usually learn about them from texts, sculptures and coins rather than from the objects themselves [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india; @src.arnold-2000-garland-south-asia].

## The four families

Sanskrit writers on music grouped instruments by how they make sound.
The *Natyashastra*, a Sanskrit treatise on drama, dance and music, gives a four-fold scheme, which later writers repeated [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india]:

1. **Tata vadya (stretched, stringed):** the chordophones. Examples: the *veena*, *sitar*, *sarod*, *tambura*.
2. **Avanaddha vadya (covered, skin-headed):** drums with a membrane. Examples: *mridangam*, *pakhawaj*, *tabla*.
3. **Sushira vadya (hollow, wind):** wind instruments. Examples: the *bansuri* flute, *shehnai*, *nadaswaram*.
4. **Ghana vadya (solid):** instruments whose body is struck or shaken, such as the *ghatam* (clay pot), *manjira* (cymbals) and *jal tarang* (tuned bowls).

Its date is debated: it is a composite text, and scholars give a wide range running from about the last centuries BCE to the early centuries CE [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
This fourfold system is close to the modern Hornbostel-Sachs scheme used by museums worldwide, which is why scholars often call it a remarkable early classification [@src.arnold-2000-garland-south-asia].

## Early evidence

The earliest layer of Indian musical thought is the chanting of Vedic verses, in which texts are set to a small number of tones, as in the Samaveda [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
This tradition is still carried by oral transmission today and is recognised by UNESCO [@src.unesco-2003-tradition-vedic-chanting].
Vedic and later texts name a range of instruments, including harps, flutes and drums, but identifying exactly which instrument a given ancient word denotes is often uncertain [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].

The *Natyashastra* describes several drums and a stringed instrument ensemble used in theatre, giving us a picture of the stage orchestra of its time [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
A reliably dated image comes from the Gupta period: gold coins of Samudragupta, who ruled in the 4th century CE, show the king seated and playing a stringed instrument, which John Allan's catalogue of Gupta coins describes as a *vina* [@src.allan-1914-gupta-coins].

## Strings

The word *veena* has been used for many different stringed instruments over time, from ancient harps to the modern South Indian *saraswati veena* [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india; @src.arnold-2000-garland-south-asia].
So the name tells us less than you might expect.
The *sitar* and *sarod* are better known from the 18th and 19th centuries, when courts in Delhi, Lucknow and elsewhere developed them from earlier long-necked lutes and from the Afghan rabab [@src.miner-1993-sitar-and-sarod].
The *tambura*, which gives the steady drone, is common to nearly all Indian art music [@src.arnold-2000-garland-south-asia].

## Drums

The *Natyashastra* already names drums such as the *mridanga*, and the word *mridangam* is still used today for the double-headed barrel drum of South India [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
Modern mridangams are made by specialist craftsmen, and T. M. Krishna's study of one family of makers shows that their skill and their place in the tradition have been under-acknowledged [@src.krishna-2020-sebastian-and-sons].
The north Indian *pakhawaj* is a related barrel drum, while the *tabla* is a pair of single-headed drums [@src.arnold-2000-garland-south-asia].

## Winds and solid instruments

The *bansuri*, a side-blown bamboo flute, and the double-reed *shehnai* and *nadaswaram* are widely used in classical, temple and ceremonial music [@src.arnold-2000-garland-south-asia].
The nadaswaram is a loud South Indian instrument played at temples and weddings.
Ghana instruments such as the *ghatam* and *manjira* are among the simplest and most widely used [@src.arnold-2000-garland-south-asia].

## How we know, and a correction

The popular attribution that Persian instruments, including the sitar and the tabla, were introduced in the 13th century by Amir Khusrau is not supported by early evidence.
Allyn Miner finds that the sitar is documented from the 18th century and developed from earlier instruments, and James Kippen shows that the story about Khusrau and the tabla is a later legend rather than a documented fact [@src.miner-1993-sitar-and-sarod; @src.kippen-1988-tabla-of-lucknow].
Scholars disagree about the real origins of the tabla.
Some look to Indian drums depicted in earlier sculpture, others to West Asian influence, and the tradition itself tells several stories [@src.kippen-1988-tabla-of-lucknow].
What can be said safely is that the modern tabla is well documented only from about the 18th and 19th centuries.
