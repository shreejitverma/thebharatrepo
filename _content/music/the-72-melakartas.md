Hindustani music, as the companion article on the ten thaats shows, picked ten parent scales from a larger set.
Carnatic music, the classical tradition of South India, did something bolder: it wrote down every possible parent scale, gave each a name and a number, and arranged them in a grid.
The result is the system of the 72 *melakartas* (mēḷakartā, "creators of scales").
It is one of the neatest pieces of theory in world music, and it can be understood with nothing more than counting [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india; @src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems].

## The ingredients: sixteen note names on twelve pitches

Carnatic theory uses seven note names: Sa, Ri, Ga, Ma, Pa, Da, Ni.
Sa and Pa are fixed.
Ma comes in two forms (*shuddha*, natural, and *prati*, raised), and Ri, Ga, Da and Ni each have three named forms.
That makes 1 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 16 note names [@src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems].

Within one octave, however, there are only twelve distinct semitone positions, so some names land on the same pitch.
In the idealised twelve-step picture (real intonation is more subtle, see the note below), counting semitones above Sa:

| Position | Name(s) |
|---|---|
| 0 | Sa |
| 1 | Ri1 (shuddha Ri) |
| 2 | Ri2 (chatushruti Ri) and Ga1 (shuddha Ga) |
| 3 | Ri3 (shatshruti Ri) and Ga2 (sadharana Ga) |
| 4 | Ga3 (antara Ga) |
| 5 | Ma1 (shuddha Ma) |
| 6 | Ma2 (prati Ma) |
| 7 | Pa |
| 8 | Da1 (shuddha Da) |
| 9 | Da2 (chatushruti Da) and Ni1 (shuddha Ni) |
| 10 | Da3 (shatshruti Da) and Ni2 (kaishiki Ni) |
| 11 | Ni3 (kakali Ni) |

The shared pitches are the secret.
The tradition keeps the names apart, and a scale has to use one name of each kind, in order.

## The rule that generates 72

A *melakarta* is a seven-note scale that has exactly one note of each name, Sa, Ri, Ga, Ma, Pa, Da, Ni, in rising order, in both ascent and descent.
Now count the choices [@src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems; @src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india]:

1. **Ma** has two choices: Ma1 or Ma2.
2. **Ri and Ga** together have six valid choices. Ri must lie below Ga, and the two must not be the same pitch. That rules out Ri2 with Ga1 (same pitch), Ri3 with Ga2 (same pitch) and Ri3 with Ga1 (wrong order). The six that remain are Ri1 Ga1, Ri1 Ga2, Ri1 Ga3, Ri2 Ga2, Ri2 Ga3 and Ri3 Ga3.
3. **Da and Ni** follow exactly the same pattern, which again gives six.

So the number of scales is 2 x 6 x 6 = **72**.

That is the whole idea.
Seventy-two is not a number picked for its symbolism: it is the count of scales you get when you apply one rule consistently.
A key sense in which the scheme is "complete" is that every seven-note scale with this note-naming rule appears somewhere in the grid.

A note on intonation: the table uses equal semitones for clarity.
Carnatic theory in practice also discusses finer microtonal positions (*shrutis*) and the gliding ornaments (*gamakas*) that make a note sound like itself, so the twelve-step picture is a simplification of how the scales sound [@src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems].

## The grid: twelve chakras of six

The 72 are arranged in twelve groups of six called *chakras* (wheels).
The rule is simple:

- The first 36 scales (1 to 36) use Ma1, the next 36 (37 to 72) use Ma2, so the second half is the first half with a raised fourth.
- Within each half there are six chakras, one for each Ri-Ga combination, in the order given above.
- Within each chakra the six scales run through the six Da-Ni combinations in the same order every time.

The chakras are named with words that double as numbers, using the old Sanskrit habit of "object numerals" in which a familiar word stands for a number (the moon is one, eyes are two) [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india; @src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].

| Chakra | Name | Meaning of number word | Scales | Ri-Ga |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Indu | moon (1) | 1 to 6 | Ri1 Ga1 |
| 2 | Netra | eyes (2) | 7 to 12 | Ri1 Ga2 |
| 3 | Agni | fires (3) | 13 to 18 | Ri1 Ga3 |
| 4 | Veda | Vedas (4) | 19 to 24 | Ri2 Ga2 |
| 5 | Bana | arrows (5) | 25 to 30 | Ri2 Ga3 |
| 6 | Ritu | seasons (6) | 31 to 36 | Ri3 Ga3 |
| 7 | Rishi | sages (7) | 37 to 42 | Ri1 Ga1 |
| 8 | Vasu | Vasus (8) | 43 to 48 | Ri1 Ga2 |
| 9 | Brahma | Brahmas (9) | 49 to 54 | Ri1 Ga3 |
| 10 | Disi | directions (10) | 55 to 60 | Ri2 Ga2 |
| 11 | Rudra | Rudras (11) | 61 to 66 | Ri2 Ga3 |
| 12 | Aditya | Adityas (12) | 67 to 72 | Ri3 Ga3 |

Chakras 7 to 12 repeat the Ri-Ga pairs of 1 to 6, but with Ma2.
Within every chakra, the six scales have these Da-Ni pairs in order: Da1 Ni1, Da1 Ni2, Da1 Ni3, Da2 Ni2, Da2 Ni3, Da3 Ni3.

## A formula for the scale number

Because the grid is regular, the number of any scale can be computed.
Let *m* be 0 for Ma1 and 1 for Ma2.
Let *c* be the position of its Ri-Ga pair in the list of six (0 to 5), and *d* the position of its Da-Ni pair (0 to 5).
Then the scale number is 36m + 6c + d + 1.

Check: Mechakalyani has Ma2 (m = 1), Ri2 Ga3 (c = 4) and Da2 Ni3 (d = 4).
So 36 + 24 + 4 + 1 = 65, which is indeed its number.
One consequence: the scale that is 36 higher than a Ma1 scale is the same scale with the Ma raised.
Dheerasankarabharanam (29) becomes Mechakalyani (65) [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].

## The Katapayadi code: names that carry their number

The scales were given names such that the number can be read off the first two syllables.
This uses the *kaṭapayādi* system, an old Indian method of encoding numbers in letters, found in astronomical and mathematical writing and well known in Kerala [@src.plofker-2009-mathematics-in-india].
The consonants are assigned digits:

| Digit | ka-group | ṭa-group | pa-group | ya-group |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ka | ṭa | pa | ya |
| 2 | kha | ṭha | pha | ra |
| 3 | ga | ḍa | ba | la |
| 4 | gha | ḍha | bha | va |
| 5 | ṅa | ṇa | ma | śa |
| 6 | ca | ta | - | ṣa |
| 7 | cha | tha | - | sa |
| 8 | ja | da | - | ha |
| 9 | jha | dha | - | - |
| 0 | ña | na | - | - |

The rule has two steps:

1. Take the first two consonants of the name (in a conjunct, the last consonant counts) and convert each to its digit.
2. Reverse them, because "numbers go from right to left" (*ankanam vamato gatih*): the first digit is the units, the second the tens.

Some examples [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india]:

| Name | Consonants | Digits | Reversed | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kanakangi | ka, na | 1, 0 | 01 | 1 |
| Hanumatodi | ha, na | 8, 0 | 08 | 8 |
| Mayamalavagowla | ma, ya | 5, 1 | 15 | 15 |
| Kharaharapriya | kha, ra | 2, 2 | 22 | 22 |
| Harikambhoji | ha, ra | 8, 2 | 28 | 28 |
| Dheerasankarabharanam | dha, ra | 9, 2 | 29 | 29 |
| Mechakalyani | ma, ca | 5, 6 | 65 | 65 |

So a musician who hears the name Kharaharapriya can recover its number and, from the formula, its notes, without a table.
This is a beautiful fusion of a mathematical grid with Sanskrit word-craft.

## Who built it?

The history is layered, and sources describe the layers with varying detail, so it helps to separate them [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india; @src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems].

- **Earlier mela schemes.** The idea of grouping ragas under a parent scale (*mela*) is older than the 72-scale grid. Sixteenth-century writers such as Ramamatya listed a smaller number of melas based on ragas actually in use.
- **Venkatamakhin (17th century).** In the *Chaturdandiprakashika* he is credited with the systematic derivation of all 72 parent scales from the note-naming rule, and with noting that only some of them then had well-established ragas. His work comes from the Thanjavur region in the century of the Nayaka rulers.
- **Govindacharya.** The names that carry their number through the Katapayadi code, which are the names used today, are generally attributed to a later author, Govindacharya, whose *Sangrahachudamani* is dated to the late 18th or early 19th century.

This means the common phrase "the Venkatamakhin scheme" is shorthand: the structure belongs to the 17th century, but the modern names and numbering are later [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
Sources differ on details of the intermediate steps, and the authors of the main reference works are cautious about precise dates, so this article states centuries, not years.

## From parent scales to ragas

A melakarta is a theoretical parent, in the way the thaat is in Hindustani music.
The ragas people actually perform are mostly *janya* ragas, "derived" ones, which draw some of their notes from a parent scale.
A janya may omit notes, zigzag, or add a note from outside the parent [@src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems].
Many of the best-known parent scales are also performed as ragas in their own right, as Dheerasankarabharanam, Kharaharapriya and Hanumatodi are.

Some melakartas are rarely used, because their notes sit awkwardly together.
The grid is complete by construction, and not every cell is a musical favourite.
That is the difference between a system built to enumerate, as this one is, and a system built to catalogue practice, as the thaats are.

## Take-away

- The count of 72 is the product 2 x 6 x 6: two Ma, six Ri-Ga pairs, six Da-Ni pairs.
- They fall in twelve chakras of six, with a regular formula for the scale number.
- The Katapayadi code lets a name reveal its number.
- The structure goes back to the 17th century; the names and numbers we use were assigned later.
