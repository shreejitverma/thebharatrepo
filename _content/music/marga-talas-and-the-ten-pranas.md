Modern talas such as Adi or Teentaal are the visible tip of a very old theory.
Sanskrit writers on music, from the Natyashastra of Bharata onward, treated tala as a science of time with its own units, its own families and its own mathematics.
This article sets out what that older theory said, and corrects some popular claims that have gathered around it.

## Time measured in matras

The older theory measures time in matras (mātrā).
A matra is defined by the speech organs: the time taken to pronounce five short syllables [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana; @src.katz-1987-sangitanarayana].
Tala is built from angas (limbs) whose lengths are fixed multiples of that unit.
The values given by the Sangitaratnakara of Sarngadeva (13th century) and by earlier and later treatises are [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana; @src.katz-1987-sangitanarayana]:

| Anga | Length in matras |
|---|---|
| Druta | one half |
| Laghu | 1 |
| Guru | 2 |
| Pluta | 3 |

A smaller unit, the anudruta ("after the druta", half a druta), is described in the 17th-century Sangitanarayana and in later texts.
Rao notes that Sarngadeva himself does not mention it, and that it became an independent limb in the Vijayanagara period [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana; @src.katz-1987-sangitanarayana].
The word "anga" for these limbs is likewise missing from Sarngadeva and earlier texts [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana].

The older and newer systems must not be confused here.
In today's Carnatic practice a laghu is a variable count (3 to 9) and the other limbs are counted relative to it.
In the older theory a laghu is one matra and a guru two.
Some popular summaries give the old limbs as 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 16 beats; those numbers mix the two systems and are not what these sources say.
A sixth limb, kakapada, is sometimes added, but I could not confirm its value in the sources consulted.

## The five marga talas

"Marga" means "path" and names the classical, ritual-theatrical stream of music, as against "desi", the music of regions and of the day.
Five marga talas are listed in the early tradition: Caccatputa, Cacaputa, Satpitaputraka, Sampakvestaka and Udghatta [@src.katz-1987-sangitanarayana].
The Sangitanarayana gives their limb structures as follows, and the totals are my own arithmetic from the table above:

| Tala | Limbs | Total |
|---|---|---|
| Caccatputa | guru, guru, laghu, pluta | 2+2+1+3 = 8 matras |
| Cacaputa | guru, laghu, laghu, guru | 2+1+1+2 = 6 matras |
| Satpitaputraka | pluta, laghu, guru, guru, laghu, pluta | 3+1+2+2+1+3 = 12 matras |
| Sampakvestaka | pluta, three gurus, pluta | 3+6+3 = 12 matras |
| Udghatta | laghu, three gurus | 1+6 = 7 matras |

Treatises differ in the exact forms of these talas, so this table gives one text's reading and not the only one [@src.katz-1987-sangitanarayana].
It is also common to read that each marga tala belongs to a particular god (Brahma, Vishnu, Shiva and so on).
That association is not found in the sources consulted for this article, and it is left out.

## Desi talas and the question of 108

Sarngadeva divides music into marga and desi, and his tala chapter describes both [@src.katz-1987-sangitanarayana].
Later compilers extended the catalogue of desi talas.
The Sangitanarayana, for instance, supplements the talas used in practice with a list of 101 more, which it calls unsuitable for performance and gives "for the delight of connoisseurs" [@src.katz-1987-sangitanarayana].

The figure 108 is often quoted for "the classical talas", and so is a claim that one of them, Simhanandana, has 128 beats.
Neither figure could be verified in the sources consulted here.
The Sangitanarayana lists Simhanandana as a sequence of limbs which comes to about 28 matras on my count (two gurus, laghu, pluta, laghu, guru, two drutas, laghu, pluta, laghu, pluta, guru, two laghus and four silent laghus), and the transmitted verse is textually uncertain.
One manuscript reading in its apparatus gives 32 matras [@src.katz-1987-sangitanarayana].
It is a speculation of mine, not a finding, that 128 might come from reckoning 32 matras at four aksharas each; the source does not say so.
The safest statement is that the older tradition knew a very large family of talas, that treatises list different numbers of them, and that the round figure 108 has not been traced to a text in this research.

## The ten pranas

A later stage of theory summed up everything a tala involves in ten "vital elements", the tala-dasa-prana.
They are kala, marga, kriya, anga, graha, jati, kala (again), laya, yati and prastara [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana].
Despite common statements, the scheme is not a feature of Sarngadeva's 13th-century text.
Rao shows that earlier works, Sarngadeva's among them, describe most of the individual concepts, but that the ten were grouped together under that name for the first time in the Taladipika, written around 1474 at the Vijayanagara court, with the terms anga and jati added then [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana].
A verse often quoted for the list and attributed to the Sangitamakaranda could not be checked here.

In brief, following Rao's survey of Sarngadeva and his successors [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana]:

1. **Kala** (the first): the measure of time. Two ways of fixing it appear, one through the matra, the other through a ladder of tiny units (ksana, lava, kastha, nimisa), with the ksana pictured as the time taken to pierce a hundred lotus petals.
2. **Marga**: the "path" or manner of rendering, in which the time units are stretched. Sarngadeva's four margas are defined by the length of the kala (guru): dakshina 8 matras, vartika 4, citra 2 and dhruva 1. The Sangitacudamani adds finer, faster ones (citratara, citratama, aticitratama).
3. **Kriya**: the hand action. Sarngadeva names four sounded actions (dhruva, samya, tala, sannipata) and four silent ones (avapa, nishkrama, vikshepa, pravesha).
4. **Anga**: the limbs described above.
5. **Graha**: where the tala begins in relation to the song or instrument. It is sama when both start together, anagata when the tala starts first and atita when the song starts first.
6. **Jati**: the class of tala. In the older texts the five names (caturasra, tryasra, khanda, misra, sankirna) classify the marga talas by how many gurus they contain, not the length of one laghu as in later Carnatic teaching.
7. **Kala** (the second): the number of kalas (gurus) in a tala, which doubles in the ekakala, dvikala and catushkala forms.
8. **Laya**: tempo, defined as the rest following an action, in three kinds: druta, madhya and vilamba.
9. **Yati**: the rules for changing laya. Sama keeps the laya the same, srotogata moves from slow to medium to fast, and gopuccha is the opposite.
10. **Prastara**: the systematic breaking of a unit such as a guru into smaller units (a guru into two laghus, a laghu into two drutas), listing every resulting pattern. It comes with tools to count the patterns (sankhya), to find the pattern for a given number (nashta) and the number for a given pattern (uddishta), and with tables called meru.

Several popular accounts differ from this on the details.
The anecdotal yati shapes found in lists, such as the "river" or "hourglass drum" outlines, are later additions and were not found in the sources consulted; numerical examples of phrase lengths (6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 and so on) are likewise unsupported here.
The interpretation of jati as the count of a laghu also belongs to the later system, and the same word therefore carries two meanings across the centuries [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana].

## What the older theory still teaches

The older theory is not a museum piece.
Several of its ideas, such as graha (the entry point of a melody within a cycle), laya and yati, are used every day by performers under the same or slightly changed names.
The prastara tradition, with its counting and look-up methods, is a striking early piece of combinatorial thinking, applied to rhythm [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana].
The two living traditions that grew from this heritage are described in the companion articles on Hindustani tala and the Carnatic tala system [@src.clayton-2000-time-in-indian-music; @src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
