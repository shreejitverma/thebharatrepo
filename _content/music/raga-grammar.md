Ask a musician what a raga is and you will usually get a story rather than a definition.
It is a mood, a personality, a family of melodies that share a character.
Ask a theorist and you will get a list of rules.
Both answers are correct, and the interesting part is how the rules produce the personality.
A raga is not simply a scale, because two ragas can use exactly the same seven notes and still sound entirely different [@src.powers-2001-india-raga].
What separates them is grammar: which notes are stressed, which are touched lightly, where phrases begin and end, and which little turns of phrase are essential.

This article walks through that grammar.
It keeps apart three layers that are often blended together: the ancient doctrine, the vocabulary of modern Hindustani teaching, and the conventions that only some traditions follow.

## A raga is a recipe, not a ladder

Imagine two cooks with the same seven ingredients.
One makes a soup, the other a stew.
The ingredients (the notes) are the same; the proportions, the order of adding them and the finishing touches differ.
A raga works the same way.
Its notes are drawn from the seven svaras: Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni (shadja, rishabha, gandhara, madhyama, panchama, dhaivata, nishada), each of which may sit in a natural or an altered form.
The raga then specifies a path through them [@src.powers-2001-india-raga; @src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems].

The word raga itself (rāga, from a root meaning "to colour") is old in the technical sense.
In the early medieval treatise *Brihaddeshi*, attributed to Matanga, a raga is already described as a melodic form that "colours" the mind, and the later tradition kept that image [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india; @src.widdess-1995-ragas-early-indian-music].
Scholars place that treatise somewhere in the first millennium CE, and the exact century is disputed, which is why this article treats its date as a range and not a fact.

## The ten features in the old treatises

The most important early checklist for describing a raga appears in the medieval Sanskrit treatises, most fully in Sarngadeva's *Sangitaratnakara* of the 13th century, which draws on Matanga.
It lists ten features (the *dasha lakshana*), and it is worth knowing them because the modern vocabulary is a simplified descendant of this list [@src.widdess-1995-ragas-early-indian-music; @src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].

| Feature | Meaning in plain terms |
|---|---|
| graha | the note on which a melodic statement begins |
| amsha | the dominant or "sounding" note of the raga |
| tara and mandra | how far the raga reaches into the upper and lower registers |
| nyasa and apanyasa | the notes on which phrases come to rest, final and intermediate |
| alpatva | notes used sparingly |
| bahutva | notes used abundantly |
| shadava and audava | whether notes are omitted, leaving six or five |

Two things are worth noticing.
First, this is a list of tendencies, a description of how a raga behaves in practice, not a mathematical definition.
Second, the central idea of the modern system, the dominant note, is already here as the *amsha*.
Where early treatises list ten features, a modern teacher will usually name only a handful: the ascent and descent, the vadi and samvadi, and the pakad.

## Ascent and descent

The most basic rule of a raga is its *arohana* (ascent) and *avarohana* (descent).
Together they say which notes may be used going up and going down.
In many ragas the two are identical apart from direction; in others they differ, and the difference is part of the raga's identity [@src.powers-2001-india-raga].

The count of notes in each direction gives the raga's *jati* (class).
A raga with five notes is *audava*, with six *shadava*, with seven *sampurna*.
Because ascent and descent are counted separately, there are nine combinations.
Some well-known examples [@src.powers-2001-india-raga]:

| Raga | Ascent | Descent | Class |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bhupali | 5 notes (Sa Re Ga Pa Dha) | 5 notes | audava-audava |
| Malkauns | 5 notes (Sa, komal Ga, Ma, komal Dha, komal Ni) | 5 notes | audava-audava |
| Khamaj | 6 notes (Re omitted) | 7 notes | shadava-sampurna |
| Marwa | 6 notes (Pa omitted) | 6 notes | shadava-shadava |
| Bhairav | 7 notes | 7 notes | sampurna-sampurna |

Note that the word *jati* has an older, different meaning in the *Natyashastra*, where it names an ancient family of melodic types.
The modern jati of a raga, based on note counts, is a later usage that shares the name [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].

Some ragas do not move in a straight line.
They zigzag, for example by stepping back before going forward.
Such a path is called *vakra* (crooked), and it is one reason a raga cannot be reduced to a list of notes: the same notes in a different order are a different raga.

## Vadi and samvadi: the king, the minister and the rest

The best-known pair of terms is *vadi* and *samvadi*.
In modern Hindustani teaching the vadi is the most important note of the raga, the one the musician lingers on and returns to.
The samvadi is the second most important, standing in a consonant relationship with the vadi, usually a fourth or a fifth away.
Teachers often use the image of a king (vadi) and a minister (samvadi), with the other notes as the court.

The words are old but their modern meaning has shifted.
In the *Natyashastra*, *vadi*, *samvadi*, *anuvadi* and *vivadi* name relationships between notes, and the royal imagery of king, minister, servant and enemy comes from there: notes a certain number of microtonal steps (*shrutis*) apart were called *samvadi*, meaning consonant, and the notes in such a pair could "speak to" each other [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
The modern sense, where one note is the raga's dominant, grew out of that vocabulary in later centuries [@src.powers-2001-india-raga].

The idea that one note is "the" vadi is a useful teaching device, but it is not as simple as it sounds.
Study of how musicians actually perform shows that note importance comes from several overlapping factors, such as how often a note is sung, how long it is held, and where it falls in a phrase.
The ethnomusicologist N. A. Jairazbhoy examined these factors in North Indian ragas, treating the stated vadi as something to be tested against practice [@src.jairazbhoy-1972-important-notes].
Anyone learning from a teacher should therefore treat vadi and samvadi as helpful labels, and listen for what the musician actually emphasises.

## The pakad: a raga's signature

The *pakad* (literally "catch") is the short phrase, or set of phrases, by which a listener, or an examiner, can recognise a raga.
It is the melodic equivalent of a few bars of a famous song.
A student who plays the notes of a raga in order has shown knowledge of its scale; a student who plays its pakad has shown that they know how it moves.

The pakad is a modern teaching term rather than a medieval one, and it fits the general trend of the modern period: reducing a rich oral tradition to compact, teachable, notatable units [@src.bakhle-2005-two-men-and-music].
The medieval equivalent of the idea is spread across the ten features above.
The pakad should not be confused with the raga itself.
The raga is the whole set of tendencies; the pakad is its shortest fingerprint.

## Time of day: the *samay* convention

Hindustani musicians have a strong convention that each raga belongs to a particular time of day or night, and that performing it at the right hour deepens its effect.
Dawn ragas are sung before sunrise, late-night ragas after midnight, and so on.
The cycle is usually described as eight three-hour watches (*prahara*).

Three rules of thumb are commonly taught, and they are worth stating because they show that the convention has an internal logic [@src.powers-2001-india-raga]:

1. **Twilight ragas (*sandhiprakasha*).** Ragas for the junctions of day and night, dawn and dusk, tend to use komal (flat) Re and komal Dha. At dawn the Ma is natural, as in Bhairav. At dusk it is the sharp *tivra* Ma, as in Marwa and Poorvi.
2. **Position of the vadi.** Ragas whose dominant note lies in the lower half of the scale (Sa to Ma) are sung in the half of the cycle from noon to midnight. Ragas whose dominant lies in the upper half (Pa to the upper Sa) belong to the half from midnight to noon.
3. **The role of Madhyama.** The natural Ma tends to mark daytime ragas, and the sharp Ma marks evening and night.

How old is this? The convention is not a feature of the early treatises in the way the ten features are.
It was set out in its present systematic form in the modern period, and it applies chiefly to Hindustani music; Carnatic practice does not treat time of day as a rule of performance in the same way [@src.powers-2001-india-raga; @src.bakhle-2005-two-men-and-music].
Even within Hindustani music there are many exceptions, and modern concert practice (afternoon recitals of "midnight" ragas, recordings, radio) has loosened it.
Claims that the time rules reflect measurable effects on the body or the hormones are common in popular writing, but they are not supported by the music-theoretical literature cited here, so they are left out.

## Mood: raga and rasa

Indian aesthetics speaks of nine moods or *rasas*: the erotic, the comic, the compassionate, the furious, the heroic, the terrible, the odious, the wondrous and the peaceful.
The idea of rasa comes from the *Natyashastra*, where it is a theory of drama, and it was later applied to music [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
Many musicians and listeners associate particular ragas with particular moods: a slow, flat-noted raga may feel sad, a bright one joyful.

These associations are real in the sense that they are widely shared inside the tradition, but the exact table that assigns each raga one rasa differs from school to school.
This article does not give such a table, because no single standard assignment exists to cite.

## What to take away

- A raga is defined by its notes and by how they are used: ascent and descent, emphasis, resting points, characteristic phrases.
- The old treatises described a raga by ten features; the modern vocabulary (vadi, samvadi, pakad) is a compact later descendant of that list.
- The time-of-day convention is a Hindustani system with internal rules, not an ancient universal law, and it is a convention that musicians increasingly treat flexibly.
- Labels such as "vadi" are guides for learning; listening to a master shows how much more there is.
