## Parents and children

Think of a family tree for melodies.
In Carnatic (South Indian classical) music, the **melakarta** ragas are the parents: 72 scales, each with all seven note names in ascending order, arranged in a regular table. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
Almost everything a concert actually sings, however, is a **janya** (जन्य, "born of") raga, one derived from a parent. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music; @src.powers-2001-mode-in-south-asia-raga]
No reliable count is available, but janya ragas make up the large majority of the concert repertoire.

A janya is not just a scale with a label.
It keeps its own phrases and its own shape of movement, and it is judged by those, not only by its note list. [@src.powers-widdess-2001-india-theory-practice]

## Classification by notes: varja and krama

A raga that leaves out one or more notes of its parent in ascent or descent is called **varja** (वर्ज्य, "omitted").
Ascent and descent are counted separately, and a raga is named by the count of notes: seven is *sampurna*, six *shadava*, five *audava*, and four *svarantara*. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]

| Pattern (ascent / descent) | Example raga | Parent melakarta |
|---|---|---|
| 7 / 7 | Bhairavi | Natabhairavi (no. 20) |
| 6 / 7 | Kambhoji | Harikambhoji (no. 28) |
| 5 / 7 | Bilahari | Shankarabharanam (no. 29) |
| 6 / 6 | Sriranjani | Kharaharapriya (no. 22) |
| 5 / 5 | Mohanam | Harikambhoji (no. 28) |
| 5 / 5 | Hamsadhvani | Shankarabharanam (no. 29) |

The assignment of a raga to a parent is partly a matter of convention: Mohanam uses notes that belong to several melakartas, and it is conventionally placed under Harikambhoji. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
The table lists only examples whose patterns are well established.

To make Kambhoji concrete, its ascent is S R2 G3 M1 P D2 S', with no N, and its descent is S' N2 D2 P M1 G3 R2 S. In plain terms, singers go up without the seventh note and come down with it. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]

## Classification by movement: vakra

A raga is **vakra** (वक्र, "crooked") if its ascent or descent does not move in a strict straight line but takes a detour, going back a step before going forward again. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
Ragas whose lines are straight are called *krama* ("orderly").
Vakra may affect the ascent only, the descent only, or both, and ragas such as Sahana, Anandabhairavi and Reetigaula are often cited as examples of winding motion.
Full note-by-note patterns are omitted here because they differ between sources.

## Classification by notes outside the parent: upanga and bhashanga

Here is the most interesting distinction.
An **upanga** raga uses only notes belonging to its parent melakarta. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
A **bhashanga** raga adds, in particular phrases, a note that is not in the parent. That foreign note is called an *anya svara*. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
The foreign note is not used freely: it belongs to certain phrases, and without it the raga loses its identity.

| Raga | Parent | Type | Foreign note (anya svara) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mohanam | Harikambhoji | Upanga | none |
| Hamsadhvani | Shankarabharanam | Upanga | none |
| Kambhoji | Harikambhoji | Bhashanga | Kakali nishada (N3), in certain descending phrases |
| Bilahari | Shankarabharanam | Bhashanga | Kaisiki nishada (N2) |
| Bhairavi | Natabhairavi | Bhashanga | Chatushruti dhaivata (D2) |

Other ragas, such as Anandabhairavi, Mukhari and Begada, are also described as bhashanga, but the foreign notes and phrases involved are not listed here.

## The five Ghana ragas

Carnatic tradition singles out five ragas, called the **Ghana ragas** (घन, "dense" or "weighty"): Nattai, Gaula, Arabhi, Varali and Sri. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
They are linked to a particular style of rendering a raga in a rhythmic, flowing improvisation called *tanam*.
The composer Tyagaraja (1767 to 1847) wrote a set of five celebrated songs, the Pancharatna kritis ("five gems"), in these five ragas. [@src.jackson-1992-features-of-the-kriti]

| Raga | Parent melakarta | Pancharatna kriti |
|---|---|---|
| Nattai | Chalanata (no. 36) | Jagadanandakaraka |
| Gaula | Mayamalavagowla (no. 15) | Dudukugala Nanne |
| Arabhi | Shankarabharanam (no. 29) | Sadhinchane O Manasa |
| Varali | Jhalavarali (no. 39) | Kana Kana Ruchira |
| Sri | Kharaharapriya (no. 22) | Endaro Mahanubhavulu |

Scale patterns are deliberately omitted: old ragas such as Gaula and Arabhi are sung with distinctive phrases that a bare note list misrepresents.

## Other groupings

Older literature also sorts ragas by octave range, by emotional directness ("rakti") and by borrowing from other traditions ("desiya"). The specific examples vary by author and are not listed here.

## A caution

Every classification above is a way of talking about ragas, invented after the music existed, and different authors draw the lines differently.
The sensible approach is to treat the labels as a vocabulary for describing a raga's behaviour and to let the music have the last word. [@src.powers-widdess-2001-india-theory-practice]
