Carnatic music, also spelled Karnatak, is the classical music of South India.
It is practised across Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Kerala, and in diaspora communities around the world [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india; @src.arnold-2000-garland-south-asia].
Like Hindustani music of the north, it rests on two ideas: a melodic framework called *raga* and a cycle of beats called *tala*.
What makes it distinctive is the way a performer combines a fixed, composed repertoire with improvisation inside the rules of the raga.

## Old roots, modern form

The ideas behind Carnatic music are old, but the music you hear in a concert hall today is not an unbroken survival from antiquity.
Sanskrit treatises on music and drama, above all the *Natyashastra*, discuss scales, the division of the octave into small intervals (*shruti*) and the grammar of melody, and they are the common ancestor of both North and South Indian practice [@src.rowell-1992-music-and-musical-thought-early-india].
The chanting of the Samaveda, which sets verses to a small set of tones, is another very early layer of Indian musical thought, preserved by oral transmission [@src.rowell-1992-music-and-musical-thought-early-india; @src.unesco-2003-tradition-vedic-chanting].

The idea of the *raga* as a named melodic type, with its own characteristic phrases and mood, emerges in the first millennium CE.
Richard Widdess traces how older "modes" (*jati*) gave way to ragas in texts and inscriptions between the Gupta period and about 1250 CE, a story that ends with Sarngadeva's *Sangitaratnakara*, a 13th-century work that both North and South Indian traditions claim as their own [@src.widdess-1995-ragas-of-early-indian-music].
Inscriptions that record musical notation, such as the one at Kudumiyanmalai in Tamil Nadu (roughly 7th to 8th century), show that raga practice was already a written art in the south [@src.widdess-1995-ragas-of-early-indian-music].

### When did North and South diverge?

An older version of this article said that Hindustani music "evolved as a distinct form around the 13th century".
That is too precise.
Surveys describe a gradual divergence over the second millennium CE, as courts in the north absorbed Persianate influences and courts in the south, such as the Vijayanagara and later Maratha courts at Thanjavur, kept developing the Sanskrit-based theory [@src.arnold-2000-garland-south-asia; @src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
There is no single date, and the two traditions still share much vocabulary.

## The Trinity and the 18th-century repertoire

The core repertoire of Carnatic concerts comes largely from three composers who lived in or near Thanjavur district in the Kaveri delta, usually called the Trinity [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india]:

- **Tyagaraja** (1767-1847) composed devotional songs, mostly in Telugu, many addressed to Rama.
- **Muthuswami Dikshitar** (about 1775-1835; sources vary by a year) composed mainly in Sanskrit, often in slow, grave, veena-like melodic style, and wrote cycles of songs on temple deities.
- **Shyama Sastri** (1762-1827) composed in Telugu, Sanskrit and Tamil, and is remembered for songs with subtle rhythmic structure and for the *swarajati* form.

Popular accounts say Tyagaraja composed tens of thousands of songs.
Such large numbers are traditional claims and are not documented; what survives in printed collections is a few hundred compositions, and the exact count depends on the edition [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].

The phrase "golden age of the 18th century" is also a retrospective judgement.
Historians of the period show that the Trinity were later raised to canonical status, as part of a 20th-century effort to define a "classical" tradition with a pedigree [@src.subramanian-2006-tanjore-court-madras-music-academy; @src.weidman-2006-singing-the-classical].

## Forms and improvisation

The central composed form is the *kriti*.
A kriti usually has a *pallavi* (opening refrain), an *anupallavi* (second part) and one or more *charanam* verses, though many songs depart from this plan [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
Rhythmically, each song is set in a *tala*, a repeating cycle of beats counted with hand claps and waves.

Improvisation, called *manodharma* ("what the mind creates"), is where performers show their craft.
It takes several forms [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india]:

- **Alapana:** an unmetered exploration of a raga, note by note, phrase by phrase.
- **Tanam:** a flowing, pulsed passage with no fixed tala, usually on the veena or sung with syllables.
- **Niraval and kalpanaswara:** improvisation on a line of a composition, and improvised passages sung with the note names (*sa ri ga ma pa dha ni*), both returning to a fixed point in the cycle.
- **Tani avartanam:** a solo by the percussionists, usually the *mridangam* with the *ghatam* or *kanjira*.

The raga itself is not a scale alone.
It carries characteristic ornaments (*gamaka*), phrases that may be used or avoided, and conventions about emphasis [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
Ragas are commonly organised in teaching by a scheme of parent scales called *melakarta*, a systematisation that belongs to the later part of this history [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].

## Instruments of the concert

A typical concert has a lead vocalist, a drone instrument (the *tambura*), a violin, and a *mridangam* [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
The *violin* is a European import that was adopted in South India in the 18th and 19th centuries.
It does not simply play in unison with the singer: it follows and answers the voice, and Weidman argues that the Carnatic violin was shaped to imitate vocal style [@src.weidman-2006-singing-the-classical].
The *veena* (the South Indian *saraswati veena*) is a plucked instrument with a long tradition as both a solo and an accompanying instrument, and the *flute*, *ghatam* and *kanjira* often join [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
Mridangam makers, who work with animal skins and wood, have long been drawn from communities with little social standing, a fact that T. M. Krishna argues has been erased in the tradition's story of itself [@src.krishna-2020-sebastian-and-sons].
For more on instruments, see the companion article on musical instruments of India.

## The 20th-century concert and who made it

The Carnatic concert as we know it, with a fixed order of items and a structured main piece, took shape in the early 20th century.
Lakshmi Subramanian shows that this was linked to the move of patronage from princely courts to urban middle-class institutions such as the Madras Music Academy, founded in the late 1920s [@src.subramanian-2006-tanjore-court-madras-music-academy].
Amanda Weidman and Davesh Soneji show that this "classicisation" also had social costs, as hereditary performers, especially temple and court women performers (*devadasis*), were pushed to the margins of the new classical stage [@src.weidman-2006-singing-the-classical; @src.soneji-2012-unfinished-gestures].
Understanding the music well means holding both facts: the artistry is real and deep, and the canon we inherit is a product of choices made in particular times.
