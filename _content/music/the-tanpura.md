Next time you hear a recording of Hindustani or Carnatic music, listen below the singer.
A soft, endless chord that never changes is turning over and over: that is the tanpura (also written tambura or tanpuri).
Nobody plays a tune on it.
Its job is to give the musicians, and the listener, a fixed point to hear every note against.

## Why a drone?

Indian melody is built on a home note, Sa, and its relation to the other notes (see the article on shrutis and svaras).
Because Sa and Pa are the two "unmoving" notes of the system [@src.shringy-1978-sangitaratnakara-vol1], a drone that sounds Sa and Pa continuously gives every raga a stable reference.
A singer who slides to a note can judge whether it is right by how it sounds against the drone.
That is a practical explanation.
There is also a philosophical one, discussed at the end of this article.

## How it is built and tuned

The usual concert tanpura has four strings, though some have five or six.
They are plucked one after another in a steady cycle, each allowed to ring into the next.
The standard Hindustani tuning in a Pa-based raga is [@src.ghosh-2017-tanpura-drone-brain-response]:

| String | Pitch |
|---|---|
| 1 (outer) | Pa, a fifth above Sa |
| 2 and 3 (middle pair) | Sa, in unison |
| 4 (thick, inner) | Sa, one octave lower |

When a raga omits or avoids Pa, performers often replace the first string with Madhyama (Ma) or a lower Ni, so that the drone does not clash with the mode.
Which note is chosen for which raga is a matter of performance practice and varies by teacher and school, so no rigid list is given here.
Carnatic practice uses the same idea, with the instrument called tambura.
The overall pitch of the Sa is chosen to suit the singer or the instrument: lower for many male voices, higher for many female voices.

The instrument has a long neck, a large hollow resonator (traditionally a dried gourd, though some Carnatic instruments are carved from a single block of wood), and a flat, wide bridge.
The bridge is where the interesting acoustics happen.

## The jivari: why it buzzes

Tanpura players call the bridge's special effect *jivari* (or *javari*; the word means roughly "giving life").
The bridge top is wide and gently curved.
A cotton or silk thread is slipped between each string and the bridge, and moved a hair's breadth by the tuner until the sound "opens".

The physics, in plain terms, is this.
On a guitar the string ends at a sharp point, so its vibration is dominated by a few low partials.
On a tanpura the string, as it swings, touches the curved bridge a little further along on each cycle, so its effective length shortens and lengthens as it vibrates.
That repeated grazing contact is a non-linear process, which transfers energy from the fundamental up to many higher partials, so the spectrum grows rich in overtones that decay in a characteristic order [@src.mandal-2015-strings-against-obstacle; @src.vyasarayani-2009-unilateral-constraint-string].
Published physical models of the closely related sitar string, which has a similar curved bridge, show how bridge curvature and string-bridge contact shape this behaviour [@src.siddiq-2012-nonlinear-sitar-string].
These studies concern sitar and vina-type strings and curved obstacles in general; they are a good guide to the tanpura but not a direct measurement of every tanpura.

What the listener hears is not individual strings but a gently moving cloud of overtones.
As the harmonics line up and drift, the ear can pick out "phantom" higher notes that no string is sounding.
This is a perceptual effect of the harmonic series.
Players speak of the drone "singing" when the thread is placed well, and a badly set thread gives a dull or buzzing sound.

## Does the drone do anything to the listener?

Claims that tanpura sound calms the nervous system and aids concentration are common.
There is some research on this.
Groups studying EEG responses to tanpura recordings report measurable changes in brain activity during listening [@src.ghosh-2017-tanpura-drone-brain-response].
Treat that as preliminary: the studies are small, come largely from one network of researchers, and have not established a mechanism.
Specific claims about "alpha and theta entrainment" and "millisecond self-correction of intonation" are not well supported by the sources that could be verified, so they are left out here.

## Sound as spiritual practice

Many musicians and listeners place the drone in the tradition of *nāda yoga*, the idea that sound (*nāda*) is the fundamental ground of music and of the self.
In this tradition, the unstruck, ever-present sound (*anāhata*) is thought of as underlying the struck sound (*āhata*) that we hear on instruments; the drone is the audible reminder of the unstruck sound.
This is a theological and aesthetic framework held by practitioners, not a measurable fact, and it is presented here as tradition.
Early treatises such as the Natyashastra and the Sangitaratnakara do discuss sound and its categories and give the idea philosophical weight [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india; @src.shringy-1978-sangitaratnakara-vol1].
The tanpura in its current form is much later than those texts, so it cannot be said to be the instrument they describe.

## What we know and do not know

- **Well supported:** the instrument's role as a Sa-Pa reference and the string layout above; the curved-bridge contact as the source of the overtone-rich sound.
- **Practice-dependent:** which note is used on the first string for which raga, and the string gauge and pitch.
- **Open:** the early history of the instrument; the strength of the effects claimed on listeners.
- **Tradition:** the nada yoga reading of the drone.
