Hindustani music is the classical tradition of North India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, and its closest relative is the Carnatic music of the south.
Both inherit the same body of theory (see the articles on origins and core concepts), but the forms, repertoires and performance styles described here belong mainly to the period from about the fifteenth century onward [@src.tenijenhuis-1974-indian-music; @src.neuman-1990-life-of-music].

## Where does it come from?

Older summaries say it is "born from Vedic chants" combined with an ancient *gandharva* tradition and a Persian one.
The truth is more careful.
The musical theory goes back to the treatises discussed elsewhere on this site, and Vedic chant is one of the oldest traditions from which later thinkers drew vocabulary, but a straight line from Samavedic chant to a present-day khyal cannot be demonstrated [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india; @src.widdess-1995-ragas-early-indian-music].
From the thirteenth century, the courts of the Delhi Sultanate and then the Mughals and regional rulers brought new patrons, instruments and aesthetic ideas, and the exchange ran in both directions [@src.neuman-1990-life-of-music; @src.sanyal-widdess-2004-dhrupad].
Stories crediting a single figure with inventing a form or instrument deserve caution unless they are backed by early evidence.

## The vocal forms

### Dhrupad

Dhrupad is a slow, serious form of sung raga performance, accompanied by the drone and the two-headed drum *pakhawaj*, and it opens with an unmetred exploration called *alap*, in which syllables are sung without a song-text, before a composition in a rhythmic cycle [@src.sanyal-widdess-2004-dhrupad].
Sanyal and Widdess trace its development in the courts of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, with royal patronage in Gwalior and later at the Mughal court [@src.sanyal-widdess-2004-dhrupad].
It is among the oldest living forms of North Indian classical song, but its claim to be the oldest is a matter of how one counts, since earlier genres are known from treatises and are not performed today.

### Khyal

Khyal (Persian-Arabic for "idea" or "imagination") is the dominant form of vocal performance in Hindustani music today [@src.wade-1984-khyal].
It consists of a short composition with a refrain, followed by extended improvisation of melodic phrases, and is generally performed at a slower or faster tempo in sequence.
Wade's study places its rise to prominence in the eighteenth century, and describes competing accounts of its origins, which include earlier regional song forms, and shows that it was transformed by court musicians and later by the great hereditary families [@src.wade-1984-khyal].
It allows more elaboration of the raga in a performer's own style than dhrupad, though "more romantic" is an impression more than a technical difference.

### Thumri and lighter forms

Thumri is usually called semi-classical.
It is a lyrical form built on a short text, often about love and separation, whose meaning is explored by the singer in ornament and by changing the words' expression, and its history is tied to the courts of Lucknow and to women performers in the cities of the north [@src.manuel-1989-thumri].
Ghazal is a verse form that came into India from Persian poetry; as sung music it belongs to the light-classical and popular world, not to the classical core [@src.manuel-1989-thumri].

## Gharanas

A *gharana* is a lineage or school of style, tied to a family of musicians or a place and passed down through teacher-to-student training.
Neuman describes how hereditary families of musicians, many of them Muslim, held the main body of repertoire and moulded the style that is marked by each gharana's name [@src.neuman-1990-life-of-music].
Well-known schools in vocal music include Gwalior, Agra, and Jaipur-Atrauli, and each is associated with typical ways of presenting a raga, but the differences are tendencies, not rules, and styles have blended as musicians moved between lineages [@src.neuman-1990-life-of-music; @src.wade-1984-khyal].
The idea of a gharana as an ancient, sealed tradition is largely a modern construction.

## Instruments

The most common instruments are:

- **Sitar:** a long-necked plucked lute with frets and sympathetic strings.
- **Sarod:** a fretless plucked lute with a metal-covered fingerboard.
- **Tabla:** a pair of hand drums for rhythmic accompaniment, which keeps the tala [@src.clayton-2000-time-in-indian-music].
- **Sarangi:** a bowed instrument whose sound is closely associated with the voice and the accompaniment of khyal and thumri [@src.manuel-1989-thumri].
- **Bansuri:** a transverse bamboo flute.

Instrumental music borrows the vocal forms and elaborations, and a drone (tanpura) supplies the tonic throughout [@src.neuman-1990-life-of-music].
The history of each instrument is debated: the claims that the sitar or tabla were invented by one named person are not backed by early evidence so they are not repeated here.

## What we can say with confidence

- The theory behind the music is ancient, while the main forms and the gharana system are documented from the fifteenth to the nineteenth centuries.
- Hindustani music grew from many contributors, Hindu and Muslim, courtly and religious.
- Many popular origin stories about forms and instruments are legendary, not documented.
