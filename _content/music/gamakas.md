Play a piano and Middle C is a point.
Listen to an Indian vocalist or a vina player and a note is a shape: it arrives from somewhere, leans, trembles, and leaves toward something else.
The Sanskrit word for these movements is *gamaka*, from the root *gam*, "to go".

## A note is a gesture

Western ornaments are often described as decoration laid onto a fixed note.
In Indian music the relationship is closer to the reverse.
Studies of South Indian performance describe the note as a movement through pitch space, with the moving parts carrying as much of the raga's identity as the pitch where it lands [@src.viswanathan-1977-raga-alapana; @src.pearson-2016-coarticulation-gesture].
A computational study of Carnatic ragas likewise finds that a svara's intonation differs between ragas and that gamakas shape the pitch distributions of each raga [@src.koduri-2014-intonation-carnatic].
This is the practical reason two ragas built on nearly the same set of notes can sound entirely different: they are not sung with the same movements.

Be careful with the slogan that a note without gamaka is "dead".
Some ragas and passages are sung with plain, steady notes, and the amount of ornament varies by raga, school and performer.
The accurate statement is that ornament is structural in many ragas, not that it is universal.

## What the old treatises say

The Natyashastra's music chapters concern notes, gramas and their shrutis [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
The best-known classical list of gamakas comes later, from the Sangitaratnakara of Sarngadeva (thirteenth century), which defines gamaka as a trembling or movement of a svara and gives a list of fifteen types, among them *tiripa*, *sphurita*, *kampita*, *līna*, *āndolita*, *vali*, *ullāsita*, *plāvita*, *gumphita*, *mudrita*, *namita* and *miśrita* [@src.shringy-1978-sangitaratnakara-vol1].
Even among specialists, how each of these was performed is reconstructed from short Sanskrit definitions and is debated, so this article does not give a single confident description for each.
The treatise itself is best read in the Shringy and Sharma edition, which gives text and translation with notes.

Two cautions about popular lists:

- The Sangitaratnakara's list of fifteen should not be mixed with modern Carnatic terms such as *nokku* or *orikkai*, which are used in present-day teaching and are not Sarngadeva's names.
- Different texts and teachers give different inventories, so "the fifteen gamakas" and "the ten Hindustani ornaments" are conventions, not a single fixed standard.

## A working vocabulary

The following are widely used terms in present-day performance.
The descriptions are simplified, and teachers differ.

| Term | Rough meaning |
|---|---|
| *Kaṇ* (kan, kana svara) | A grace note: a brief touch of a neighbouring note before landing on the target |
| *Mīṇḍ* (meend) | A continuous glide between two notes, with no audible steps |
| *Āndolan* | A slow, wide oscillation around a note |
| *Gamak* | A heavier, faster oscillation or shake, often involving a jump of a step or more |
| *Murkī* | A quick, light turn of a few notes around a note |
| *Khaṭkā* | A rapid cluster of notes that punctuates a phrase |
| *Kampita* (Carnatic) | Oscillation on a note, wider or narrower depending on the raga |
| *Jāru* (Carnatic) | A slide between notes |
| *Nokku*, *orikkai* (Carnatic) | Terms for a stress or flick onto a note, used in modern teaching |

Note that "gamak" has two senses: the general word for all such movements, and the specific heavy shake of Hindustani vocal and instrumental styles, which is why the same word turns up in the lists of both traditions.

## Gamakas and ragas

Some of the clearest examples are the ragas whose character depends on a specific oscillation.
Many musicians describe the komal Re and Dha in Bhairav as sung with a slow, wide andolan, and the komal Ga and Dha in Darbari Kanada as held with a very slow, deep oscillation.
These are performance-practice descriptions from teaching traditions, and they have not been verified here in a peer-reviewed source, so treat them as widely repeated but unchecked.
What the verified research does support is the broader point: pitch movement, not only pitch position, is a defining part of raga in South Indian music [@src.viswanathan-1977-raga-alapana; @src.koduri-2014-intonation-carnatic].

## Where this leaves us

- **Well supported:** gamaka is an old and central concept, named in the Sangitaratnakara with a fifteen-fold list; South Indian svaras are shaped by movement.
- **Convention:** modern counts of "ten" or "fifteen" and the exact definitions of terms.
- **Not verified here:** raga-by-raga rules for which ornament is mandatory.
