Picture a jazz player taking a solo, then add two rules: every phrase must stay faithful to a named melodic personality, and every beat must land on a rhythmic cycle that may be sixteen or more counts long.
That is a fair first picture of improvisation in Indian classical music.
It is free, but it is not random.

## What manodharma means

In the Karnatak tradition, improvised music is called manodharma sangita, roughly "music of the creative mind".
Hindustani musicians speak of upaj (invention) and vistar (elaboration), and they use the same underlying ideas. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music; @src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]
Improvisation draws on three things at once: the grammar of the raga (which notes, which phrases, which resting places), the shape of the tala (the rhythmic cycle), and what a musician has absorbed from teachers over years. [@src.widdess-2001-raga-grove]

A composition, called a bandish in the north and a kriti in the south, acts as an anchor.
The performer leaves it, explores, and returns to it.
Figures sometimes quoted for the share of a concert that is improvised are not backed by a published measurement, so this article gives none.

## Free and measured music

Medieval Sanskrit treatises distinguish music that is bound to a rhythmic framework (nibaddha) from music that is not (anibaddha), and they describe the free melodic exposition of a raga under the name alapti. [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india]
That distinction survives in modern practice.
A performance usually begins with a free, unmetered section and gradually acquires a pulse, then a tala.

## Hindustani practice

### Alap, jod and jhala

In dhrupad and in the instrumental styles descended from it, the opening section is the alap.
It is slow and unmetered and introduces the raga note by note, usually starting in the lower register and working upward. [@src.sanyal-widdess-2004-dhrupad]
Next comes the jod, in which a steady pulse emerges but there is still no drum cycle, and then the jhala, a faster and more rhythmically driven passage. [@src.sanyal-widdess-2004-dhrupad]
On a string instrument such as the sitar, jhala is often played by alternating strokes on the melody string with strokes on the sympathetic and drone strings, producing a lively, shimmering rhythmic texture. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]
Rhythm theory for this music, including the relation between unmetered and metered sections, is analysed in detail by Martin Clayton. [@src.clayton-2000-time-in-indian-music]

### Vistar, badhat and taan

When the drum (pakhawaj or tabla) enters and the composition begins, the improvisation continues inside the tala.
In khayal this is called vistar, and a central principle is badhat, step-by-step growth: the singer builds the raga upward from a resting note to the next, giving each note a place to rest before moving on, so that the raga is revealed gradually. [@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]
Later, in faster tempo, come taans, rapid melodic runs sung on a vowel, on note names, or on the words of the composition (bol-taan).

Teachers distinguish several types of taan by shape.

| Type | Idea |
|---|---|
| Sapat | A straight run up or down the notes of the raga |
| Kut | A complex, twisting pattern that is hard to predict |
| Bol | A fast run articulated with the words of the song |
| Gamak | A heavy run in which each note is shaken or oscillated |

[@src.grove-2001-hindustani-music]
Other terms exist, and teaching lineages differ on both names and definitions.

## Karnatak practice

### Ragam and tanam

A Karnatak performer's improvisation is traditionally divided into several arts: ragam (alapana), tanam, niraval, and kalpanaswara, and these converge in the pallavi section of a ragam tanam pallavi (RTP). [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
The ragam alapana is the unmetered exploration of the raga.
In his analysis of the alapana, T. Viswanathan described how a skilled musician builds it in stages, moving up the registers, and expanding phrases from a characteristic opening to a complete portrait of the raga. [@src.viswanathan-1977-raga-alapana]

Tanam follows.
It is a rhythmic, pulsed form of raga exploration on syllables such as "a-nam" and "tha-nam" and it plays a role similar to the jod of the north, though the two are not identical. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]

### Niraval and kalpanaswara

Two arts of the south work closely with a composition.

- **Niraval** ("filling") takes one line of text from a kriti or pallavi and sings it again and again in new melodic versions. The words and their rhythmic position inside the tala stay fixed, and only the melody changes. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
- **Kalpanaswara** is the improvisation of strings of solfege syllables (sa, ri, ga, ma and so on) in the raga and tala, which usually ends by returning to a chosen point in the composition. Its patterns are often built from arithmetic, in the spirit of the pre-composed rhythmic cadences called korvais that close such passages. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]

The first of these tests a musician's feeling for how melody carries words.
The second tests a feeling for rhythm and proportion.

### The pallavi in an RTP

In an RTP, the pallavi is a single line of text set to a long tala.
Its starting point (eduppu) and a point of balance in the middle are carefully chosen, and the musician develops it in several ways. [@src.grove-2001-karnatak-music]
Among them is trikalam, singing the same line at successive speeds while the hand-kept tala stays steady.
Another is a switch of the subdivision from groups of four to groups of three (tisram).
Many performances end with swaras in a succession of other ragas (ragamalika) before the singer returns to the main raga.
Exact details vary between singers and schools, so what follows here is a sketch and not a rule.

## Why rules make freedom possible

It is tempting to see a contradiction between rule and improvisation.
For performers there is none.
A raga is a melodic personality, not a list of permitted notes, so a good performer must know its characteristic phrases, which notes it lingers on, and which it passes quickly. [@src.widdess-2001-raga-grove]
The tala sets a time-grid that any phrase must respect.
Within those constraints the number of possible phrases is enormous, and the artist's skill is in choosing, linking, and pacing them.
That is why training takes years of listening and imitation under a teacher before improvisation begins to feel natural. [@src.sanyal-widdess-2004-dhrupad]
