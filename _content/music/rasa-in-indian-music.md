Why would anyone choose to listen to a sad song?
In life, grief is something we try to avoid.
In a concert hall, the same grief can leave us moved, calm, even happy.
Indian thinkers asked this question more than two thousand years ago, and their answer is called rasa.

## The word and the idea

Rasa literally means "juice", "sap" or "taste".
In aesthetics it names the savour that a spectator or listener relishes when a work of art succeeds. [@src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader]
The theory comes from the Natyashastra, a Sanskrit treatise on theatre attributed to the sage Bharata, which also has chapters on music, instruments, and rhythm. [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india; @src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader]
Scholars date the text within a wide window, commonly placing it somewhere between about 200 BCE and 200 CE, and many regard it as a composite work built up over time. [@src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader]

## Bharata's formula

The famous formula is found at Natyashastra 6.31: rasa arises from the combination (samyoga) of vibhava, anubhava and vyabhicaribhava. [@src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader]

| Term | Meaning | Example in a love scene |
|---|---|---|
| Vibhava | Determinants: the persons and circumstances that arouse a feeling | The beloved, a moonlit evening |
| Anubhava | Consequents: the outward signs of the feeling | A glance, a sigh, trembling |
| Vyabhicaribhava | Transitory states that pass through the mind | Anxiety, longing, joy |

Beneath these sits the sthayibhava, the enduring emotional state that the work evokes.
Bharata lists eight such states and eight matching rasas: love (shringara), laughter (hasya), pathos (karuna), anger (raudra), heroism (vira), fear (bhayanaka), disgust (bibhatsa), and wonder (adbhuta). [@src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader]
Many later authors add a ninth, shanta (peace or tranquillity).
Whether Bharata himself recognised shanta is disputed, and the ninth rasa became firmly established in the later tradition, in which Abhinavagupta was its great defender. [@src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader]
So the common phrase "the nine rasas" describes a later consensus, not the Natyashastra's original list.

## How can sorrow give delight?

The ancient commentators disagreed over what exactly happens in the spectator, and four positions are especially well known. [@src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader]

1. **Bhatta Lollata** held that rasa is produced in the characters and actor and is, in some sense, attributed to the actor.
2. **Shankuka** held that the spectator infers the emotion from the actor's skilled imitation.
3. **Bhatta Nayaka** held that the spectator enjoys the emotion in a generalised form, freed from the particular person and circumstances (a process he called sadharanikarana).
4. **Abhinavagupta** held that rasa is revealed or manifested (abhivyakti) in the spectator's mind. He took over Bhatta Nayaka's idea of generalisation, and described the experience as a relishing of a latent emotion that is free from personal worry.

Abhinavagupta also compared the experience to tasting the bliss of the ultimate reality, brahman. [@src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader]
A common summary of the generalisation idea is that, in art, "my" grief becomes grief as such, and so it can be savoured and not merely suffered.
Attribution matters here: sadharanikarana belongs first to Bhatta Nayaka, and Abhinavagupta developed it further. [@src.pollock-2016-rasa-reader]

## Notes and moods

Bharata's chapters on music link particular notes (svaras) with particular rasas.
In his scheme, sa and ri belong to the heroic, furious and marvellous; ga and ni to pathos; ma and pa to the erotic and comic; and dha to the disgusting and terrible. [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india]
These associations are theatrical and belong to an ancient system of scales that differs from the modern Hindustani and Karnatak arrangement of notes, so they cannot be mapped directly onto modern ragas. [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india]
Raga as a named melodic type is described in treatises that come after the Natyashastra. [@src.widdess-2001-raga-grove]
Later theory and practice came to speak of the mood of a raga, and each raga acquired a reputation for a particular character.

## Ragamala: ragas as pictures

From roughly the sixteenth century into the nineteenth, courts in several regions produced ragamala ("garland of ragas") paintings, in which each raga is depicted as a figure in a scene and often accompanied by a short verse. [@src.bahadur-ramaswami-2022-ragamala; @src.aitken-2013-laud-ragamala]
Many sets arrange ragas as a family: male ragas, their consorts (ragini) and in some systems their sons.
A raga such as Bhairava is commonly shown as the god Shiva, while Hindola is typically shown with a swing. [@src.bahadur-ramaswami-2022-ragamala]
Different regional schools of painting, including Rajput, Pahari and Deccani, give the same ragas different looks. [@src.bahadur-ramaswami-2022-ragamala]
These paintings record how patrons and painters imagined moods and seasons, and they should not be read as a sound-to-colour system with scientific precision.

## Does the claimed mood reach listeners?

The theory predicts that listeners feel something specific, and that is testable, at least in part.
Two studies are worth knowing.

- Balkwill and Thompson played raga excerpts to Western listeners who were unfamiliar with Indian music. The listeners could identify the emotions that Indian performers intended (joy, sadness, anger and peace) at better than chance, and their judgements related to cues such as tempo and pitch range. [@src.balkwill-thompson-1999-cross-cultural-emotion]
- Mathur and colleagues studied emotional responses to Hindustani ragas and reported that musical structure was related to the emotions listeners reported. [@src.mathur-2015-emotional-responses-raga]

These results support the idea that some mood cues in raga music are widely perceptible, and they do not prove the specific traditional pairings of raga and rasa. [@src.balkwill-thompson-1999-cross-cultural-emotion; @src.mathur-2015-emotional-responses-raga]
Scholars and musicians therefore take at least two views.
One holds that ragas carry stable moods rooted in tradition.
The other holds that the associations are conventions that shift across regions and periods, with the mood of a performance depending as much on tempo, ornament, and the performer's choices as on the raga's name.
