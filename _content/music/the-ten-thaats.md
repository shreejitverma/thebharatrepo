Hindustani music has hundreds of ragas, and for centuries no one agreed on how to organise them.
One older scheme grouped them as families: six male "parent" ragas, each with several "wives" (*raginis*) and "sons".
It is poetic, and it appears in the illustrated *ragamala* paintings, but it is not a very useful way to learn which notes a raga uses [@src.bakhle-2005-two-men-and-music].

Early in the twentieth century a scholar and teacher named Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande (1860 to 1936) proposed something more practical.
He sorted ragas by the scale each one is built on and gave each scale a name: a *thaat* (ṭhāṭa) [@src.powers-2001-bhatkhande].
Today almost every beginner in North India learns the ten thaats, so it is worth understanding what they are, and what they are not.

## Who was Bhatkhande, and why did he do it?

Bhatkhande was trained as a lawyer, and became an energetic collector of compositions, a theorist and an organiser of music schools and conferences.
He travelled to hear and record performers and consulted older Sanskrit and regional treatises [@src.powers-2001-bhatkhande].
His aim, as historians of the period describe it, was to bring a respected but hard-to-teach oral art into a form that could be taught in schools, written down and compared [@src.bakhle-2005-two-men-and-music].

That aim shapes the system.
It is a tool for teaching and cataloguing, built by a scholar who needed a map, rather than a discovery about how great musicians themselves think.
Keeping that in mind helps explain both its strengths and its awkward corners.

## What a thaat is

A thaat is a parent scale.
By Bhatkhande's rules, a thaat:

- contains seven distinct notes (Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni) taken in ascending order,
- uses only one form of each note, so it cannot contain both a natural and a flat Re, for instance,
- is a scale only: it has no prescribed ascent and descent as a raga does, no dominant note and no phrases, so it is a classification label rather than something a musician performs, and
- is named after one well-known raga built on it.

A raga is then assigned to a thaat by looking at the notes it uses [@src.powers-2001-india-raga].
The thaat is a mother of ragas, as an old saying goes, only in the sense of a filing system: a raga can have fewer than seven notes, but it can be filed under the thaat that contains them.

Where do ten come from?
The seven notes include two that never change, Sa and Pa.
The other five can each take two forms, which gives 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 = 32 possible seven-note scales if you keep one form of each.
Bhatkhande did not use all 32.
He chose ten that matched the ragas then actually in use [@src.powers-2001-bhatkhande; @src.bakhle-2005-two-men-and-music].
That is a useful fact to hold on to: the ten thaats are chosen from a larger space of possibilities by practice, not by arithmetic, and that distinguishes the Hindustani system from the Carnatic one discussed in the companion article on the 72 melakartas.

## Reading the notation

Notes are shown here with the usual Hindustani conventions:

- *shuddha* means the natural form of a note,
- *komal* (flat) applies to Re, Ga, Dha and Ni, which can be lowered,
- *tivra* (sharp) applies only to Ma, which can be raised.

## The ten thaats

The table gives the altered notes in each thaat (all others are natural), a Western modal name where one exists, and some typical ragas filed under it [@src.powers-2001-india-raga; @src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems].

| Thaat | Altered notes | Western equivalent | Some ragas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bilawal | none | Ionian (major scale) | Alhaiya Bilawal, Bihag |
| Kalyan | tivra Ma | Lydian | Yaman, Bhupali |
| Khamaj | komal Ni | Mixolydian | Khamaj, Desh |
| Kafi | komal Ga, komal Ni | Dorian | Kafi, Bhimpalasi |
| Asavari | komal Ga, Dha, Ni | Aeolian (natural minor) | Asavari, Jaunpuri |
| Bhairavi | komal Re, Ga, Dha, Ni | Phrygian | Bhairavi, Malkauns |
| Bhairav | komal Re, komal Dha | no common name | Bhairav, Ramkali |
| Marwa | komal Re, tivra Ma | Lydian with lowered second | Marwa, Sohini |
| Poorvi | komal Re, komal Dha, tivra Ma | no common name | Poorvi, Puriya Dhanashree |
| Todi | komal Re, Ga, Dha, tivra Ma | no common name | Miyan ki Todi, Gujari Todi |

A few remarks on the table.

The first six rows are the ones whose notes match the familiar Western church modes, which is why Western musicians often say that Bilawal "is" the major scale.
That correspondence is useful as a first approximation only: Indian ragas are sung with their own intonation and with gliding ornaments, and a single note in a raga is not always the equal-tempered pitch of a piano key [@src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems].
Treat the Western names as a way into the system, not as a statement that the music is the same.

Notice also that the thaats fall into two groups.
Six use the natural Ma (Bilawal, Khamaj, Kafi, Asavari, Bhairavi, Bhairav) and four use the sharp Ma (Kalyan, Marwa, Poorvi, Todi).
This split reappears in the Carnatic system, where it is the very first division of the 72 parent scales.

## The thaats and the Carnatic melakartas

South Indian music organises its parent scales differently, but the underlying pitches overlap, and each Hindustani thaat has a counterpart among the 72 melakartas.
The pairing is by pitches only, and it is not an identity of musical practice [@src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems; @src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].

| Thaat | Melakarta (number) |
|---|---|
| Bilawal | Dheerasankarabharanam (29) |
| Kalyan | Mechakalyani (65) |
| Khamaj | Harikambhoji (28) |
| Kafi | Kharaharapriya (22) |
| Asavari | Natabhairavi (20) |
| Bhairavi | Hanumatodi (8) |
| Bhairav | Mayamalavagowla (15) |
| Marwa | Gamanashrama (53) |
| Poorvi | Kamavardhani (51) |
| Todi | Shubhapantuvarali (45) |

Names deserve caution here.
A raga called Bhairavi in the North and one called Bhairavi in the South are different ragas with different scales, and the Hindustani Bhairavi thaat corresponds to the Carnatic Hanumatodi, not to a Carnatic raga that shares its name [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
The companion article on the 72 melakartas explains how the numbers are derived.

## Where the system strains

Bhatkhande's scheme has been enormously influential, and it has also been criticised, in ways worth knowing.

**Ragas with a foot in two thaats.**
Some ragas use both forms of a note.
Lalit uses both the natural and the sharp Ma, and some ragas use both natural and flat Ni.
Such a raga has to be filed under one thaat by a judgment call, and different books disagree.
Ahir Bhairav, which uses komal Re and komal Ni but a natural Ga, is one such case [@src.powers-2001-india-raga].

**Scale against practice.**
Two ragas filed under the same thaat can differ more than ragas in different thaats, because what makes a raga distinctive (its phrases, emphasis, ornaments, time of day) is not in the thaat at all [@src.powers-2001-india-raga].

**A historical choice.**
The scheme was designed to teach, and some historians argue that it fitted a modern, institutional, written approach to music that was being built at the time and that changed what the tradition stressed [@src.bakhle-2005-two-men-and-music].
Others, including many working musicians, simply use it as a convenient index and find it practical.
Both views are compatible with the thaats being useful.

It is tempting to call the Carnatic system "better" because it is complete, but that is a value judgment.
The two systems solve different problems: the Carnatic scheme enumerates every possible parent scale, whereas the Hindustani one lists the ones that mattered for the ragas then in use [@src.powers-2001-india-tonal-systems].

## Take-away

- A thaat is a seven-note parent scale used to classify ragas; it is not itself a raga.
- Bhatkhande chose ten of the 32 possible combinations, in the early twentieth century, as a teaching tool.
- Western modes and Carnatic melakartas give useful comparison points but not exact equivalents.
- Ragas that bridge two thaats show where the map and the territory differ.
