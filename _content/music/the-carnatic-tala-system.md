A Carnatic concert is held together by a pulse that the performers mark with their hands, often visibly, throughout a performance.
The singer and violinist keep the cycle on their laps with claps, finger counts and waves, while the mridangam player works out patterns against it.
The system behind this is compact: three kinds of unit, seven ways of combining them, and five ways of sizing one of the units [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].

## The three limbs

Carnatic tala is built from angas (aṅga, "limbs"), and three are in use today [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india]:

| Anga | Symbol | Length in beats | How it is shown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anudrutam | U | 1 | a single clap |
| Drutam | O | 2 | a clap followed by a wave of the hand |
| Laghu | I | 3, 4, 5, 7 or 9, depending on the jati | a clap followed by finger counts |

The hand actions are called kriyas: the sounded clap (tattu), the silent wave (vicchu) and the finger count (viral ennikkai).
The kriya is one of the ten classical "vital elements" of tala, discussed in the companion article on marga talas and the ten pranas [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana].

## Seven talas

The suladi sapta tala ("seven suladi talas") are seven fixed orderings of these limbs [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india]:

| Tala | Limbs | Beats if the laghu has 4 |
|---|---|---|
| Dhruva | I O I I | 4+2+4+4 = 14 |
| Matya | I O I | 4+2+4 = 10 |
| Rupaka | O I | 2+4 = 6 |
| Jhampa | I U O | 4+1+2 = 7 |
| Triputa | I O O | 4+2+2 = 8 |
| Ata | I I O O | 4+4+2+2 = 12 |
| Eka | I | 4 |

Triputa with a four-beat laghu is the cycle most Carnatic musicians simply call adi tala, "the first tala", and it is the most commonly met cycle in the repertoire [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].

Tradition credits the poet-composer Purandara Dasa with setting out these seven in the 16th century.
The scholarly literature used here does not document the attribution, so it should be read as tradition and not as established history.
What the literature does show is that the scheme is a later simplification of a much richer older family of talas, discussed in the companion article on marga talas and the ten pranas [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana].

## Five jatis and the number 35

The laghu is the only limb whose length changes.
Its jati (jāti, "class") sets the count [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india]:

| Jati | Laghu count | Commonly taught syllables |
|---|---|---|
| Tisra | 3 | ta-ki-ta |
| Chaturasra | 4 | ta-ka-dhi-mi |
| Khanda | 5 | ta-ka, ta-ki-ta |
| Misra | 7 | ta-ki-ta, ta-ka-dhi-mi |
| Sankirna | 9 | ta-ka-dhi-mi, ta-ka-ta-ki-ta |

The syllables are the spoken drum language called konnakol or solkattu, and teachers vary in the exact syllables chosen; the grouping (3, 4, 2+3, 3+4, 4+5) is the point [@src.nelson-2008-solkattu-manual].

Seven talas times five jatis give 35 cycles.
Each total is simple arithmetic from the table above:

| Tala | Tisra | Chaturasra | Khanda | Misra | Sankirna |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dhruva | 11 | 14 | 17 | 23 | 29 |
| Matya | 8 | 10 | 12 | 16 | 20 |
| Rupaka | 5 | 6 | 7 | 9 | 11 |
| Jhampa | 6 | 7 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
| Triputa | 7 | 8 | 9 | 11 | 13 |
| Ata | 10 | 12 | 14 | 18 | 22 |
| Eka | 3 | 4 | 5 | 7 | 9 |

The jati changes the laghu wherever it appears, so a "Misra jati Jhampa" is a laghu of seven, an anudrutam of one and a drutam of two, ten beats in all [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].

## Gati and the count of 175

Within each beat, the notes or syllables can be grouped in threes, fours, fives, sevens or nines.
That grouping is called gati or nadai, and it is a separate matter from the jati, which sizes the laghu.
Counting each of the 35 talas in each of the five gatis gives the figure of 175 that is sometimes quoted [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
Whether a change of gati makes a "different tala", or only changes the subdivision inside the same cycle, is a question of definition, and I did not find a source in this research that settles it; the figure 175 is best treated as a convenient teaching count.

## Cycles outside the 35

Some of the most frequently performed cycles are not among the 35 as usually tabulated.
Misra chapu (7 beats) and khanda chapu (5 beats) are the best known.
They have a lilting, uneven feel quite unlike the even sections of the seven suladi talas [@src.viswanathan-2004-music-in-south-india].
Accounts of the exact pattern of hand actions in the chapu talas differ between teachers and books, so this article does not fix one.

## Older and newer meanings of "laghu" and "jati"

The vocabulary of the Carnatic system is old, but its meanings have shifted.
In the older Sanskrit theory a laghu is a fixed unit of one matra, and the five jati names classify talas by how many gurus they contain, not the length of a laghu [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana].
The seven-by-five table above is therefore a later reworking of older terms, useful and easy to calculate, but not a direct inheritance from the classical theory.
Learning to read the table is the beginning of Carnatic rhythm, and the older theory in the companion article shows where its words came from [@src.rao-2013-evolution-tala-dasa-prana].
