Nobody can say when music began in the subcontinent, because music leaves almost no trace until someone writes about it.
What we can do is follow the written record: texts that describe singing and playing, the theory that grew up around them, and the courts and teachers who carried the art forward.
That record starts with ritual chant and becomes, over some three thousand years, the classical traditions heard today.

## Chant in the Vedic world

The oldest music we can describe in any detail is Vedic liturgical chant.
The Samaveda, the Veda of melodies, was compiled for the priests who sang during the Soma ritual, and almost all of its verses are borrowed from the Rigveda and re-set to melody [@src.gonda-1975-vedic-literature; @src.singh-2008-ancient-early-medieval-india].
Scholars date the Samaveda only by reasoning from language and ritual, to roughly the later second millennium BCE, so the date is an estimate with a wide margin and not a fixed point [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].

The chants (*saman*) are not simply recited verses.
Syllables are stretched, repeated and broken up with extra sounds, and the melodies are built from a small number of pitches that are named, and in written tradition marked with numerals [@src.howard-1977-samavedic-chant].
Wayne Howard's study of living Samavedic traditions shows how the melodic skeleton is held in memory and passed on aurally, in the way the whole Vedic corpus was preserved [@src.howard-1977-samavedic-chant].
UNESCO recognised Vedic chanting as a masterpiece of intangible heritage in 2003 for exactly this continuous oral transmission [@src.unesco-2003-tradition-vedic-chanting].

It would be wrong to call this "the first music of India".
Song, drums and flutes surely existed before and beside the ritual, and the later literature speaks of a secular and courtly singing tradition called *gandharva* [@src.rowell-1992-music-early-india].
Vedic chant is simply the earliest tradition we can examine in detail, because it was fixed in a text and guarded by priests.

## The treatises: swara, grama, jati

The next large body of evidence is theoretical.
The Natyashastra, a manual of drama attributed to Bharata, devotes several chapters to music, and so do later works such as the Dattilam [@src.rangacharya-1996-natyasastra; @src.rowell-1992-music-early-india].
The Natyashastra cannot be dated precisely, and scholars place its compilation across several centuries around the turn of the era, an estimate that rests on internal evidence and style [@src.rowell-1992-music-early-india].

Its musical vocabulary is not "swara, raga and tala" arriving together, as popular summaries sometimes say.
Seven named pitches already appear in earlier tradition, and the Natyashastra organises them into two scale systems called *grama* and into modes derived from them (*murchana* and *jati*) [@src.rowell-1992-music-early-india; @src.tenijenhuis-1974-indian-music].
The *jati* system describes melodic types by their characteristic notes and emphases, which is the closest ancestor of the later idea of a raga.
Raga as a defined term appears later, and the Brhaddesi attributed to Matanga is generally regarded as the earliest surviving text to treat it systematically [@src.sharma-1992-brhaddesi-matanga; @src.widdess-1995-ragas-early-indian-music].

## From jati to raga

Richard Widdess's study of early melodic material shows how modes and melodies were described between the Gupta period and about 1250 CE, and includes some of the earliest notated music from the region [@src.widdess-1995-ragas-early-indian-music].
These sources let us see ragas at an earlier stage: not the improvised, free-flowing performance of today, but sets of notes and characteristic phrases attached to named categories of song.

The summit of this literature is the Sangita-ratnakara of Sarngadeva, a thirteenth-century encyclopaedia from the Deccan.
It collects and systematises inherited theory, including pitch intervals, ragas, rhythm and instruments, and is cited as an authority by later musicians in both north and south [@src.shringy-1978-sangita-ratnakara; @src.tenijenhuis-1974-indian-music].

## Courts, sultanates and the north-south divide

After about 1200 the north of the subcontinent was increasingly governed from Muslim courts, and new patrons, musicians and ideas entered the musical world.
The older story that "Persian influence" created Hindustani music is too simple, though.
Scholars describe a long and layered exchange, in which Hindu and Muslim musicians shared repertoires, and genres such as dhrupad were performed in many kinds of court [@src.sanyal-widdess-2004-dhrupad; @src.neuman-1990-life-of-music].
Khyal, the dominant vocal form of the north today, rose to prominence in the eighteenth century, and its origins are debated [@src.wade-1984-khyal].

North and south were never cut off from each other, and both claim the same earlier treatises as ancestors.
By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the two regions were developing different systems of classifying ragas and different performance practices, which is how Hindustani and Carnatic music came to be seen as separate traditions [@src.tenijenhuis-1974-indian-music].
Neither is "closer" to an ancient original: both have changed continuously.
Ghazal and qawwali, sometimes listed as products of this period, are better understood as other genres that grew in the same cultural world, not as branches of classical music.

## The modern period

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries court patronage declined, and music moved to concert halls, radio and the recording industry.
Teachers and scholars compiled notation systems and raga classifications, and the old master-to-disciple (*guru-shishya*) teaching took on new institutional forms [@src.neuman-1990-life-of-music; @src.jairazbhoy-1971-rags-north-indian-music].
Neuman's fieldwork documents how hereditary families of musicians adapted to these changes while keeping their style lineages [@src.neuman-1990-life-of-music].
Film music, popular song and fusion belong to the same story, but they are outside the scope of this article.

## How we know, and what we do not

- Before the treatises, almost all knowledge is indirect.
  We have texts, and in the case of chant a living oral tradition, but no recordings or reliable notation.
- Treatise dates are estimates, and a text can describe an older practice or a contemporary one.
- Theory and practice are not always the same: the treatises tell us what scholars wrote, and what musicians did may have differed [@src.rowell-1992-music-early-india].
- Claims of unbroken continuity from the Vedic chants to a present-day raga go beyond the evidence.
  The links are real, but they are traced through texts and shared vocabulary, and each step changed what it inherited.
