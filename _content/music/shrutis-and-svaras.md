Sing "Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni" and you have sung the seven notes (svara) that Indian music has used for well over two thousand years.
Ask a trained musician how many pitches lie between those seven notes and you may hear "twenty-two", the number of shrutis.
That number is old, famous and surprisingly slippery.
This article separates what the early treatises actually say from what later scholars have built on them.

## Seven notes, named for their roles

The seven svaras are Ṣaḍja, Ṛṣabha, Gāndhāra, Madhyama, Pañcama, Dhaivata and Niṣāda, shortened to Sa, Re (or Ri), Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni.
Ṣaḍja is usually explained as "born of six" and is the home note from which the others are measured.
The seven-note system is already fully formed in the early music chapters of the Natyashastra, a treatise on performance traditionally attributed to Bharata, and in the Dattilam, another early treatise on music [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india; @src.nijenhuis-1970-dattilam].
Rowell's survey of early Indian musical thought also shows how much older ritual practice, including the chanting of the Samaveda with its own small set of named pitches, lies behind the theory [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
The Samaveda is the Veda of melodies, and Vedic chanting is a living tradition that can be heard today.

Later texts link each svara with a bird or animal call, and the lists differ from text to text, so the associations are better read as a mnemonic and symbolic tradition than as acoustics [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].

Two notes are special.
Sa and Pa are called achala, "unmoving", because they have no raised or lowered variants; Sarngadeva's thirteenth-century Sangitaratnakara states this and sets out the others as movable [@src.shringy-1978-sangitaratnakara-vol1].
That is why the drone of the tanpura is built on Sa and Pa (see the article on the tanpura).
In present-day Hindustani practice the movable notes Re, Ga, Dha and Ni each have a flat (komal) form, and Ma has a sharp (tivra) form, which gives twelve pitch positions per octave [@src.jairazbhoy-1963-intonation-north-indian].

## What is a shruti?

The word comes from the root *śru*, "to hear".
A shruti is a unit of pitch distance that the ear can tell apart; the early texts use it as a counting unit rather than a measured size.
In the Natyashastra the seven notes sit within an octave of twenty-two shrutis, and each note is assigned a number of them.
In the standard scale, the Ṣaḍja-grāma ("grama" is a tonal framework or scale family), the allotment is [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india; @src.nijenhuis-1970-dattilam]:

| Svara | Sa | Re | Ga | Ma | Pa | Dha | Ni |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shrutis from the previous note | 4 | 3 | 2 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 2 |

The seven numbers add up to 22.
Notice what is being counted: gaps between notes, not notes themselves.
Re is three shrutis above Sa, Ga is two above Re, and so on.

The Madhyama-grāma, a second framework, differs in one place.
Its Pa is one shruti lower, so Pa has 3 shrutis and Dha 4 [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
This is the only structural difference between the two, and everything else in the shruti story hangs on it: one shruti is the small step by which a note can be shifted while remaining the "same" note.
A third framework, the Gāndhāra-grāma, is described in the Natyashastra as belonging to the heavens and not used by people [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
Treat that as tradition: no practical description survives.

## Bharata's two-vina demonstration

How could anyone prove that twenty-two steps exist?
The Natyashastra offers a thought experiment, called the *sāraṇā-catuṣṭaya* ("four-fold shifting"), using two identical seven-stringed vinas.
One is kept fixed; on the other, certain strings are lowered by one shruti in stages, and the listener is meant to hear that the shifted notes take on the pitch character of the neighbouring notes, which marks out the shruti steps [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
I do not give the stage-by-stage tuning here, because the passage is terse and interpreters read the individual steps differently.
What matters is that Bharata argues from the ear and from instruments, not from numerical ratios.

## Did the early texts give ratios?

No.
There are no fractions such as 9/8 or 5/4 in the early treatises.
Ratios come from modern reconstruction, and several incompatible reconstructions exist.
You will often see a table of twenty-two ratios, each paired with a named shruti, presented as if it were Bharata's.
It is not; it is one modern reading among several, and such tables should not be treated as ancient evidence [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].

The main modern positions are:

- **Equal shrutis.** If the octave is cut into twenty-two equal parts, each step is about 54.5 cents (a cent is one hundredth of a Western semitone). With the 4-3-2-4-4-3-2 allotment this puts Pa 13 steps above Sa (about 709 cents, close to the 702 cents of a 3/2 fifth) and Ma 9 steps above Sa (about 491 cents, close to a 4/3 fourth), which is the arithmetic reason the scheme looks so plausible [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
- **Unequal shrutis tied to just ratios.** Here the shrutis have different sizes, derived from simple whole-number ratios, and the gap between a "major tone" (9/8) and a "minor tone" (10/9), the ratio 81/80, is identified with the *pramāṇa* (measuring) shruti. This is attractive because the Sa-grama to Ma-grama shift of Pa is then the size of one such step. It is a modern interpretation, not a statement found in the treatises [@src.rowell-1992-music-musical-thought-early-india].
- **A counting framework only.** On this view the 22 is a tool for describing which notes are close to which, not a claim that twenty-two separate pitches are sung.

I have not found a way to decide between these using the early texts alone, and the question remains open.

## What do performers actually sing?

Jairazbhoy and Stone measured the pitches of North Indian performers with instruments rather than the ear.
They found that the intonation of the same-named note shifts with the raga and the context, and could not map the measured pitches onto a single fixed twenty-two-step scale [@src.jairazbhoy-1963-intonation-north-indian].
Later computational work on Carnatic music reaches a related conclusion: svara positions differ from raga to raga, and each svara is better described as a region of pitch with a shape than as a single point [@src.koduri-2014-intonation-carnatic].
The ornaments that move around a note are part of that picture (see the article on gamakas).

The common textbook ratios (Re 9/8, Ga 5/4, Ma 4/3, Pa 3/2, Dha 5/3, Ni 15/8) are therefore best read as a convenient ideal for a Hindustani "natural" scale, not as the exact pitches any given performer uses [@src.jairazbhoy-1963-intonation-north-indian].

## Where this leaves us

- **Established:** a seven-note system with named notes, two fixed (Sa, Pa) and the rest movable, and a count of twenty-two shrutis grouped 4-3-2-4-4-3-2, all described in early treatises (grade B for the existence of the scheme, grade C for its date).
- **Reconstruction:** the sizes of individual shrutis, the identification of the shruti with a comma, and the tables of named shruti ratios.
- **Tradition:** the Gandhara-grama in heaven, and the animal associations.
- **Observation:** performers adjust pitches by raga and context.

The shruti is best understood as the early theorists' way of saying that the gaps between notes are small, musically meaningful and movable.
