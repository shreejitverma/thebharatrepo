The English word *mother* and Sanskrit *mātṛ* resemble one another for a historical reason.
So do English *new* and Sanskrit *náva*, and English *three* and Sanskrit *tráyas*.
These are not isolated resemblances: regular sound correspondences across many languages support descent from a shared, unattested ancestor called Proto-Indo-European (PIE) [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture; @src.mallory-adams-2006-oxford-introduction-pie].

## The discovery

In 1786 the British judge and scholar Sir William Jones told the Asiatic Society in Calcutta that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin were bound by affinities too strong to be accidental, and that they must have sprung from a common source that might no longer exist.
His remark is often treated as the birth of Indo-European studies, although others had noticed similar resemblances before him [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture; @src.mallory-adams-2006-oxford-introduction-pie].
Since then, linguists have built a method for comparing related languages, reconstructing their lost ancestor, and testing the results.
In that work Sanskrit has played an outsized role, for reasons discussed below.

## How the sounds line up

The correspondences are regular, which is what separates real relatedness from coincidence.

- **English consonants shifted.** Germanic languages changed *p*, *t*, *k* into *f*, *th*, *h*, so Sanskrit and Latin *p* matches English *f* in *pitṛ́* and *father*, and Latin *ped-* in *pēs* matches English *foot* [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].
- **Sanskrit merged vowel contrasts.** PIE *e* and *o* generally appear as *a* in Sanskrit, while Greek preserves a clearer distinction in many forms.
  Sanskrit *bhárati* (“carries”) and Greek *phérei* illustrate a related verb with different branch-specific sound changes [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].
- **Hundred is *satem* in Sanskrit.** The PIE sound *\*ḱ* became a sibilant, *ś*, in Sanskrit and *s* in Avestan, but a hard *k* in Latin (*centum*).
  This split gives the "satem" and "centum" groups their names, and Sanskrit and Avestan are both satem [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].
- **Several consonant contrasts merged.** Sanskrit does not preserve the full set of reconstructed PIE labiovelars as a separate series, one reason it is informative but not a direct copy of the parent language [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].

## Sound laws seen in Sanskrit

Four changes are particularly well illustrated in Sanskrit.

**Grassmann's Law.** In Sanskrit, when a word contains two aspirated stops in successive syllables, the first loses aspiration in particular morphological environments.
The reduplicated verb *dádhāti* (“puts”) illustrates the pattern; Greek shows a parallel change, generally analyzed as an independent development [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].

**Bartholomae's Law.** Indo-Iranian shows a sound change in certain clusters involving a voiced aspirate and a following voiceless consonant.
Forms such as Sanskrit *buddha* (“awakened”) and *lubdha* (“greedy”) are commonly discussed in connection with this change, whose precise historical environment is more technical than a simple letter-substitution rule [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].

**The RUKI rule.** In Indo-Iranian, *s* commonly became retroflex *ṣ* after *r*, *u*, *k* or *i*, subject to phonological conditions.
The name RUKI is a mnemonic for those environments; related changes in other language branches require their own historical analysis [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].

**Brugmann's Law.** Indo-Iranian *ā* in certain open syllables is traditionally explained as the outcome of PIE *o* under specified conditions.
The exact formulation and application of the law are debated, so examples should be read within the full comparative reconstruction rather than as a one-step equation [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].

## Is Sanskrit the closest to PIE?

A popular claim holds that Sanskrit is the most faithful survivor of the parent language.
Comparative linguistics instead shows that every branch preserves some features and changes others [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].

Sanskrit preserves a great deal.
Its three numbers (singular, dual, plural), eight cases and elaborate verb system are close to what PIE is reconstructed to have had, and the Vedic pitch accent matches what Greek and other languages suggest [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].
But Sanskrit also innovated heavily.
It merged the vowels *e*, *o* and *a* into one, it merged the labiovelars, and it reshaped several consonants.
Other branches preserve features that Sanskrit lost: Greek keeps the PIE vowel contrast, and Hittite, among the earliest attested Indo-European languages, preserved the laryngeal consonants that left only traces elsewhere [@src.mallory-adams-2006-oxford-introduction-pie].

One of those traces is visible in Sanskrit itself.
Sanskrit *pitṛ́* has an *i* where Greek *patḗr* and Latin *pater* have *a*, and the standard explanation is that the *i* is the descendant of a laryngeal, *\*h₂*, in *\*ph₂tḗr* [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].

The accurate summary is that Sanskrit is one of the most informative members of the family, not that it is the oldest or the purest [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture; @src.mallory-adams-2006-oxford-introduction-pie].

## Sanskrit's nearest relative

Sanskrit's closest major relatives are the Iranian languages, including Avestan, an Old Iranian language preserved in Zoroastrian texts.
The regular sound correspondences and shared vocabulary place Indo-Aryan and Iranian within the Indo-Iranian branch [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture; @src.kuzmina-2007-origin-indo-iranians].

The most visible correspondence is Sanskrit *s* against Avestan *h*:

| Sanskrit | Avestan | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *soma* | *haoma* | a sacred plant and its drink |
| *saptá* | *hapta* | seven |
| *ásura* | *ahura* | lord (in the Avestan sense) |
| *yajñá* | *yasna* | worship, sacrifice |

The word for "god" shows a famous difference of meaning.
Sanskrit *devá* means "god", while Avestan *daēva* means a demon; and Sanskrit *ásura*, used of gods in the Rigveda and later of their rivals, matches Avestan *ahura*, the title of the highest deity [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].
Scholars have long discussed how this reversal came about, and no single explanation has won general agreement.

Indo-Aryan words also turn up outside India.
In a treaty of about 1380 BCE between a Hittite king and a king of Mitanni, a kingdom in northern Mesopotamia and Syria, the gods Mitra, Varuna, Indra and the Nasatyas are invoked under names that look Indo-Aryan [@src.thieme-1960-aryan-gods-mitanni-treaties].

## Where did the family begin?

The location and timing of the PIE homeland remain debated, although the steppe model is the dominant account in current scholarship.

**The steppe hypothesis.** The dominant model places the PIE homeland on the Pontic-Caspian steppe, north of the Black and Caspian Seas, and dates its dispersal broadly to the 5th to 3rd millennia BCE.
Its case draws on reconstructed vocabulary for wheeled vehicles and pastoral life alongside archaeological evidence; David Anthony and J. P. Mallory offer influential syntheses [@src.anthony-2007-horse-wheel-language; @src.mallory-adams-2006-oxford-introduction-pie].
Ancient DNA studies add that steppe-related ancestry appears in South Asia in the second millennium BCE [@src.narasimhan-2019-formation-human-populations].

**The Anatolian hypothesis.** Colin Renfrew proposed that Indo-European spread with farming from Anatolia, thousands of years earlier than the steppe account.
A 2012 Bayesian analysis by Remco Bouckaert and colleagues supported an Anatolian origin, while later genetic and archaeological work has strengthened steppe-based explanations for several dispersals; the inference from language-tree models remains contested [@src.bouckaert-2012-origins-indo-european; @src.narasimhan-2019-formation-human-populations].

**Out-of-India arguments.** Writers including Shrikant Talageri have argued that Indo-European languages spread outward from South Asia and have proposed an earlier date for the Rigveda [@src.talageri-2000-rigveda-historical-analysis].
This is a minority position and is not accepted by most specialists in historical linguistics; it faces difficulties accounting for the comparative linguistic and archaeological evidence used in steppe models [@src.bryant-2001-quest-origins-vedic-culture; @src.anthony-2007-horse-wheel-language].

The homeland debate does not undo the comparative evidence: Sanskrit, Avestan, Greek, Latin and English display regular correspondences that historical linguists explain through shared ancestry.
What remains disputed is how that ancestor spread and where its earliest speakers lived [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture; @src.bouckaert-2012-origins-indo-european].
