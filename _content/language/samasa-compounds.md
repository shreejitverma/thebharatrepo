English has compounds too: "bookshop", "sunrise", "blackboard".
Sanskrit takes the idea much further.
A *samāsa* (समास), literally "putting together" or "throwing together", fuses two or more nouns into a single word, with all the case endings except the last removed. [@src.whitney-1896-sanskrit-grammar; @src.macdonell-1927-sanskrit-grammar-for-students]
Panini states that the case endings of the members are deleted in a compound (A 2.4.71). [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini]

The result can be compact and ambiguous.
*Rājapuruṣaḥ* might be "the king's man" or something looser, and only context decides.
Grammarians therefore teach the *vigraha* (विग्रह), an explicit paraphrase that "pulls apart" the compound into a clause, as the key to reading it.

## Four types, and two subtypes

The traditional grouping has four main types, all named in the Ashtadhyayi: *avyayībhāva*, *tatpuruṣa*, *bahuvrīhi* and *dvandva*.
Two more names, *karmadhāraya* and *dvigu*, are subtypes of *tatpuruṣa*, which is why some textbooks count four and some six. [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini; @src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions]

| Type | What dominates | Example | Vigraha |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tatpuruṣa | the last member | *rājapuruṣaḥ* | *rājñaḥ puruṣaḥ*, "the king's man" |
| Karmadhāraya | the last member, with a descriptive first | *nīlotpalam* | *nīlam ca tad utpalam ca*, "a blue lotus" |
| Dvigu | the last member, with a number first | *trilokam* | *trayāṇāṃ lokānāṃ samāhāraḥ*, "the three worlds" |
| Bahuvrīhi | something outside the compound | *pītāmbaraḥ* | *pītam ambaraṃ yasya saḥ*, "he whose garment is yellow" |
| Dvandva | all the members equally | *rāmalakṣmaṇau* | *rāmaś ca lakṣmaṇaś ca*, "Rama and Lakshmana" |
| Avyayībhāva | the first member, an indeclinable | *pratidinam* | "day by day" |

## Tatpuruṣa: "his man"

The name itself is a compound of this type: *tasya puruṣaḥ*, "his man".
In a tatpuruṣa the first member relates to the second as one of the case relations: of, by, from, in.
The relation can be any of the oblique cases, so tatpuruṣas are sorted by it. [@src.whitney-1896-sanskrit-grammar]

- Genitive: *rājapuruṣaḥ*, "king's man".
- Accusative: *grāmagataḥ*, "gone to the village".
- Instrumental: *hastalikhitam*, "written by hand".
- Ablative: *caurabhayam*, "fear from thieves".
- Locative: *śāstranipuṇaḥ*, "skilled in the treatises".
- Negative (*nañ*): *adharmaḥ*, "non-dharma", with *a-* before consonants.

The head noun is the last, so the compound takes its gender and number: *paravalliṅgaṃ dvandvatatpuruṣayoḥ* (A 2.4.26). [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini]

### Karmadhāraya and dvigu

The karmadhāraya is a tatpuruṣa whose members refer to the same thing, one describing the other (A 1.2.42).
So *nīlotpalam* is a lotus that is blue; *mahārājaḥ* is a king who is great.
Panini's rule for joining a noun with its qualifier is A 2.1.57. [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini]

The dvigu has a numeral as its first member (A 2.1.52).
It often names a collective, such as *trilokam*, "the three worlds", or *pañcavaṭī*, a grove of five banyan trees. [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini]
Because the dvigu belongs to the large domain that Panini's rule on the tatpuruṣa opens, it counts as a subtype. [@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions]

## Bahuvrīhi: the compound that points elsewhere

A bahuvrīhi is a descriptive compound that describes something outside itself, usually a person.
The name is the model: *bahuvrīhi* means "having much rice", a rich man (A 2.2.23: *śeṣo bahuvrīhiḥ*, "the remainder is bahuvrīhi"). [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini]

Compare these two words, which differ only in their endings and in which analysis applies:

| Word | Analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *pītāmbaram* | *pītaṃ ca tad ambaram*; karmadhāraya | "a yellow garment" |
| *pītāmbaraḥ* | *pītam ambaraṃ yasya saḥ*; bahuvrīhi | "one whose garment is yellow", a common epithet of Vishnu and Krishna |

*Daśānanaḥ*, "the ten-faced one", is likewise a bahuvrīhi, and by tradition names Ravana. [@src.whitney-1896-sanskrit-grammar]
The bahuvrīhi is where a beginner most often goes wrong, because the compound's apparent head, *ambaram*, is not what it refers to.

## Dvandva: "and" in one word

A dvandva joins members with "and", and each keeps equal weight.
Panini's rule is two words: *cārthe dvandvaḥ*, "dvandva, when the sense is 'and'" (A 2.2.29). [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini]
Grammarians distinguish two chief kinds. [@src.whitney-1896-sanskrit-grammar]

- Enumerative (*itaretara*), for separate individuals: *rāmalakṣmaṇau*, "Rama and Lakshmana"; *mātāpitarau*, "mother and father". The compound is dual or plural, according to the number of members.
- Collective (*samāhāra*), for a set treated as a whole: *pāṇipādam*, "hands and feet" as one unit, neuter singular.

*Sukhaduḥkhe*, "pleasure and pain", is the first kind, in the dual.

## Avyayībhāva: adverbs made of nouns

An avyayībhāva has an indeclinable first member, and the whole compound behaves as an indeclinable adverb. [@src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini, A 2.1.6]
Examples: *pratidinam*, "every day"; *yathāśakti*, "according to one's ability". [@src.whitney-1896-sanskrit-grammar]

## How to unpack one

A short method that works for most compounds:

1. Mark the last member and its ending; that carries gender, number and case.
2. Ask whether the compound names the last member itself (tatpuruṣa or one of its subtypes), the members together (dvandva), or something outside (bahuvrīhi).
3. Write the vigraha, supplying the missing case endings.
4. Test the meaning against the context.

When several readings remain possible, that is not a failure of the method: Sanskrit authors sometimes exploit compound ambiguity deliberately. [@src.whitney-1896-sanskrit-grammar]

## What is debated

The four-type scheme is old, and the sources are not all in agreement about where the lines fall.
The status of the dvandva and the bahuvrīhi as true compounds differing in kind from tatpuruṣa, and the proper analysis of compounds that look like phrases, are live issues in modern linguistics.
Work such as John Lowe's study of Sanskrit compound syntax examines how compounds in the earliest texts relate to ordinary syntax, and shows that what is a tidy category in the textbook is more varied in the texts. [@src.lowe-2015-syntax-sanskrit-compounds]

## A compound in Bhagavad Gita 2.47

The Gita's *karmaphalahetuḥ* is a compound of several members: *karma* ("action"), *phala* ("fruit") and *hetu* ("cause", "motive"). [@src.vanbuitenen-1981-bhagavadgita-mahabharata, BG 2.47]
It is commonly read as a bahuvrīhi, "one whose motive is the fruit of action", so the instruction translates as "do not be someone who acts for the fruit".
The compound itself is a chain: "the fruit of action" is a genitive tatpuruṣa, and the chain as a whole is used as an adjective on the understood "you".
This reading is the usual one among translators, though renderings differ in emphasis.
