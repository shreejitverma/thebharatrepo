Open the Rigveda and then a poem by Kalidasa and you will notice that both are called Sanskrit, yet they do not read alike.
The Rigveda is full of unfamiliar verb forms, extra word endings and a musical accent that later Sanskrit lost.
Kalidasa's language is smoother, more regular and more uniform.
The comparison is only an analogy: Vedic and Classical Sanskrit are historical stages with a much closer grammatical relationship than English at those two points.

## Layers of Sanskrit

Scholars do not treat "Vedic" as one block.
Michael Witzel distinguished five layers within it, moving from the oldest to the youngest: the language of the Rigveda; the mantra language of the other Samhitas; the prose of the Samhitas; the prose of the Brahmanas; and the language of the Sutras [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru].
Alongside the change in language goes a shift in geography, from the Punjab in the Rigveda toward the Kuru and Panchala region of the upper Ganga basin in later texts [@src.witzel-1997-early-sanskritization-kuru; @src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].

After this comes the language that Panini describes in the *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, usually dated to the 5th or 4th century BCE [@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions; @src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini].
Epic Sanskrit, the language of the Mahabharata and Ramayana, follows, with some forms that Panini's rules do not allow; later grammarians called such forms *ārṣa*, "of the seers" [@src.cardona-jain-2003-indo-aryan-languages].
The ornate court poetry and technical prose of the first millennium CE is the last stage.
A well-known early example is the inscription of Rudradaman at Junagadh, about 150 CE, which is already in polished Sanskrit [@src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods].

The dates of the earliest layer are inferred from language, ritual references and comparison with related languages, not from manuscripts made at the time.
Vedic recitation traditions transmitted the texts orally, and the surviving manuscripts are much later than the compositions they preserve.
Some Hindu traditional chronologies associate the Vedic collection with the beginning of the present Kali age; this is a traditional reckoning, not a date established by historical linguistics [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism; @src.unesco-2003-tradition-vedic-chanting].

## Seven differences you can see

The table below sets out the best-documented contrasts.
The Vedic forms are from the Rigveda or its neighbours, and the classical forms are those taught by Panini and used in later literature [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda; @src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions].

| Feature | Vedic | Classical |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Accent | A pitch accent is recorded in Vedic recitation, with udātta, anudātta and svarita distinctions | The Vedic accent is not normally a feature of Classical Sanskrit literary pronunciation, though grammarians preserved rules about it |
| Subjunctive (*leṭ*) | A living mood for intention, expectation and prayer | Gone; Panini treats it as a feature of the Veda |
| Infinitives | Many formations, such as *-tave*, *-dhyai*, *-se* | One main formation, *-tum* |
| Nominative plural of *deva* | *devā́saḥ* beside *devā́ḥ* | *devāḥ* only |
| Instrumental plural of *deva* | *devébhiḥ* beside *devaíḥ* | *devaiḥ* only |
| Locative of *vyoman* | *vyòman* (bare stem) beside *vyomani* | *vyomani* only |
| Prefixes | Can stand apart from their verb | Fixed to the verb |

A few of these deserve a closer look.

**Infinitives.** The Rigveda has well over a dozen different ways to form an infinitive, each with its own noun case behind it.
Vedic *gántave* and *gamádhyai* ("to go") coexist, whereas in the classical language the single form *gántum* has taken over [@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions].

**Prefixes.** In the Rigveda a preverb such as *ā* ("towards") can be separated from its verb by other words, a feature called tmesis, whereas classical *āgacchati* is always one word [@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions].

**Verb stems.** Vedic often has two stems where the classical language kept one.
The Vedic poets say *kṛṇóti* as well as *karóti* for "he makes" [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].

## The Vedic accent

Vedic had a pitch accent, not a stress accent.
Each word has one syllable that is high (*udātta*), and the syllables around it are low (*anudātta*) or falling (*svarita*).
Because the accent sits on different syllables in different words, it could distinguish meanings, as in the pair *bráhman* ("sacred formulation") and *brahmán* ("priest") [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].

Accent marks in printed Rigveda editions represent a recitational and grammatical tradition.
Here is the first line of the hymn in one common notation:

> अ॒ग्निमी॑ळे पु॒रोहि॑तं य॒ज्ञस्य॑ दे॒वमृ॒त्विज॑म् ।
> होता॑रं रत्न॒धात॑मम् ॥

In this notation, anudātta is marked below and svarita above; udātta is generally unmarked.
A modern transliteration is *agním īḷe puróhitaṃ yajñásya devám ṛtvíjam | hótāraṃ ratnadhā́tamam* (RV 1.1.1) [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].
It opens the hymn by praising Agni, the sacrificial fire, as the household priest and the god who conducts the offering [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].

The same line contains another feature that disappeared later.
The letter ळ (*ḷ*) is the Rigveda's pronunciation of an intervocalic *ḍ*, so *īḷe* corresponds to what is *īḍe* in the later language [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda].

The accent has not been lost to the living tradition.
Reciters in the Vedic schools still chant with the traditional pitch, and UNESCO recognised Vedic chanting as a masterpiece of intangible heritage in 2003 [@src.unesco-2003-tradition-vedic-chanting].
Panini's grammar still includes rules for accent, although it marks many of them as applying to the Veda [@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions].

## The subjunctive that disappeared

The Vedic subjunctive, called *leṭ*, formed verbs that express what someone intends, expects or hopes will happen, such as the poet's prayer.
Classical Sanskrit has no such mood, and its work is done by other moods, the optative (*liṅ*) and the imperative (*loṭ*) [@src.jamison-brereton-2014-rigveda; @src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions].
The Greek of Homer has a very similar subjunctive, which is one of the reasons scholars think this mood goes back to the common ancestor of the two languages [@src.fortson-2010-indo-european-language-culture].

## How are the stages related?

There are several serious answers.

**Panini described and codified a language norm.** Cardona's study reads the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* as a sophisticated account of usage associated with Panini's northwest Indian setting, alongside rules explicitly marked for Vedic (*chandas*) [@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions; @src.katre-1987-astadhyayi-panini].
Scholars differ on how directly this norm represents ordinary speech, as opposed to educated or learned usage.

**Sanskrit as a cosmopolitan literary language.** Sheldon Pollock emphasizes the role of Classical Sanskrit in courtly literature, political expression and transregional culture, especially from the early centuries CE [@src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods].
This account shifts attention from an either-or question about speech to the social settings in which Sanskrit was cultivated and used.

**The traditional view.** In the tradition, Vedic speech is eternal and not composed by human authors, and Panini's grammar describes a language that already existed rather than creating it [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].
The tradition also credits careful memorisation for preserving the Veda, and the survival of the accent in recitation is consistent with that claim [@src.unesco-2003-tradition-vedic-chanting].

These accounts address different questions: Panini's grammatical description, the relationship of learned Sanskrit to spoken varieties, and the later social uses of Sanskrit.
The traditional account of an eternal Veda is a theological understanding of the text's authority; it does not function as a historical linguistic date [@src.flood-2003-blackwell-companion-hinduism].

## What the grammarians knew

Indian scholars were aware of these differences from an early date.
Yaska's *Nirukta* explains difficult Vedic words, and Panini's grammar treats the Veda (*chandas*) and the standard language (*bhāṣā*) in different ways [@src.cardona-1988-panini-work-traditions].
The word *saṃskṛta*, "perfected, refined", was applied to the standard language to set it apart from everyday speech [@src.pollock-2006-language-of-the-gods].
Awareness of the two stages is therefore built into the Indian grammatical tradition itself.
